    Introduction

    VibeRounds is a fast, exploratory tool for clinical cohort data. It ships with presets for the most-used analyses, so an analyst can go from a question to a first look quickly and safely, with an optional LLM to help interpret and propose next steps. Its purpose is to surface insights worth pursuing, so that proper research can follow.

    Discovery and replication: how the pieces fit

    Four analytics work together to find a pattern and check whether it holds up.

    1. DiscoverBackward lift and Backward factors start from an outcome, such as escalation, and look back for factors more common in those patients. Lift is the rate with the factor divided by the rate without it. 1 means no association.
    2. Interpret (optional)The LLM can explain a result in plain language or draft a hypothesis with a test query. It never produces the numbers.
    3. TestForward hypothesis is a manual test: you pick one idea and see which outcomes follow. Closed loop is automatic: it re-tests the strongest backward findings on a fresh cohort and marks each as replicated or not.
    4. DecideEvery number comes from deterministic code. A person reviews the result and approves or rejects it before it counts as a finding candidate.
    A replication is only as independent as the fresh cohort. In the demo it is regenerated synthetic data, so it shows the workflow, not clinical truth. Lifts are often small, comparisons are unadjusted, and scanning many factors produces some chance findings. Confirm anything promising in independent real data.

    Where it fits

    VibeRounds is built for speed to a first insight, not for scale or confirmation. It reads FHIR data directly, including uncoded text, and runs its preset analytics automatically, so there is no OMOP conversion or network access to arrange first. Established tools such as OHDSI ATLAS (OMOP data) and network platforms such as TriNetX are stronger on large real-world datasets, multi-site federation, adjusted effect estimation and validation. Use VibeRounds for clinical research prototyping and AI-assisted acceleration: try ideas quickly, check data quality, find patterns worth pursuing and draft testable queries. Then take the promising ones to a proper platform and a properly designed study. It is not clinical decision support.

    What VibeRounds is for

    VibeRounds helps a clinical analyst turn a clinical question into a transparent, repeatable analysis of cohort data. It supports exploring data and generating hypotheses. It does not replace study design, ethics and governance review, or confirmatory analysis, and it is not clinical decision support.

    Ask and define

    Frame the question as PICO or PECO, or type a plain-language phrase. Build the cohort with include and exclude criteria and save it as a named definition.

    Explore and test

    Run more than 100 analytics: description, associations, comparisons, time to event, safety signals, care quality and equity.

    See the person, not only the average

    Move from cohort-level results to one patient's record, trajectory and position against the cohort.

    Keep the reasoning auditable

    Every calculation is deterministic and repeatable. The AI only drafts text, you decide, and every run is logged.

    What the three data depths mean for your analysis

    V1 · what is written

    Matches the words in the record. "Fever" and "pyrexia" count as different things. Works on any data.

    V2 · what is coded

    Understands SNOMED CT, RxNorm and LOINC. Synonyms match, and "all cardiovascular conditions" can be asked for. Use it for stable phenotype definitions across sources.

    V3 · when it happened

    Understands order and timing: what came before what, time lags, treatment exposure windows. Use it when sequence matters.

    From clinical question to evidence

    The app follows the same steps as an evidence-based analysis.

    1. AskFrame the question with PICO/PECO Study, or describe it in a phrase that the keyword parser turns into filters.
    2. Define the cohortCombine criteria such as sex, age, category and comorbidity with AND, OR and NOT. The app shows how many patients match.
    3. Appraise the dataRun the data quality assessment. It grades each field family as good, limited or weak, and reports terminology coverage.
    4. ExploreDescribe the cohort, then look for associations and patterns.
    5. TestCompare matched patients with the rest, follow time to event, or check the effect of a medication after a delay.
    6. ReviewThe AI drafts a hypothesis. You read the numbers and approve or reject. Only approved drafts count as finding candidates.
    7. RecordSave the whole suite run, including outputs, and rely on the audit log of runs and decisions.

    Appraising results: good practice

    • Treat findings as hypotheses. Read the numbers before approving. Only approved drafts become finding candidates.
    • Check data quality first. A weak grade means the result should not be trusted.
    • Look at group size. Small groups give unstable rates.
    • Expect chance findings. Many analytics run at once, so some patterns will arise by chance. Use replication, as Closed loop does, and confirm in independent data.
    • Beware confounding. Matched-versus-rest comparisons are unadjusted. Use the multivariate options where adjustment matters.
    • Define phenotypes carefully. Text matching and coded matching can select different patients.
    • Order is not cause. Time-based analytics show what came first, not why. In the demo data, outcomes such as escalation and readmission are modeled at import, so stratifying them is circular.
    • Know your data. The default cohort is synthetic and illustrates the workflow, not clinical truth.
    • Keep governance in view. Record the run, follow your ethics and data-governance rules, and remember this is not clinical decision support.

    Community analytics: an open suite

    Direction, not a shipped feature. Each analytic is already a self-contained module that declares the patient fields it needs. That makes it possible for a community to add new buttons as preset analytics, while the base stays open standards: FHIR for exchange and SNOMED CT, RxNorm and LOINC for meaning. A site connects and maps its data once, and every preset that its data can support then runs.

    1. ConnectPoint at a FHIR server or import a CSV or NDJSON export.
    2. MapMap the data once to the standard model and terminologies. The engine version (V1, V2, V3) shows what the data can support.
    3. Run presetsEvery analytic whose required fields and engine depth are met is available as a button.
    4. ContributeAnyone can propose a new module, which passes review before it joins the suite.

    One module contract

    An id, the required fields, a run step that turns cases into a result, a data-quality check, a plain-language how-to and an audit note. This is the shape the existing analytics already follow.

    Standards first

    FHIR is the base today, with SNOMED CT, RxNorm and LOINC in V2 and V3. Converters from OMOP or openEHR would be new work, and a shared logic standard such as CQL could let rules move between systems.

    A review gate

    Code review, sandboxed execution with no network access, version numbers, a synthetic test case and clear authorship. The existing audit verdicts (ok, caution, serious) could become the submission checklist.

    Maturity levels

    More buttons can look like more evidence, so each module should say how far it has been checked.

    LevelWhat it means
    ExperimentalRuns and passes its synthetic test case. Not checked on real data.
    TestedCode reviewed and documented, and run on named real datasets with results recorded.
    ValidatedIndependently validated, with the evidence published.
    Risks to manage: unreviewed analytics creating false confidence, privacy and security because the code touches clinical data, and fragmentation if contributors define fields their own way. A strict shared data model and the review gate address these.

    Feature deep dives

    The four larger features in detail.

    Patient journey map (7)

    Every patient's trajectory on one swimlane canvas — compare, align, zoom.

    Swimlane trajectories
    One lane per data category (comorbidities, diagnoses, symptoms, medications, labs, procedures, outcomes); each patient is a chronological traced line with a dot per event. Dots grow with repeat frequency (×count on hover), and hover shows patient ID, term [code], date and repeats. Click a dot to zoom the axis one level, centred on it.
    Multi-patient comparison
    Pick a patient from the dropdown and “＋ Add to map” to stack several patients — each gets its own color and traced line; color chips list who is plotted and remove with one click. Ideal for comparing diagnostic odysseys side by side.
    Plot full cohort
    One button overlays every patient's trajectory at once (500+ lines tested); lines auto-fade as count grows so the cohort's shape stays readable on top of individual colors.
    Switchable time axis
    “📅 Calendar” plots absolute dates; “⏱ Since day 0” anchors every patient at their own first event and reads minute 1, 2, 3… hour… day… week… month… year… decade — so patients admitted years apart align at day 0. Hover keeps the real date alongside the relative position.
    Time-zoom ladder
    Zoom in/out steps the axis through decade → year → month → week → day → hour → minute (12 buckets per window), with drag-to-pan and a scroll slider; works in both axis modes.
    Lane visibility
    Checkbox per lane; Diagnoses, Medications and Outcomes are on by default so the chart starts uncluttered. Hiding a lane removes its band and its data everywhere.
    Works on the Suite too
    Open the journey map as a Suite output and all controls work in place — buttons re-run the analytic and update that block's result without navigating or duplicating.
    Process mining (V3) (5)

    Pathways, timing, clusters, association rules, outliers — v3 event-stream analytics.

    Pathway discovery
    Each patient's events become a signalline (ordered, repeats collapsed); frequent contiguous sequences are mined anywhere in the line with distinct-patient support, plus the directly-follows graph with median step gaps — Disco/ProM-style pathway discovery over the cohort's event log.
    Timing intervals
    First-event-to-first-event gaps for ordered pairs (symptom → diagnosis, lab → medication, …): median/IQR/p90 day gaps, cohort coverage, fast-tracked (≤7 d) vs delayed (>90 d) shares.
    Trajectory clusters
    Standardized per-patient feature vectors (event counts per category, pathway length, time-to-diagnosis/medication, span) grouped by deterministic seeded k-means; clusters are labeled by their most distinguishing features with exemplar patient IDs.
    Pre-diagnosis association rules
    Apriori baskets from the 90-day window before the anchor diagnosis; antecedent → consequent rules ranked by lift with support and confidence.
    Trajectory outliers
    Patients flagged on interpretable deviations — rare pathway (support ≤2%), atypical time-to-diagnosis (robust median/MAD z-score), missing steps ≥50% of the cohort takes — ranked by combined score with plain-language reasons.
    Cohort analysis (8)

    Aggregated frequency/timing view of every plotted concept across the whole cohort.

    Frequency-ordered layout (default)
    Within each lane, nodes are sorted by INCREASING patient count — left = rarest, right = most common. Node size = distinct patients sharing the concept; the most common is drawn largest so the dominant patterns jump out immediately.
    By timing toggle
    Switch to position nodes by typical day offset since day 0, with a quartile bar showing the IQR — useful for reading when a concept typically appears.
    Zoom in / out
    ＋ Zoom in halves the visible window, － Zoom out doubles it, both centred on the current view; the mouse wheel zooms toward the cursor. Use it to focus on a narrow band of rare concepts or to pull back and see the whole distribution.
    Reset
    One button restores the default view that shows every node.
    Drag-to-pan
    Click and drag inside the SVG to slide the view horizontally.
    Native SVG tooltips on hover
    Hover a node to see its label, patient count and share, occurrence count, average repeats per patient, typical day with quartiles, and a rare-outlier flag when support is ≤2%.
    Empty lists hidden
    “Most repeated”, “Rare / possible outliers” and “Widest timing spread” only render when they have qualifying concepts — a cohort with no repeats shows only “Most common (by patients)”.
    Works on the Suite too
    Open cohort analysis as a Suite output and all controls work in place — buttons re-run the analytic and update that block's result without navigating or duplicating.
    Patient data explorer (5)

    Browse, search and visualize the cohort itself.

    Paginated case table
    Every patient in the loaded cohort as a sortable, searchable table (ID, category, age, sex, LOS, 90-day encounters, disposition, admit month, comorbidities). Click a row to expand its full record. Runs in both modes: SQL paging against PostgreSQL in database mode (so it scales to million-patient cohorts), in-memory filter/sort/slice otherwise — the server picks based on what's loaded.
    Search & filter subset awareness
    Searching narrows the table to the matching subset ("12,400 of 1,000,000 cases") with debounced keystrokes; page size is selectable, and the view resets cleanly when the cohort is switched or re-imported.
    One-click deep-dive analyses
    Five explorations render below the table on demand: cohort overview (age, sex, LOS, readmission + category breakdown), top conditions & medications, distributions & outliers (age/LOS histograms with outlier counts), group comparison (readmission, escalation and LOS across category, sex, age band, disposition, insurance or region), and a data-quality assessment (field completeness, terminology coverage, internal consistency, per-analytic readiness). Panels stack, so several can be compared side by side.
    Multi-patient timeline drawer (v2)
    Select patients — individually, per page, or "select entire cohort" (paged walk, capped at 5,000) — and open a bottom-anchored drawer that fetches each patient's coded events in parallel and plots them on a shared longitudinal timeline.
    Knowledge graph view (v2)
    The same selection rendered as a patient knowledge graph — coded SNOMED/RxNorm events as interconnected nodes instead of a time axis. Both views are v2-mode features since they need terminology-coded data; the drawer says so honestly when coded data is missing. In the Suite both render as inline outputs — the Knowledge graph tile is gated to v2/v3 and disabled with a version hint on v1.

    LLM-assisted features

    The LLM is optional and runs server-side. It reads a computed summary of the cohort and drafts text and proposals. It never produces the numbers: every statistic comes from deterministic code, and a person approves before anything counts.

    AI interpretation

    Each analytic output gets a plain-language interpretation. For a cohort overview, it summarizes the age profile, sex split, largest diagnostic groups, escalation rate, length of stay and readmission rate, then suggests what a reviewer should investigate next. A Re-interpret button regenerates it.

    Live hypothesis: generate and test

    Beyond interpreting results, the LLM proposes a testable hypothesis and the app tests it.

    1. HypothesisThe LLM states a hypothesis from the summary, such as older Neuro / MSK patients driving long stays and high escalation.
    2. Proposed test queryIt proposes filters, for example category eq Neuro / MSK and age gte 65. These run as deterministic code.
    3. Deterministic resultThe app compares the matched group with the rest on escalation rate, 90-day readmission and mean length of stay, and reports the lift.
    4. Human decisionThe result is marked pending until a reviewer clicks Approve or Reject. Only an approved draft counts as a finding candidate.
    In the example run, 65 matched patients had a 92.3% escalation rate against 86.7% for the other 435, a lift of 1.07×. The app prompts the reviewer to read the numbers against the hypothesis before deciding.

    What the LLM does and does not do

    LLM (server-side, optional)

    • Writes the interpretation of each output
    • Drafts a hypothesis
    • Proposes the filters to test it
    • Sees only the computed summary

    Deterministic code and people

    • Runs the proposed query on the cohort
    • Computes every statistic and the lift
    • Reviewer approves or rejects
    • Never exposes case-level data to the model

    In the Build-your-own query engine, describe what you are looking for in plain language and the LLM drafts a query. The draft is validated against the field catalog and is ready to run on the cohort as deterministic code.

    Working with a browser AI assistant

    VibeRounds is an ordinary web page, so you can also use it side by side with an AI assistant in your browser, such as Gemini. Share the VibeRounds tab and the assistant can read what is on screen and talk you through it. This is separate from the app's own LLM: the assistant works from what you share, not through the app's settings.

    Find a research question

    Ask it to spot something worth exploring in the cohort. It proposes a focused question, for example how polypharmacy and clustered comorbidities relate to escalation and 90-day readmission in older adults.

    Plan the study as buttons

    Ask it to lay out the whole study as a sequence of buttons to click next. It returns steps such as create the cohort, apply the age and category filters, then run the matching analytics in order.

    Summarize an output

    Ask it to summarize a result on screen. For the co-occurrence output it lists the most common individual comorbidities with their case counts, then the most frequent pairs.

    A browser assistant sees what is on screen, including cohort values, so it sits outside the app's own guardrails. With real patient data, check that your organization allows sharing the tab before using one.

    Natural language to cohort filter

    The cohort builder lets you type a phrase such as "female patients having chronic kidney diseases" and parse it into query conditions. This step is not LLM-driven: it uses a deterministic keyword parser, so no patient data leaves the browser. Recognized terms become validated conditions, here Sex is F and Comorbidity includes Chronic kidney disease, matching 60 of 500 patients. Unrecognized phrases are rejected and listed rather than guessed, so you can add those filters by hand.

    From there you can combine include and exclude conditions with AND/OR and NOT, match a hypothesis, apply the result as the cohort filter for all analytics, reset to the full cohort, or save it as a named definition.

    Architecture: code goes to the data

    The intended design keeps patient data where it is stored and moves only the question and the answer. The app builds a query from your cohort and analytic choice and sends it to a database connection. The analytics run next to the database, and only the output returns to the browser. The app never fetches patient records. It builds on database mode, where analytics already run as SQL. This lets several centers be analyzed together without the analyst or the LLM ever seeing patient-level data.

    How a request flows

    1. Build the queryYour cohort filters become a structured query in the app. No data is fetched.
    2. Send to each siteThe app sends the query, the analytic name and its parameters to each connected database.
    3. Run at the siteThe site's reviewed analytics library runs on its own data, either the patient database or a de-identified replica.
    4. Check the outputSmall counts are suppressed and the request is logged before anything leaves the site.
    5. Return resultsOnly aggregate results reach the browser.
    6. InterpretThe LLM or assistant reads only those summaries.

    Why this design is good

    • Patient-level data stays inside each center
    • The analyst and the LLM see only summaries
    • It matches the rule that the model sees only computed summaries
    • Every site runs the same analytics locally, so it scales to many centers

    Where it can still leak

    • Row-level views expose individuals
    • Small groups can identify a person
    • Overlapping queries can be subtracted to isolate someone
    • Free-form code sent to a database is a major risk
    • LLM text can repeat a small count or unusual detail
    • De-identified data can still re-identify, through dates, rare diseases or free text

    Safeguards that make it safe

    Fixed analytics, not free code

    The site runs only a reviewed library of analytics. The app sends parameters such as filters and the analytic name, and the site validates them.

    Small-count suppression

    Every output hides counts below a minimum cell size (commonly under 5 to 11) before leaving the site.

    Query limits and audit

    Limit and log repeated queries so results cannot be subtracted. Add noise (differential privacy) to sensitive outputs where needed.

    Aggregate-only views

    In distributed mode, disable or limit views that show individual patients: Patient data explorer, Timeline, Knowledge graph with case IDs, Cohort details and Exposure → Event → Outcome timelines.

    Suppress before the LLM

    Apply the small-count rule before any summary reaches the LLM, so its text cannot repeat a small count.

    De-identification and access

    Review the replicated data for re-identification risk. Add authentication, per-site approval of analytics and audit logs of every query.

    Multicenter practicalities

    Common data model

    Every site needs the same structure and terminology mapping. FHIR with SNOMED CT, RxNorm and LOINC supports this.

    Analytics that combine

    Counts, rates and Kaplan–Meier curves combine across sites easily.

    Analytics that need care

    Clustering, Cox models and process mining need federated methods or are only valid within one site.

    This describes the intended design, not a statement of what is built today. A privacy or compliance officer should review it against the regulations that apply, such as HIPAA, GDPR or local law.

    Technical details

    VibeRounds is a deterministic-compute, LLM-interpret, human-gate analytics platform for clinical cohort research. It is built as a monorepo with more than 100 registered analytics across three engine versions.

    1. Deterministic computeStatistics come from cohort data through pure functions.
    2. LLM interpretationThe model drafts hypotheses only and never sees case-level data.
    3. Human gateA person approves or rejects every draft.

    Code structure

    packages/core

    Shared domain types, stat utilities (mean, median, Kaplan–Meier, percentile rank), the deterministic synthetic cohort generator, the data-quality gate and the v3 event-stream model.

    packages/analytics (v1, v2, v3)

    Three registries: v1 text-based (113 analytics), v2 terminology-coded (119) and v3 longitudinal (49). Each analytic is a pure cases-to-result module registered by id.

    apps/api

    A Fastify server with routes for cohorts and analytics, version routing, an in-memory audit log and server-side FHIR fetching.

    apps/web

    A React and Vite shell with sidebar, data-source controls, quality badge, cohort builder, Suite, Patient data explorer and analytics pages. Charts use shared line and bar components.

    How each engine version models data

    V1 · text

    Conditions, medications and symptoms are plain text labels. FHIR ingestion keeps only display text and discards codes. Analytics match exact strings. Limits: no hierarchy, no synonyms ("fever" is not "pyrexia"), and each case is a flat snapshot with no sense of time.

    V2 · coded

    Text labels sit beside SNOMED CT, LOINC and RxNorm codes in the same case, with dates for onset, recording and medication start and end. The FHIR loader keeps full Coding objects. Analytics group by code and match by hierarchy, so "all cardiovascular conditions" needs no hardcoded list. V1 keeps running untouched; the API routes by an Accept-Version header (v1 default).

    V3 · event stream

    The atom is the patient event: one dated clinical fact for one patient. A patient's events sorted by time are the primary record; episodes, observation periods and exposure windows derive from it. Every event sits at its own date, and outcomes are dated at discharge, when they became known. No new loaders are needed, because V2 cases already carry dated coded arrays.

    V3 outcome events such as escalation and 90-day readmission are modeled deterministically at import. Stratifying them by age, comorbidity or polypharmacy is therefore circular, and every temporal result should be read with that in mind. The audit log carries this caveat.

    V2 visual and statistics analytics (Cox, FP-Growth, lab plots) are thin wrappers that convert cases to the V3 event stream and call the same implementation, so numbers match across versions. Each version keeps its own quality gate, so a V2 wrapper on text-only data honestly reports weak coverage.

    Data flow and storage

    End-to-end data flow. Dashed boxes are outside the process: FHIR servers, uploaded files, your own database and the LLM providers.

    StageWhat happens
    SourcesA FHIR R4 server (SMART Health IT, HAPI or custom), CSV or NDJSON upload in 8 MB chunks, the synthetic generator, or an existing PostgreSQL database connected through /db/connect.
    StorageSmall cohorts stay in memory as case objects. Database mode bulk-loads PostgreSQL (tables: cohort_cases, analytic_runs, audit_log, llm_hypotheses, llm_settings, saved_cohorts, jobs).
    ComputeAnalytics run as TypeScript over cases, or as SQL aggregation for 100k to 1M+ patients with the same result shapes. Analytics that cannot run as SQL report themselves skipped instead of degrading silently.
    VersioningThe version router picks the v1, v2 or v3 registry. Each run is persisted and audited.
    LLMProvider adapters for OpenRouter, Anthropic, OpenAI and Gemini receive computed summaries only. Keys are held server-side. Unplugging the LLM leaves every deterministic number unchanged.
    Human gateEvery draft stays pending until approved or rejected.
    AuditEvery analytic run, FHIR query, LLM call and gate decision is recorded, in PostgreSQL or memory.

    How the code fits together

    The import graph covers 182 files and 277 import edges.

    Every arrow is a real import found in the source, aggregated for readability. Read it top-down: apps sit on the registries, the registries sit on one shared core, and nothing points upward.

    • Flow is one-way: apps import the registries, the registries import the core, and the core imports none of them. There are no cycles, so every analytic can be tested in isolation.
    • All three registries reach the core through a single entry point that re-exports ten modules.
    • V1 and V2 lean on the same stat helpers (62 and 71 import edges into the core). V3 touches the core far less (7 edges) and routes through the event model.
    • A wrong number traces down one hop at a time: analytic, registry package, core module, pure function.

    How the Suite behaves

    • Version-aware buttons. Every group always shows its full set. An analytic missing from the active engine appears disabled with a version hint, so switching versions never empties a group.
    • Inline page outputs. PICO/PECO Study, Patient data explorer and Knowledge graph open as blocks in the results flow, each with its own remove button. Re-run suite skips them, and save and load keep them.
    • In-place updates. Interactive outputs such as the patient journey map replace their own block instead of appending duplicates.
    • Reload persistence. The active page and the whole suite flow are saved to local storage and restored on reload, with a 4 MB size guard.
    • Pagination. Tables that grow with cohort size paginate at 10, 25, 50 or 100 rows. Bar charts cap at 30 entries, with the long tail in the table.
    • No raw JSON. A fallback renderer turns any result into a summary line and a paginated table.

    Disease workflows

    Each disease module has 15 dedicated passes and 4 decision analytics. Patients carry diseases independently, so a patient with several conditions appears in each disease cohort automatically. Results appear in the Suite flow. Shown here for Hypertension.

    Cohort and trajectory (3)
    Disease cohort summary
    Summarizes the patients with the chosen disease: who they are, how severe it is and how they are doing. Gives a baseline for everything else.
    Disease care trajectory
    Shows how care for the disease unfolds over time. Reveals typical routes and delays.
    Disease index case
    Compares a chosen index group with the rest of the disease cohort. Shows what sets it apart.
    Medication (2)
    Medication comparison
    Compares the drugs or drug classes patients receive and how each group fares. Supports regimen review.
    Choose a regimen (matched-cohort projection)
    Pick a regimen and see the outcomes of similar, matched patients on it. Gives a data-based preview of what to expect.
    Drivers and outcomes (2)
    Variation drivers (multivariate)
    Tests several factors together to see which ones explain differences in outcome. Separates real drivers from noise.
    Closed loop (disease)
    Turns a finding into a new test on the disease cohort. Helps confirm whether it holds up.
    Passes (15)
    Medication class response
    Compares how patients respond to each drug class. Shows which classes work best in this cohort.
    Comorbidity modulation
    Shows how other conditions change the disease's outcomes. Finds patients who need different management.
    Adherence and control
    Links taking medicines as prescribed to how well the disease is controlled. Shows the value of adherence.
    Symptoms (time in range)
    Tracks the share of time a patient's readings stay in the target range. Gives a fuller picture of control than a single visit.
    Resistant hypertension flag
    Flags patients whose blood pressure stays high despite treatment. Finds who may need specialist review.
    Social determinants (SDOH)
    Relates social factors to disease control. Shows non-clinical barriers to care.
    Insurance / coverage
    Compares control and outcomes by insurance type. Shows access gaps.
    Age band
    Breaks results down by age group. Shows who is doing better or worse.
    Sex differences
    Compares outcomes between men and women. Reveals differences in treatment or response.
    Guideline concordance
    Checks whether care follows guideline recommendations. Shows where practice differs.
    Dose tier
    Groups patients by dose level and compares results. Shows how dose relates to control.
    White-coat pattern
    Finds patients whose office readings run higher than their readings elsewhere. Helps avoid overtreating.
    Intensification candidates
    Finds patients who are uncontrolled and may need stronger treatment. Gives a review list.
    ED risk lens
    Estimates which patients are more likely to visit the emergency department. Targets prevention.
    Office-vs-TIR divergence
    Compares office readings with time in range. Shows where a clinic visit misleads.
