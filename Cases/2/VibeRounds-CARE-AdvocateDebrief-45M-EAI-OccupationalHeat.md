# Part A — CARE-Format Case Report

## 1. Title
**45-year-old man with a reticular leg lesion following return to occupational driving and self-treatment with a hot water bottle: erythema ab igne as a teaching model for history-driven diagnosis and avoidance of unnecessary investigation in reticular skin presentations**

## 2. Abstract

**Background:** Reticular (net-like) skin lesions present a recurring diagnostic trap: their visual appearance is shared across a spectrum of conditions ranging from entirely benign and self-limited (erythema ab igne, cutis marmorata) to markers of serious systemic disease (livedo reticularis associated with antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis, or cholesterol embolization). Without a precise exposure history, clinicians risk either missing a benign diagnosis and over-investigating, or — in rarer but higher-stakes cases — missing a systemic disease marker.

**Case Summary:** A 45-year-old professional driver with residual right-hand weakness from a road traffic accident two years prior returned to a physically demanding 12-hour daily driving schedule. Shortly afterward, he developed right leg pain and began self-treating with a hot water bottle applied behind his legs. As the pain progressively worsened over the following days, he presented to hospital, where examination revealed a reticular erythematous lesion on the posterior right leg. The lesion was diagnosed clinically as erythema ab igne, attributed to the hot water bottle exposure, without need for laboratory testing or biopsy. Management consisted of advising avoidance of the heat source.

**Key Learning Points:**
1. The single most useful discriminator between erythema ab igne and its reticular-lesion mimics (livedo reticularis, cutis marmorata) is the direction of temperature exposure (heat versus cold) in the patient's history, not the lesion's visual appearance.
2. A confident, history-congruent clinical diagnosis is sufficient grounds to avoid unnecessary investigation, provided no red-flag features (systemic symptoms, atypical morphology, incongruent history) are present.
3. Chronic, repeated heat exposure — even at sub-burn temperatures over a relatively short period — can produce reticular hyperpigmentation through cumulative superficial vascular injury and hemosiderin deposition, and carries a long-term, distant risk of malignant transformation if exposure becomes chronic and recurrent.

**Conclusion:** This case demonstrates that accurate, efficient diagnosis of a visually ambiguous skin finding depends on a single, well-targeted history question more than on investigation, and illustrates a recognizable occupational-medicine pattern in which return-to-work timing and self-directed symptom management together produce an avoidable secondary condition.

## 3. Introduction

Erythema ab igne is clinically important precisely because it is easy to miss or to over-investigate: its reticular morphology overlaps visually with livedo reticularis, a condition that can signal serious systemic disease requiring urgent autoimmune or vascular workup. This case is educationally valuable because it presents a clean, classic example where a precise exposure history alone — without any laboratory test — was sufficient for confident diagnosis and correct management, and because the precipitating chain of events (occupational return-to-work, residual injury, and self-directed heat therapy) offers a teaching opportunity beyond the dermatological diagnosis itself, into occupational medicine and patient self-management behavior.

*What happened:* The diagnosis was made entirely on history (heat exposure) and visual pattern recognition, without any laboratory test.
*What should have happened:* This is, in fact, what should have happened, and the report uses the case specifically to model this correct approach.

*What would prevent under-recognition in future cases:* Embedding a standard heat-exposure history question into the routine assessment of any reticular skin lesion, so the diagnosis is reached by default questioning rather than relying on an individual clinician happening to think of it.

**Teaching Point 2 — Visual morphology alone cannot discriminate this differential; only history can.**
*What happened:* Three conditions with overlapping reticular appearance were considered, and the diagnosis was resolved by exposure history rather than lesion appearance.
*What should have happened:* This is again the correct approach demonstrated by the case, included as a teaching point because the underlying principle (history over appearance, for this specific lesion type) generalizes well beyond this one patient.
*Why the gap occurred:* N/A.
*What would prevent under-recognition in future cases:* Teaching the heat-versus-cold exposure discriminator explicitly alongside the visual image of erythema ab igne, rather than presenting the image in isolation, since the image alone cannot teach the discriminating logic.

**Teaching Point 3 — A self-directed return to physically demanding work, paired with self-treatment rather than medical evaluation, produced an avoidable secondary condition.**
*What happened:* The patient returned to 12-hour driving shifts shortly after a period of presumed reduced activity (following his accident), developed new leg pain, and self-treated with a hot water bottle rather than seeking medical assessment until the pain became severe.
*What should have happened:* Earlier medical evaluation of the new leg pain — before escalating self-treatment with prolonged heat application — could have identified and addressed the underlying musculoskeletal issue without producing the secondary skin finding.
*Why the gap occurred:* This reflects a common and understandable pattern of self-management for non-severe symptoms, particularly when returning to work creates pressure to manage minor discomfort independently rather than seek care.
*What would prevent it:* Return-to-work guidance following injury, especially for physically demanding occupations, could include explicit counseling on when self-treatment of new symptoms (especially prolonged heat application) warrants seeking care rather than continued self-management.

# Part B — Patient-Journey Debrief

**Opening:** A patient-journey debrief, adapted here from the standard advocate debrief format, is not a critique of the patient's self-management choices — it is a structured clinical-systems analysis of the decision points in this patient's own journey from injury to self-treatment to presentation, examining what information would have changed his choices at each step. Because no family caregiver or advocate narrative is present in this source, the framework is applied directly to the patient as his own primary decision-maker and historian.

**The Patient's Role:** Across this case, the patient functioned as: (1) **his own injury historian**, providing the accident history and persistent hand weakness as relevant background; (2) **self-directed symptom manager**, choosing to treat new leg pain with a hot water bottle rather than seeking medical evaluation at onset; (3) **eventual care-seeker**, presenting to hospital once the pain (and, unknowingly, the resulting skin lesion) had progressed.

### Inflection Point 1 — Return to a physically demanding driving job shortly after a road traffic accident with residual weakness
**What happened:** The patient returned to 12-hour daily driving shifts 12 days before presentation, despite ongoing right-hand weakness dating back two years to his accident.
**What the patient likely understood:** That his hand weakness, having persisted stably for two years, was a fixed, manageable baseline that did not preclude returning to his driving occupation.
**What the patient needed to know:** That returning to a physically demanding role after a period of reduced activity can place new strain on other parts of the body (in this case, apparently the legs) as it compensates for the existing impairment, and that new musculoskeletal symptoms following such a transition warrant prompt evaluation rather than assumption that they will resolve on their own.
**The question the patient needed to ask:** "Now that I'm going back to long driving shifts, should I expect any new aches or pains, and when should I get those checked rather than just managing them myself?"
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 19, occupational return-to-work counseling framework.

### Inflection Point 2 — Onset of new right leg pain
**What happened:** Shortly after resuming driving, the patient developed right leg pain.
**What the patient likely understood:** Likely that this was a minor, expected consequence of long hours of driving — a reasonable interpretation for new musculoskeletal discomfort in this context.
**What the patient needed to know:** That new, persistent pain following a significant change in activity level (rather than typical, mild, self-limiting soreness) is worth a brief medical check, particularly given his existing injury history, rather than immediate self-treatment.
**The question the patient needed to ask:** "Is this leg pain something I should just manage at home, or does my driving and past accident history mean I should get it looked at first?"
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 11, patient education framework for when self-management is and is not appropriate.

### Inflection Point 3 — Choice to self-treat with a hot water bottle, escalating as pain worsened
**What happened:** The patient applied a hot water bottle behind his legs for relief, continuing and apparently increasing this practice as the pain persisted and worsened.
**What the patient likely understood:** That heat is a generally safe, common, over-the-counter remedy for musculoskeletal pain — a reasonable and widely-held belief, since heat therapy is indeed appropriate for many types of muscular discomfort.
**What the patient needed to know:** That prolonged, repeated, direct heat application to the same area of skin carries its own distinct risk (skin injury and reticular hyperpigmentation) separate from whatever was causing the original pain, and that if pain is not improving with home heat therapy within a short period, this is itself a signal to seek evaluation rather than to continue or intensify the same approach.
**The question the patient needed to ask:** "If using heat on my leg isn't helping after a few days, should I stop and see a doctor instead of continuing or using it more?"
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 11, red-flag table for escalating home remedies without improvement.

| Red Flag | Plain-Language Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Pain worsening despite home heat therapy for several days | The remedy isn't working and may itself be causing a separate problem | Stop the heat source and seek medical evaluation rather than continuing or intensifying use |
| New skin changes appearing where heat is applied | This can be a sign of skin injury from the heat itself, not the original pain source | Mention this specifically to a clinician, as it changes the diagnostic picture |

### Inflection Point 4 — Eventual presentation to hospital
**What happened:** Worsening pain eventually prompted the patient to present to hospital, where the skin lesion was identified and correctly diagnosed.
**What the patient likely understood:** That his pain had reached a threshold requiring professional evaluation.
**What the patient needed to know:** This decision point represents the correct eventual action; the learning value here is in recognizing that earlier presentation (at Inflection Point 2 or 3) could likely have avoided the secondary skin finding altogether, without changing the fact that seeking care now was the right call.
**The question the patient needed to ask:** At this stage, simply: "Can you tell me what's causing both the pain and this skin pattern, and are they related?" — which appears to be exactly the question that led to the correct diagnosis being made.
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 2, real-time information capture at point of presentation.

He sought care once his self-management was clearly not resolving the problem, rather than continuing indefinitely. **Patient Learning Summary** *(Module 2, Step 2.8 — Bloom's Remember → Understand → Apply)*:

1. **Remember:** "What caused the pattern on my leg?"
   *Model answer:* "Repeated, prolonged contact with the hot water bottle damaged the small blood vessels near the surface of my skin, which is what created the net-like pattern and discoloration — not the original pain itself."

2. **Understand:** "Why didn't the doctors need to run any tests to figure this out?"
   *Model answer:* "Because the pattern of the marks matched exactly where I'd been applying the hot water bottle, and the timing matched too — when the story and the appearance line up that clearly, no further testing is usually needed."

3. **Apply:** "If I get new pain again in the future, what should I do differently?"
   *Model answer:* "If a home remedy like heat isn't helping within a few days, or if I notice any new skin changes where I'm applying it, I should stop and get it checked rather than continuing or using more heat — especially given my ongoing hand weakness from my accident, which means new pains elsewhere are worth a closer look."

**Recommendations for Future Similar Cases:**
1. **For the clinical team:** When taking history for any reticular skin lesion, make the heat-versus-cold exposure question a standard, explicit part of the assessment, rather than relying on the patient volunteering this detail unprompted.
2. **For occupational/return-to-work counseling:** Provide explicit guidance to patients returning to physically demanding roles after injury about monitoring for new symptoms elsewhere in the body and seeking evaluation rather than indefinite self-treatment.
3. **For patient education on home remedies:** Standard counseling on heat therapy for musculoskeletal pain should include a specific caution about prolonged or repeated direct skin contact and a clear threshold ("if not improved in X days, seek care") for escalating to medical evaluation.
4. **For the published case-report format itself:** Even brief "Images in Clinical Medicine" reports could include a single line on the status of unrelated but clinically relevant chronic findings mentioned in the history (here, the right-hand weakness), to avoid leaving an open clinical thread unaddressed for the reader.
