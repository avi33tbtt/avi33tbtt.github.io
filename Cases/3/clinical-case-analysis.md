# Clinical Case Report & Analysis
## Adolescent Pancytopenia with Dual Nutritional Deficiency Anaemia and Jaundice

**Source:** E-Log by P. Ruchitha Reddy | June 30, 2020
**Institution:** Kamineni Institute of Medical Sciences, Nalgonda, Telangana

---

## Case Report at a Glance

> A 15-year-old girl from a low-income rural family presents with a month-long decline — fatigue, anorexia, and headache — culminating in jaundice, bilious vomiting, and splenomegaly. Investigations reveal pancytopenia, megaloblastic peripheral smear, and iron deficiency, pointing to a dual nutritional deficiency unmasked by years of inadequate diet and a missed earlier diagnosis.

---

## Top Insights

1. **Dual deficiency anaemia masquerading as haemolysis** — The unconjugated jaundice is not from red cell destruction in the periphery, but from *intramedullary haemolysis* of defective megaloblasts — a distinction critical for management.

2. **Pancytopenia ≠ Aplastic Anaemia** — The megaloblastic smear (macro-ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils) differentiates nutritional pancytopenia from aplastic anaemia; these patients respond dramatically to B12/folate replacement.

3. **A missed diagnosis at age 11** — She was treated for anaemia 4 years ago without adequate workup; the root cause (nutritional deficiency) was never addressed, leading to this more severe presentation.

4. **Herbal medication: a confounding risk** — Self-treatment with herbal remedies for 1 week likely compounded hepatic stress; the green vomiting and LFT derangement may partly reflect hepatotoxicity.

5. **The heart is telling a story** — A palpable thrill and flow murmur in a young girl without cardiac history is almost always hyperdynamic circulation from severe anaemia, not structural disease. These resolve with anaemia correction.

6. **Splenomegaly in nutritional anaemia** — Often overlooked, the 3 cm splenomegaly represents extramedullary haematopoiesis — the spleen stepping in to compensate for a dysplastic, exhausted marrow.

7. **Missed menstrual period** — Amenorrhoea in the setting of severe anaemia at menarche onset reflects HPA-axis disruption from physiological stress — but pregnancy should be formally excluded.

## 12. Clinical Analysis & Commentary

### Pathophysiology — How It All Connects

```
Poor diet (low B12 + low iron)
           ↓
Impaired DNA synthesis in bone marrow (B12 deficiency)
           ↓
Megaloblastic transformation — all precursor cell lines affected
           ↓
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Erythroblasts  │  Myeloblasts  │  Megakaryocytes  │
│  → Anaemia      │  → Leucopenia │  → Thrombocytopenia│
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
           ↓
  PANCYTOPENIA
           +
Intramedullary destruction of abnormal megaloblasts
           ↓
Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia → JAUNDICE (pre-hepatic)
           +
Low serum iron → Iron deficiency → DIMORPHIC ANAEMIA
           +
Severe anaemia → Hyperdynamic circulation → FLOW MURMUR + THRILL
           +
Marrow insufficiency → Extramedullary haematopoiesis → SPLENOMEGALY
           +
Herbal medication → Hepatotoxicity → Worsened LFT + Bilious vomiting
```

### Key Insights

**1. Dual Nutritional Deficiency:**
This patient demonstrates a classic presentation of combined iron deficiency and Vitamin B12 deficiency anaemia — commonly termed *dual deficiency anaemia* — seen in adolescent girls from resource-limited settings in India. The iron deficiency contributes to microcytic changes while B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic transformation; together, they may produce a "dimorphic" blood picture.

**2. Pancytopenia from Megaloblastic Marrow:**
B12 deficiency doesn't just affect red cells — it impairs DNA synthesis in all rapidly dividing cells, including myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages, resulting in pancytopenia. This is distinct from aplastic anaemia and responds dramatically to replacement therapy.

**3. Jaundice Mechanism:**
The jaundice here is likely of the *haemolytic/ineffective erythropoiesis* type — unconjugated bilirubin rises as abnormal megaloblasts are destroyed intramedullary (before even entering circulation). This is distinct from obstructive or hepatocellular jaundice.

**4. Role of Herbal Medication:**
The 1-week history of herbal remedy use (self-medication for jaundice) is clinically significant and should prompt suspicion for hepatotoxicity as a confounding factor in the LFT derangement.

**5. Splenomegaly in Context:**
Splenomegaly (3 cm) is a notable finding. In megaloblastic anaemia, this can arise from extramedullary haematopoiesis or from mild haemolysis of poorly constructed red cells. This should be monitored at follow-up.

**6. Missed Menstrual Cycle:**
The missed period in a girl who recently attained menarche (6 months prior) likely reflects the physiological stress of severe anaemia and nutritional deficiency, rather than pregnancy. However, this should be assessed at follow-up.

**7. Cardiac Changes:**
The presence of a palpable thrill and flow murmur are hallmark findings in severe anaemia — the heart compensates by increasing cardiac output, producing hyperdynamic circulation. These typically resolve with correction of anaemia.

## 14. Summary

A 15-year-old adolescent girl from a low-income agricultural household in Nalgonda, Telangana presented with a 1-month history of generalised weakness, loss of appetite, and headache, followed by 1 week of jaundice and 3 days of vomiting. She had a prior episode of anaemia at age 11. Examination revealed pallor, icterus, splenomegaly, and a hyperdynamic cardiovascular state (flow murmur, thrill). Investigations showed pancytopenia, low serum iron, and a peripheral smear consistent with megaloblastic anaemia. The working diagnosis is **pancytopenia secondary to combined Vitamin B12 and iron deficiency anaemia**, aggravated by self-medication with herbal remedies. She was treated with parenteral B-complex/B12, iron, folic acid, anti-emetics, and gastric protection, with continued oral supplementation at discharge.
