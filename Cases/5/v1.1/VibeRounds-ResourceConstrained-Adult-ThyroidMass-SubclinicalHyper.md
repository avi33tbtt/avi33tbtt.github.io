## Step 14.3 — Substitution & Workaround: What Replaces the Unavailable Tier-1 Tests

### Substitute 1: Serum calcitonin (MTC exclusion) — unavailable

**Best available local substitute:** There is no biochemical substitute for calcitonin at the bedside. The honest workaround is **clinical screening**, which has real but limited sensitivity:
- Family history of thyroid cancer, phaeochromocytoma, or unexplained sudden death in relatives (MEN2 is autosomal dominant — a positive family history is the single highest-yield, zero-cost clue available)
- Personal or family history of recurrent kidney stones, marfanoid habitus, mucosal neuromas (MEN2B features)
- Diarrhoea (calcitonin-driven, seen in established MTC with higher tumour burden)
- Firm, hard, non-mobile nodule on palpation — favors malignancy generally but does not distinguish MTC specifically

**Known limitation versus gold standard:** Clinical screening alone will miss early or sporadic MTC with no family history — this is the known, accepted limitation, not a hidden one. It is a sensitivity trade the team is making explicitly, not silently.

**Safety threshold — when absence of calcitonin becomes dangerous enough to mandate referral regardless of cost/distance:** If FNAC returns a Bethesda V/VI result, or if frozen section at surgery shows a C-cell / amyloid-stroma pattern suggestive of MTC, **that result alone is the trigger** — refer for RET testing and consider need for completion surgery with central neck dissection, even after a first surgery, rather than treating the absence of pre-op calcitonin as something to work around indefinitely.

### Substitute 2: Plasma metanephrines / urine catecholamines (phaeochromocytoma exclusion) — unavailable

**Best available local substitute:** Again, no biochemical substitute exists. The workaround is a **structured clinical screen before any general anaesthetic**:
- Ask specifically about episodic headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, and paroxysmal hypertension (the classic triad) — these may not have been volunteered spontaneously
- **Take and document a blood pressure reading** — this single missing data point (not listed anywhere in the original case record) is the highest-yield, zero-cost action available right now
- Family history, as above, doubles as MEN2 screening for this purpose too

**Known limitation:** A normal clinical screen does not exclude a small, biochemically silent phaeochromocytoma the way a metanephrine level would. This is accepted as a residual risk, not eliminated.

**Safety threshold:** Any documented hypertension (especially paroxysmal or labile), or any positive family history pattern, should **mandate referral for biochemical phaeochromocytoma screening before listing for surgery**, regardless of the distance or cost involved — induction of anaesthesia in an unscreened phaeochromocytoma is one of the few genuinely irreversible, high-mortality risks in this entire case, and "make do" reasoning should not extend to this specific threshold.

### Substitute 3: Molecular testing (ThyroSeq/Afirma) for Bethesda III/IV — unavailable

**Best available local substitute:** None exists locally; this is not a "find a cheaper version" situation — it is a genuine gap. The actual workaround is **accepting a lower threshold for diagnostic surgery**: in settings without molecular re-stratification, Bethesda III/IV nodules are more often taken to diagnostic hemithyroidectomy directly, since the alternative (repeat FNAC, observation) trades a small but real miss-rate for malignancy against an already-resource-constrained follow-up system where the patient may not reliably return.

**Known limitation:** This converts some patients who would have avoided surgery (in a molecular-testing setting) into surgical patients. This is a genuine trade-off of this resource tier, not a clinical error — name it as such to the patient and to learners, not as "we couldn't do the proper test."

**Safety threshold:** Not applicable in the referral sense — this substitution *is* the local standard of care at this resource tier, not a bridge to something else.

## Step 14.4 — Empirical Management: Acting Before Confirmatory Tests Return (or Without Them At All)

**Working diagnosis to commit to now, given the ceiling:** A suspicious thyroid mass requiring tissue diagnosis, in a patient with symptomatic biochemical hyperthyroidism, where MTC and phaeochromocytoma cannot be biochemically excluded pre-operatively and must instead be managed via clinical screening and an explicit referral threshold (per 14.3).

**Empirical management plan (commit first, before next section):**
1. ECG and BP today — both zero-cost, both on-site, both currently undocumented
2. Targeted history: family history (thyroid cancer, phaeo, sudden death), paroxysmal symptoms, voice change, weight loss quantified by actual weighing
3. Start beta-blockade (propranolol, if in stock) for symptomatic control while awaiting USG/FNAC scheduling — this does not require calcitonin or metanephrine results first
4. Proceed to USG and FNAC as locally available, accepting realistic turnaround
5. If Bethesda III/IV: proceed toward surgery rather than molecular testing, per 14.3
6. If frozen section or final histology raises MTC suspicion: **this is the trigger point**, not pre-op calcitonin

**Exit criterion — the specific sign that means this empirical plan has failed and escalation/referral is mandatory:**
- Any documented hypertension, paroxysmal symptoms, or relevant family history → refer for phaeochromocytoma biochemical workup **before** surgery, full stop, regardless of distance
- Stridor, voice change, or rapidly progressive swelling over days (not weeks) → reconsider ATC/lymphoma, refer urgently rather than continuing routine FNAC sequencing
- Resting HR not controlled below a safe threshold despite beta-blockade → defer surgery and escalate rather than proceeding on schedule
- Frozen section suggestive of MTC, anaplastic features, or lymphoid pattern → stop, do not extend resection, refer per the original case's pathology-integration guidance (CaseAnalysis §4.4)

## Step 14.5 — Referral Threshold & Transport-Reality Check

This case contains **two referral decisions that must not be conflated**, because they carry different urgency and different risk-of-delay profiles:

**Referral question A — Scintigraphy (hot vs. cold nodule mapping):**
- Risk of deterioration in transit: low — this is an elective, non-urgent referral
- Risk of delay if deferred: moderate — without it, the team cannot distinguish "hot nodule, low malignancy risk" from "cold nodule, full malignancy risk" by isotope mapping; FNAC partially substitutes for this distinction but does not fully replace it
- What can be monitored locally vs. not: TSH/T3/T4 trend can be monitored locally; functional mapping cannot
- Family practicality: weigh travel cost and number of trips against the marginal diagnostic value scintigraphy adds over USG + FNAC alone — in many resource-constrained pathways, **scintigraphy is reasonably deferred or omitted** if FNAC is obtainable promptly, since FNAC result drives the next step regardless
- **Lean:** Often manageable without referral if FNAC is accessible in reasonable time — justify this explicitly in the notes rather than defaulting to "refer because guideline says scintigraphy."

**Referral question B — Phaeochromocytoma biochemical screening (if clinical screen in 14.3 is positive):**
- Risk of deterioration in transit: low for the screening trip itself
- Risk of delay if NOT done before surgery: **high and potentially fatal** — unscreened phaeochromocytoma undergoing anaesthetic induction is a recognised cause of intraoperative death
- What can be monitored locally vs. not: BP trend and symptom pattern can be monitored locally; the biochemical confirmation cannot
- Family practicality: this referral should be framed to the family as non-negotiable if the clinical screen is positive, not as one option among several — Module 14's "do not assume referral is always safer" caveat does **not** apply here; this is one of the genuine exceptions where referral is unambiguously indicated regardless of distance
- **Lean:** Refer, full stop, if any positive clinical screen element is present.

## Step 14.8 — Missed Low-Cost Clue Debrief (Specific to This Re-Grounding)

The original case record, even in its high-resource framing, was missing several genuinely zero-cost, locally-available data points that matter *more*, not less, once expensive confirmatory tests are off the table:

1. **Blood pressure** — never documented anywhere in the original case files, despite being the single most relevant low-cost substitute for excluding phaeochromocytoma clinically. This is the highest-yield missed clue in the whole case.
2. **Family history** — zero-cost, takes two minutes, and is the only available substitute for both MTC and MEN2/phaeochromocytoma screening when biochemical testing is unavailable. Its absence from the original record is more consequential here than it would be at a fully-resourced centre.
3. **Actual weight, not just "loss of appetite"** — a scale is rarely a scarce resource even at a constrained facility; quantified weight loss is a stronger and cheaper discriminator between hyperthyroid-driven anorexia and malignancy-driven constitutional symptoms than any test on the original Tier 1 list.
4. **Exact heart rate** — needed to titrate beta-blockade safely; a manual pulse count costs nothing and was never recorded.

These four are easy to overlook specifically *because* the original (resource-rich) framing buried them under a long list of investigations — when the expensive tests are stripped away, free clinical data becomes proportionally more important, and that is the skill this case is best suited to teach.

## Step 14.9 — System-Level Critical Awareness

**(1) Reasoning shortcuts here that would be unsafe at a better-resourced centre:** Treating Bethesda III/IV as a direct surgical indication (Step 14.3, Substitute 3) is locally appropriate but would be a step backward in a setting where molecular re-stratification is available — if this learner later rotates through a better-resourced centre, this specific habit needs conscious unlearning, not carry-over.

**(2) Where empirical/pattern-based reasoning could fail silently here:** Relying on clinical screening alone for phaeochromocytoma (Step 14.3, Substitute 2) has a real, non-zero false-negative rate. A biochemically silent phaeochromocytoma with no hypertension and no family history would pass this screen undetected, and the failure would only become apparent intraoperatively — this is the single most dangerous silent-failure mode in this entire case.

**(3) What a critic would say:** That substituting clinical judgement for missing biochemical tests systematically under-detects rare but catastrophic diagnoses (MTC, phaeochromocytoma), and that calling this "resource-constrained reasoning" risks normalising a real gap in patient safety rather than naming it as a gap to be escalated at a system level.

**(4) Unresolved uncertainty no local resourcefulness can substitute for:** Whether this specific patient has a biochemically silent phaeochromocytoma. No amount of careful history-taking, BP monitoring, or clinical acumen closes this gap completely — it is a genuine ceiling, not a skill deficiency, and should be named as such to the patient/family and flagged as a system-level resource gap (point 3 below), not absorbed silently into routine practice.

## Flag for Supervisor / System-Level Improvement (separate from this individual case)

Calcitonin and metanephrine testing being unavailable on-site at a facility carrying "tertiary" designation is a structural gap with patient-safety implications beyond this one case — particularly because thyroid mass + neck surgery is common, recurring referral pathway work, not a rare presentation. Worth raising as a service-level gap (e.g., negotiating a standing send-out arrangement with turnaround SLA, rather than ad hoc per-patient referral) rather than re-solving individually each time it arises.
