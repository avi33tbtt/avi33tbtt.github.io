# Part A — CARE-Format Case Report (Prospective)

### 1. Title
*Adult, sex not documented, with thyroid swelling and pressure symptoms suspected for malignancy, and coexisting biochemical subclinical hyperthyroidism: a prospective case illustrating the causal interplay between functional and structural thyroid disease in pre-biopsy work-up planning.*

### 2. Abstract

**Background:** Thyroid nodules presenting with both compressive symptoms and biochemical hyperthyroidism pose a specific diagnostic challenge, because the two findings can either share a single causal mechanism (a hyperfunctioning nodule) or represent two unrelated processes — with materially different implications for malignancy risk and procedural safety. This case is educationally valuable for illustrating how functional thyroid status should actively inform, rather than sit alongside, the structural malignancy work-up.

**Case Summary (≤150 words):** An adult patient presented with a 2-month history of anorexia and a more recently noticed thyroid swelling causing pressure symptoms. The swelling was labelled "suspected malignancy," with FNAC and excision biopsy planned. Examination additionally revealed mild lid lag, tremor, and tachycardia, with biochemistry showing subclinical hyperthyroidism. Full examination detail (nodule characteristics, vocal cord status, lymphadenopathy) and key investigations (TSH/fT4/fT3 values, thyroid USG, scintigraphy, TRAb) were not documented at the time of this analysis. The case is prospective; biopsy and outcome are pending.

**Key Learning Points:**
1. Biochemical hyperthyroidism, if traceable to the palpable nodule itself, is mechanistically associated with *lower* malignancy risk for that nodule — a counterintuitive but clinically important point.
2. Anorexia is script-discordant for classic thyrotoxicosis and should raise (not lower) suspicion for malignancy or atypical thyrotoxicosis.
3. Excision biopsy under uncontrolled thyrotoxicosis carries thyroid storm risk; FNAC does not carry this same constraint.

**Conclusion:** This case illustrates why structural and functional thyroid abnormalities must be causally — not just additively — reasoned through before biopsy planning and risk communication proceed.

### 3. Introduction
This case is clinically and educationally important because it represents a commonly encountered but easily mishandled combination: a thyroid mass under malignancy suspicion, occurring alongside biochemical evidence of autonomous or autoimmune thyroid hyperfunction. The gap in practice this exposes is the tendency to treat "the lump" and "the hormone numbers" as two parallel problems running on separate tracks, rather than recognising that the functional finding can directly inform — and in some scenarios, substantially revise — the structural malignancy risk estimate. What this case contributes beyond standard teaching material is a worked demonstration of causal-network reasoning applied prospectively, before results are known, modelling the kind of forward reasoning a clinician should perform at first presentation rather than only in retrospective case review.

### 10. Discussion

**Teaching point 1 — Functional findings should actively reweight structural risk, not sit beside it.**
*What happened (projected risk):* The biochemical hyperthyroidism risks being filed as an incidental comorbidity alongside the suspected-malignant swelling.
*What should happen:* The functional finding should be used to actively revise the malignancy probability for the specific nodule, contingent on scintigraphy localisation.
*Why the gap could occur:* Clinicians commonly reason about structural and functional thyroid disease using separate mental checklists, even when one gland produces both findings.
*What would prevent it:* Routinely pairing thyroid USG with scintigraphy whenever a nodule and biochemical thyrotoxicosis coexist, before finalising the malignancy risk narrative.

**Teaching point 2 — Anorexia is a script-discordant clue that is easy to absorb into the wrong diagnosis.**
*What happened (projected risk):* Anorexia could be passively attributed to "thyrotoxicosis causing weight loss" without registering that appetite *direction* is atypical for that diagnosis.
*What should happen:* Appetite direction should be explicitly elicited and treated as a discriminating feature, actively raising suspicion for malignancy or an atypical thyrotoxicosis variant.
*Why the gap could occur:* Surface-level pattern matching ("hyperthyroid → weight loss → appetite loss") conflates weight loss with appetite loss, which are mechanistically distinct in this context.
*What would prevent it:* Explicitly asking "has your appetite increased or decreased?" as a distinct question from "have you lost weight?" in any thyrotoxicosis work-up.

**Teaching point 3 — Procedural sequencing should be safety-gated by adrenergic control, not assumed uniform across FNAC and excision biopsy.**
*What happened (projected risk):* FNAC and excision biopsy could be treated as a single "biopsy plan" with uniform timing, without recognising that excision biopsy carries thyroid storm risk that FNAC does not.
*What should happen:* FNAC can proceed without delay; excision biopsy should be sequenced after adrenergic control (beta-blockade) given the documented tremor and tachycardia.
*Why the gap could occur:* "FNAC and excision biopsy" can be mentally grouped as one step in care planning, obscuring their different risk profiles.
*What would prevent it:* Explicit, separate safety checklists for needle sampling versus surgical/excisional procedures in thyrotoxic patients.

# Part B — Advocate Debrief (Prospective Framework)

**Opening:** An advocate debrief is a structured analysis of what a patient's family caregiver or support person needs to know and ask at each stage of a clinical journey — not a critique of the family, but a systems-level look at what healthcare encounters should proactively communicate, and where gaps in understanding commonly form. Because this case has no documented advocate and is still prospective, the inflection points below are framed as **what an advocate would need to know and ask going forward**, modelled on the clinical reasoning already developed in this analysis, rather than a review of what an actual advocate did or didn't do.

**The Advocate's Role (projected):** In a case of this kind, a family caregiver would likely serve as historian (helping clarify symptom timeline and appetite changes, which the patient themselves may underreport), procedural support (accompanying the patient to FNAC/biopsy, helping process the "suspected malignancy" framing emotionally), and question-asker at each results discussion.

**Inflection Point Analysis** *(minimum 5, as required by protocol; all projected/prospective given the case stage):*

### Inflection Point 1 — Being told "suspected malignancy" before any tissue diagnosis
**What happened:** The clinical team has labelled the swelling as suspicious for malignancy and planned FNAC/excision biopsy.
**What the advocate understood (likely interpretation):** That cancer is already strongly suspected or even effectively diagnosed, and the biopsy is merely a formality.
**What the advocate needed to know:** That "suspected" means a nodule meets criteria for biopsy evaluation — many such nodules turn out benign — and that FNAC is the standard next step for *any* nodule of this type, not a sign that malignancy is already confirmed.
**The question the advocate needed to ask:** *"What specifically makes you suspicious of cancer here, and how often do swellings like this one turn out to be benign?"*
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 11 (Patient Education Query Intelligence).

### Inflection Point 2 — Learning the thyroid is also "overactive" (subclinical hyperthyroidism)
**What happened:** Blood tests show subclinical hyperthyroidism alongside the swelling.
**What the advocate understood (likely interpretation):** That this is a second, separate, possibly equally serious problem stacking on top of the cancer concern.
**What the advocate needed to know:** That this finding may actually be connected to the swelling itself, and if so, is more often a sign of a *benign* cause — not necessarily a second serious diagnosis.
**The question the advocate needed to ask:** *"Could the overactive thyroid and the lump be caused by the same thing? Is there a scan that can tell us that?"*
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 18 (Causal Network Reasoning); Module 17 (Semantic Qualifiers).

### Inflection Point 3 — Noticing tremor and a racing heart in the patient
**What happened:** The patient has visible tremor and a fast heartbeat alongside everything else.
**What the advocate understood (likely interpretation):** That this is a worsening or alarming sign, possibly that the cancer is "spreading" or the patient is getting sicker overall.
**What the advocate needed to know:** That these are specific signs of the thyroid hormone effect (not the swelling or any cancer), and that they are medically meaningful because they need to be controlled with medication before any surgery — not because they signal disease progression.
**The question the advocate needed to ask:** *"Is the racing heart and shaking connected to the lump being cancerous, or is that a separate thing from the thyroid hormone? Does this need treating before the biopsy?"*
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 4 (Pre-Procedure Preparation); Module 11 (Patient Education).

### Inflection Point 4 — Being told FNAC and excision biopsy are both planned
**What happened:** Two procedures are mentioned together as the plan.
**What the advocate understood (likely interpretation):** That these are essentially the same kind of procedure happening close together, or that one automatically follows the other.
**What the advocate needed to know:** That FNAC is a simple needle sampling test that can usually happen promptly, while excision biopsy is a surgical procedure that may need to wait until the heart rate and tremor are better controlled — these are different in scale, risk, and possibly timing.
**The question the advocate needed to ask:** *"Are these two separate procedures with separate timings? Does the racing heart need to be treated before the bigger one (excision biopsy) happens?"*
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 4 (Ward Round / Pre-Procedure Preparation).

### Inflection Point 5 — Discussing the appetite loss as part of the history
**What happened:** The patient's 2-month appetite loss is recorded as part of the presenting history.
**What the advocate understood (likely interpretation):** That this is just "feeling unwell" or stress-related, not necessarily clinically significant on its own.
**What the advocate needed to know:** That whether appetite has *increased* or *decreased* is actually an important clue doctors use to figure out what's going on — and that decreased appetite specifically is worth mentioning clearly, since it points in a different direction than the usual pattern for an overactive thyroid.
**The question the advocate needed to ask:** *"Does it matter that the appetite went down rather than up? I've heard overactive thyroids usually increase appetite — does this change what you're considering?"*
**VibeRounds module applied:** Module 15 (Illness Script Acquisition).

**What the Advocate Did Well (projected — applicable framing given no documented advocate exists yet):** While no specific advocate actions are documented in this prospective case, the underlying clinical picture itself reflects appropriate care-seeking behaviour: the appetite change was recognised and reported over a 2-month period rather than dismissed, and the swelling was brought to clinical attention promptly enough that pressure symptoms — rather than airway compromise — were the presenting concern. Bringing a 2-month systemic symptom and a newer local symptom together to clinical attention in the same presentation, rather than addressing them as unrelated separate complaints, reflects good observational continuity, whether performed by the patient or a supporting advocate.

**Advocate Learning Summary** *(Bloom's Remember → Understand → Apply, as three questions with model answers an advocate should be able to answer once results are available):*

1. **Remember:** *"What were the two main things found at this visit?"*
   *Model answer:* A thyroid swelling that needs a biopsy to check if it's cancer, and blood tests showing the thyroid is working a little too hard (causing the racing heart and shaking).

2. **Understand:** *"Why might these two things be connected, and why does that matter?"*
   *Model answer:* If the same lump that's overactive is the one being biopsied, that's actually a hint it might be less likely to be cancer — because overactive lumps are usually benign. A special scan can check if they're connected.

3. **Apply:** *"What should I watch for or ask about before the bigger procedure (excision biopsy)?"*
   *Model answer:* I should ask whether the racing heart and shaking have been brought under control with medication before the surgery happens, since operating while the heart is racing carries its own risk that's separate from the cancer question.

**Recommendations for Future Similar Cases:**
1. *For the clinical team:* Pair thyroid USG with scintigraphy whenever a palpable nodule and biochemical thyrotoxicosis coexist, before finalising malignancy-risk communication to the patient/family.
2. *For the prescribing clinician:* Proactively assess and treat adrenergic symptoms (beta-blockade) before excision biopsy, rather than waiting for the patient/advocate to raise concern about the tremor/tachycardia.
3. *For the discharge/pre-procedure process:* Provide an explicit plain-language explanation distinguishing FNAC from excision biopsy — their purpose, risk level, and expected sequencing — at the point both are first mentioned together.
4. *For the healthcare system:* Standardise a pre-biopsy checklist for thyroid nodules with coexisting thyrotoxicosis that bundles USG, scintigraphy, TRAb, and laryngoscopy as a single ordered set, rather than leaving their inclusion to individual clinician discretion.
5. *For patient communication broadly:* Explicitly separate "structural" (the lump) and "functional" (the hormone level) explanations in any conversation involving both, given how easily patients and families conflate the two into a single worsening narrative.
