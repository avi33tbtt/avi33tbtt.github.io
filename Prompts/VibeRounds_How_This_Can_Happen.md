## The Starting Point: A Single Case, Three Layers Deep

Every large system begins with a single, solvable problem. For VibeRounds, that problem is the one that has frustrated medical education for decades: how do you teach a clinician not just *what* to think, but *how* to think — especially when the case in front of them refuses to fit neatly into a textbook script?

Module 22 — Nested Analysis — answers that question with a structural commitment: zoom in on each case, zoom out across all cases, and then do the one thing that is hardest to automate — explain the variance.

The three-layer architecture is deceptively simple. Layer 1 writes an individual profile of each case, treating it as if no other case exists. Layer 2 looks across the group and describes what is typical. Layer 3 — the layer that earns the module its purpose — connects both: for every case that deviates from the group average, it names the mechanism behind the deviation and draws out its teaching implication.

The critical discipline is what the module calls the "Discussion Paragraph": a four-to-six sentence synthesis that could not have been written from any single case, and could not have been written from the average. It can only emerge from the pattern of deviations. This is where clinical insight lives — not in the outlier and not in the mean, but in the relationship between them.

The module explicitly refuses to accept summary dressed up as synthesis. Its quality test is brutal: if any synthesis paragraph could be removed and the surrounding analysis would still make sense, it is not synthesis. True synthesis is irreplaceable — it is the connective tissue that holds individual understanding and group knowledge together.

This is where VibeRounds begins. Not with a database, not with a pipeline, not with a research proposal. With the discipline of asking, for each divergent case: *why?*

## The First Expansion: A Disease-Specific Scaffold

Once the analytical discipline exists at the case level, the question becomes how to deploy it without drowning in it. The full 20-module VibeRounds pipeline is designed for depth on index cases. For disease-specific work at volume, depth must be traded for strategic coverage — without losing rigour.

Directory 2 demonstrates how this trade is made. It takes tuberculosis as the index disease — chosen deliberately, not arbitrarily. TB is the great mimicker: it presents identically to malignancy, autoimmune disease, sarcoidosis, lymphoma. Its management involves conditional weighting (a single finding like night sweats means something very different depending on geographic exposure, HIV status, and contact history). Its social determinants are structural, not peripheral. And its evidence frontier is active and contested — the BPaLM regimen for MDR-TB is not a settled question.

TB, in other words, breaks every shortcut. It cannot be managed by pattern-matching alone, cannot be stripped of its social context, and cannot be handed over to a guideline without the guideline being stress-tested.

The lightweight protocol serialises five targeted modules. It begins with phenotype sorting across a corpus of over 300,000 TB research items — not to read them all, but to identify which TB subtypes are locally relevant and which represent the highest-yield targets for deeper analysis. It moves immediately to acute action selection: not a differential list, but a single committed action, simulated forward to its failure points. Then guideline pruning, structured around a PICO question anchored to that acute decision. Then deep case report digestion against a real local patient. Then adversarial stress-testing — three arguments against the TB diagnosis, each demanding a clinical rebuttal, each designed to break the anchoring that a TB-endemic environment almost inevitably produces.

The sequence is not arbitrary. It mirrors how expert clinicians actually reason under time pressure: fast intuition first, then slow verification, then adversarial challenge. Reversing that sequence — starting with a literature review before committing to an action — does not reflect how medicine is actually practised in emergencies. It produces competent library-users, not competent clinicians.

What the lightweight model demonstrates is that analytical rigour does not require full-pipeline analysis on every case. It requires the right modules, in the right sequence, applied to the right disease at the right moment. Once that structure is validated on TB, it transfers. Every specialty has its great mimicker. Every acute setting has its time-critical emergencies. The five-step scaffold is the template; the disease is the variable.

## The Scale Jump: What Happens at 745,738 Cases

The move from a disease-specific directory to a global repository is not just a quantitative expansion. It is a qualitative transformation in what the system becomes capable of.

Running the VibeRounds protocol across all free full-text case reports on PubMed would produce something that does not currently exist: a structured, searchable, and teachable global clinical knowledge architecture. Not a database of abstracts. Not a collection of AI summaries. A repository where every case has been decomposed into its clinical modules, its paradoxes named, its variance explained, and its advocate debrief written.

Consider what this enables.

A clinician encountering a confusing presentation — the kind where the standard treatment could either save the patient or precipitate renal failure depending on a variable they have not yet measured — could retrieve hundreds of analogous cases where the same bidirectional logic was mapped and resolved. The ammonia-diarrhoea paradox, where lactulose is simultaneously the correct treatment and a potential precipitant of renal failure, is not rare in clinical medicine. Its resolution depends on specifics. Those specifics are now findable.

A junior doctor in a resource-limited setting, facing a recognised crisis pattern at three in the morning with no senior available, could access a recognition-primed decision engine built from the collective intuition of thousands of experts. Not a guideline. Not a differential. A committed first action, simulated forward, with its failure points already identified by the system.

A medical student coding a bedside narrative — translating "abdomen looks swollen" into "ascites with shifting dullness on percussion," documenting "procedure performed but result missing," noting that the patient fell again as part of a multi-year pattern — is performing an applied reasoning exercise at every shift. The coding is not administrative. It is educational. It builds the cognitive architecture that separates a clinician who treats episodes from one who treats patients.

The networked architecture connects this repository to a domain knowledge directory of PICO-structured guidelines, clinical studies, and Cochrane evidence. When the protocol identifies a clinical trigger — an ascitic tap performed without a documented PMN count — it surfaces the relevant PICO question from the guideline directory and flags the absence as a safety risk, automatically, at the point of analysis. The clinician does not have to remember to check. The system closes the gap between what guidelines recommend and what records show.

Evidence-based weights are added to causal network analysis. Instead of "high stool frequency may worsen renal function," the system can state: in cirrhotic patients with stool frequency exceeding 10 per day, studies show a statistically significant increase in hepatorenal syndrome incidence within 48 to 72 hours. The caregiver's question — "will stopping the diarrhoea hurt her brain?" — is answered not with expert opinion alone, but with population-level data drawn from matched studies, presented in language the caregiver can act on.

This is the formal definition of a Learning Health System: a continuous feedback loop between research evidence and bedside reality, where each case that enters the system makes the system better at understanding the next one.

## The Edge That Makes It Work: Narrative Over Case Report

The most important insight in the VibeRounds architecture is the least obvious one. The highest-value application of this system is not processing the 745,738 published case reports on PubMed. It is processing the raw, unedited narratives of non-expert clinicians working with real patients in real time.

This distinction requires explanation, because it runs against the assumption that published, peer-reviewed case reports are more reliable than informal clinical notes.

Published case reports are written retrospectively, with the diagnosis already known. They are structured to make the path to diagnosis appear more logical and direct than it actually was. The messy, uncertain middle — the period before the clinical picture converges, when individually unremarkable findings are accumulating without yet resolving into a pattern — is largely edited out. The author knows the ending. So they write toward it.

Raw clinical narratives preserve the middle. A junior doctor's narrative contains diagnostic uncertainty, partial observations, reasoning detours, and wrong turns that a published case report would never include. Individually, these look like noise. At sufficient volume, they are signal.

What large-scale narrative training can detect — and what cannot be found anywhere in the existing literature — is the pre-convergence phenotype: the subtle cluster of disconnected data points that consistently appears in clinical narratives before a unified diagnosis is reached. The combination of findings that individually appear unremarkable, that would not survive the editing process of a published report, but that reliably precede a specific diagnosis when they appear together in a certain sequence.

This is a category of clinical knowledge that does not currently exist in structured form. Not in textbooks. Not in guidelines. Not in case series. It is embedded in the raw, unfiltered observation records of thousands of clinicians who encountered the early phase of a disease before they knew what it was — and then wrote down what they saw, before they knew it mattered.

Current large language models are powerful at processing this kind of text. What they are not structured to do is preserve and interrogate the clinical reasoning as a process rather than as an outcome. They default to synthesis mode: merging all details into a single coherent narrative, smoothing the uncertainty, reaching a conclusion. This is precisely the behaviour that eliminates the most valuable information — the detours, the doubts, the deferred decisions that, at scale, reveal pre-convergence patterns.

The DIAR architecture addresses the technical dimension of this problem. In a long clinical narrative, a single atypical finding mentioned once — lymphadenopathy in a cirrhotic patient, a medication taken intermittently, a family history detail recorded as an aside — may carry disproportionate diagnostic significance. In a monolithic AI representation, that finding is statistically diluted by the surrounding routine text. Dimensionally Isolated Asymmetric Retrieval keeps separate vectors for distinct clinical dimensions, ensuring that rare findings remain independently queryable regardless of how much routine text surrounds them. The system can be asked "what is unusual about this narrative?" and return a meaningful answer — not a statistical average.

## The Constraint That Makes It Educational

There is a principle running through all four layers of this architecture that is not technical. It is pedagogical.

The VibeRounds system does not give answers.

This is the Socratic constraint, and it is a deliberate design choice with a specific rationale. When an AI provides a confident clinical answer, it creates a dependency response in the learner. The student checks whether their thinking matches the AI's conclusion, rather than building the reasoning independently. They are not learning to reason. They are learning to verify against the AI. The automation bias is not incidental — it is structurally produced by oracle-mode AI.

The alternative is to withhold the final synthesis and return instead the next question, the next gap, the next feature that has not yet been explained. For an MBBS student using this system at the bedside, the experience is not "the AI says this is probably TB." It is "the AI has identified that you have not yet explained the lymphadenopathy. What does that change?"

This distinction is the difference between a tool that produces passive users and a system that produces clinical thinkers.

At single-case scale, the Socratic constraint is a teaching principle. At the scale of a training dataset comprising hundreds of thousands of raw clinical narratives, it becomes something more: a structural guarantee that the effortful cognitive work — the reasoning steps that build clinical expertise — remains with the human at every stage.

## How It Can Actually Happen

The architecture described across these four documents is ambitious. Its realisation does not require a single institution, a single grant, or a single technology breakthrough. It requires a sequence.

The sequence begins with the analytical discipline — the commitment, embedded in Module 22, to explain variance rather than summarise averages. That discipline is teachable to any clinician with access to a capable language model. It costs nothing but structured prompting and intellectual rigour.

The second step is the disease-specific scaffold. One disease, five modules, serialised correctly. TB is the index. Once validated on TB, the scaffold transfers to every specialty with a great mimicker and a time-critical emergency — which is every specialty.

The third step is bedside text coding. A medical student paired with an AI, recording not just what happened but how the clinical picture unfolded — the sequence, the doubts, the deferred decisions. Not free-hand multimodal AI input, because multimodal processing cannot yet reliably distinguish between a procedure that was performed and one that was performed but whose results were never documented. Text forces explicitness. Text is auditable. Text is the medium in which both clinical observations and caregiver narratives already exist.

At sufficient volume — across enough students, enough hospitals, enough diseases — the pre-convergence phenotype library begins to emerge. Patterns that no individual clinician could detect because no individual clinician has seen enough early-phase presentations. Patterns that no published case series could detect because published case reports edit out exactly the material the system needs. Patterns that only become visible when the messy middle of thousands of clinical narratives is preserved, structured, and interrogated together.

The Discussion Paragraph from Module 22 describes what this produces: a finding that emerges from the pattern of deviations — something that neither any individual case nor the average alone could have revealed.

At global scale, that finding is a new category of clinical knowledge. The kind that cannot be extracted from textbooks, cannot be inferred from guidelines, and cannot be produced by any AI trained only on what was already known.

It can only be produced by a system that was structured, from the beginning, to ask the right question: not what does this case show, but *why does it differ from the others?*
