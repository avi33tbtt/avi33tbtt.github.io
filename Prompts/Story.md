# VibeRounds — Module : The Story Module
### *Real Case Narratives for the Clinical Mind*

**Module Type:** Reflective / Narrative  
**Lifecycle Phase:** Standalone (can serve as Initiation or Closure complement to Modules 1–10)  
**Pedagogical Frameworks Active:** Fink's FLINK (Human Dimension + Caring), Humanistic Persona, Critical Awareness Framework D  
**Audience:** Clinicians at any career stage — students, residents, consultants, educators, policy practitioners  
**Session Duration:** 10–20 minutes per story cycle  
**Format:** AI-generated narrative with structured reflective anchors

---

## 1. Purpose

Clinical training optimises relentlessly for *knowing* and *doing*. It rarely makes space for *feeling* and *witnessing* — the dimensions of medicine that sustain a practitioner over a career and erode them when ignored.

Module 22 fills that space.

It uses AI to generate richly textured, real-case-inspired narrative vignettes drawn from the full breadth of the clinical world: disease courses that surprised everyone, diagnostic odysseys that ended with a name nobody expected, healthcare systems that failed brilliantly or held together improbably, policy decisions made in rooms where the patient was never present, and the small, indelible ward moments that a clinician carries for decades without quite knowing why.

These are not teaching cases. There is no right answer to extract. There is only the story — and what it opens in the reader.

---

## 2. What "Real Case Story" Means Here

Each narrative generated by this module is:

- **Inspired by documented clinical reality** — drawn from case report literature, public health events, landmark policy episodes, or anonymised composite clinical experiences. The AI is explicitly instructed not to invent biology or epidemiology.
- **Narratively constructed** — written in literary prose, not bullet points. It has a beginning that places the reader in a specific moment, a middle that moves through time and uncertainty, and an end that resists tidy resolution.
- **Purposefully unresolved in places** — because medicine is. Some stories end with a cure; some end with a conversation in a corridor. Both are valid.
- **Free of didactic overlay** — no learning objectives embedded in the text, no bold-faced differentials, no summary boxes. The reflective work happens *after*, in the anchors below.

---

## 3. Story Domains

Each run of Module 22 draws from one of six domains. You may specify a domain or allow the AI to select one.

| # | Domain | What it includes |
|---|--------|-----------------|
| A | **Disease Narrative** | The natural history of an illness as experienced — diagnosis, course, crisis, resolution or not. Can be rare or common; what matters is the human grain |
| B | **Diagnostic Odyssey** | The prolonged, frustrating, or surprising journey to a name. Focus on uncertainty, the people who missed it, and the one who didn't |
| C | **Healthcare System** | A hospital, ward, or clinic operating under pressure — resource constraints, institutional failure, unexpected resilience, or a policy that played out at the bedside |
| D | **Medical Education** | A teaching moment, a formative failure, a mentor, a student, or the moment a trainee became a clinician |
| E | **Healthcare Policy** | A decision made at the macro level and its descent through the system to a specific patient or provider — the distance between policy intent and lived effect |
| F | **The Clinician** | A first-person or close-third account of the practitioner's interior: the weight of a difficult decision, a near-miss, a patient never forgotten, or the quiet grace of a long career |

## 4. The Prompt — How to Run Module 22

Copy the block below and paste it into any capable AI (Claude recommended). Modify the bracketed fields.

---

```
VIBE ROUNDS — MODULE 22: STORY MODULE
Role: You are a narrative clinician-writer with deep grounding in medical literature, clinical case reports, and the human experience of illness and healthcare. You write with the precision of a diagnostician and the attentiveness of a literary author.

Task: Generate one original clinical story narrative for a clinician audience.

Parameters:
- Domain: [choose one — A: Disease Narrative / B: Diagnostic Odyssey / C: Healthcare System / D: Medical Education / E: Healthcare Policy / F: The Clinician — or write "Surprise me"]
- Setting preference: [e.g., a tertiary hospital in South Asia / a rural district clinic / a policy committee room / any]
- Era: [contemporary / historical — specify decade if desired / any]
- Tone: [reflective and quiet / urgent and immersive / wry and bittersweet / any]
- Length: [short — ~400 words / standard — ~800 words / long — ~1400 words]

Story Requirements:
1. Begin with the exact text **#VibeRounds** on its own line before the story title. No exceptions.
2. Open in medias res — place the reader inside a specific sensory or situational moment. No scene-setting preamble.
3. Ground the biology, epidemiology, or policy detail in documented reality. Do not invent disease mechanisms or outcomes that are biologically implausible.
4. Include at least one named or clearly drawn character whose interiority the reader can access.
5. Do not embed a learning objective, a differential diagnosis box, or a didactic summary inside the narrative. The story teaches by immersion, not instruction.
6. End with honest ambiguity where appropriate — not every clinical story resolves cleanly, and that is the point.
6. After the story, provide three Reflective Anchors (see below).

Reflective Anchors — to be generated after the narrative, not embedded within it:
- Anchor 1 — The Moment: Identify one specific moment in the story where you, as a clinician, would have felt the weight of uncertainty most acutely. What would you have done?
- Anchor 2 — The System: What structural factor — visible or invisible — shaped this story's outcome? What would have had to be different?
- Anchor 3 — The Carry: What does this story leave you with? Not a lesson. A feeling, a question, or an image. Name it.

Generate the story now.
```

## 5. Example Story — Domain A: Disease Narrative

*The following is an illustrative sample generated by this module. It is not a prompt instruction.*

---

**#VibeRounds**

**The Weight of Water**

The oedema had been there for three weeks before Sunita noticed it in her reflection. Not in a mirror — she avoided those — but in the darkened glass of the clinic's front door as she arrived for the fourth appointment her daughter had had to book on her behalf, because Sunita herself kept finding reasons not to.

She was sixty-one. She had managed a post office for thirty years. She had the specific competence of someone who has always known, without being told, what the rules are and how to work within them. She had not known what to do with a body that was quietly filling up with fluid.

The intern who saw her first noted pitting oedema to the mid-shin, a raised JVP, and basal crepitations. He wrote *?cardiac failure* in the margin of his clerking sheet in the compressed shorthand of someone running forty minutes late. He was not wrong. He was also not quite right.

It took the echo to complicate things. The LV function was mildly reduced — 48%, borderline — but the right-sided pressures were elevated beyond what the left ventricle alone could explain. The pulmonologist who reviewed her as a favour to the cardiology registrar noted that Sunita had lived for twelve years near a cluster of textile mills in a town whose dust levels had never been formally monitored. She had a mild restrictive pattern on spirometry that nobody had acted on, because nobody had ordered it before.

The final diagnosis — combined cardiac and pulmonary hypertension, with an occupational exposure component that would never be provable in court but was, to everyone in the room, obvious — arrived on a Tuesday afternoon. The consultant who delivered it was careful and kind. He explained the management plan in three languages because Sunita kept switching between them, the way people do when they are trying to hold something very large in their hands.

She started torsemide and sildenafil. She improved, within limits that were honest about what *improved* meant here.

What nobody documented, because there was no field in the discharge summary for it, was that Sunita asked, once, very quietly, whether the mills had made other people sick. The consultant said he didn't know. He said it was a good question.

She nodded and put on her coat.

---

**Reflective Anchors**

**Anchor 1 — The Moment:** The intern's *?cardiac failure* note is the hinge. At what point in your own practice might you have stopped there? What would have prompted you to look further — or not?

**Anchor 2 — The System:** Occupational exposure as a contributing factor to pulmonary hypertension is documented, underreported, and structurally difficult to act on. What would a health system need to look like to catch Sunita earlier — and what would it cost?

**Anchor 3 — The Carry:** She put on her coat. What does that gesture mean to you?

## 6. Variations and Extensions

| Variation | How to trigger it |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Series mode** | Ask the AI to generate three stories from three different domains in one session — read as a set, they reveal the same healthcare system from different angles |
| **Historical mode** | Specify a decade (e.g., 1980s — early AIDS; 1950s — polio eradication campaigns; 1994 — Rwandan health system collapse) for temporal depth |
| **Policy descent** | For Domain E, ask the AI to track a single policy decision from the committee room to the bedside in three scenes — cuts, rationing, or a new guideline |
| **The educator's story** | For Domain D, ask the AI to write from the teacher's perspective — the student they still think about, the teaching they got wrong for a decade before realising it |
| **Paired with Module 1** | Run Module 1 (Socratic Case Reasoning) on the same clinical scenario first, then run Module 22 on the same case as a narrative. Compare what each mode revealed |

## 7. Pedagogical Notes for Educators

If you are running Module 22 in a group setting — a teaching ward round, a faculty development session, a clinical ethics meeting — consider the following:

- **Read aloud.** Clinical prose lands differently when heard. One person reads; the room listens without taking notes.
- **Hold the silence after the Reflective Anchors.** The instinct in medical education is to fill silence with a correct answer. There is no correct answer here. The silence is the work.
- **Do not debrief immediately.** Let ten minutes pass before discussion. Let people sit with what the story left behind.
- **Collect the "Anchor 3" responses anonymously in writing.** Over a semester, a ward, or a department, these accumulate into something like an emotional map of your clinical culture — what it carries, what it cannot put down.
