## Layer 1 — Individual Profiles (Zoom In)

---

**Case C1 — 76F, Intractable Generalised Pain**

A 76-year-old Caucasian woman presented with reticulated reddish-brown patches in a cape-like distribution across her back. She had been using an electrical heating pad for pain with no identifiable cause, progressing to lying on it for at least six consecutive hours daily as she became bedridden. Exposure duration was 12 months. The lesions were asymptomatic and first noticed by her husband. No burning or discomfort was reported. At follow-up 18 months after cessation, lesions had fully resolved, confirming the diagnosis clinically without biopsy. The distribution — diffuse dorsal, cape-like — directly mapped the surface on which she lay. The case represents EAI as the dermatological footprint of functional decline: the longer she lay immobile, the greater the infrared exposure, and the more extensive the lesion.

*The detail most likely to matter later is:* the heat exposure was passive, not active — she was lying on the pad because she could not get up, which means the exposure duration escalated not by choice but by incapacity.

---

**Case C2 — 52F, Uterine Fibroids, Concurrent Brain Abscess**

A 52-year-old Caucasian woman was seen as an inpatient consult following craniotomy for a brain abscess. Her mental status was impaired and she could not give a history. EAI was diagnosed on morphology — lace-like hyperpigmented patches on the lower abdomen and upper thighs — before any causative history was available. Several days later her husband confirmed she had been using a heating pad for more than eight hours daily for three years for fibroid-related abdominal pain. This case is unusual in two respects: the diagnosis was made in the absence of history, purely on pattern recognition, and the exposure duration (three years, >8 h/day) was the most extreme in the series. The gynaecological pain — not the neurological admission — was the driver of the behaviour, making the craniotomy a coincidental backdrop.

*The detail most likely to matter later is:* the history was obtained from a proxy, meaning the clinician had to act on morphological reasoning alone — this case tests whether the diagnosis can be made without a patient history, and whether the clinical pattern is sufficiently distinctive to stand alone.

---

**Case C3 — 50F, Resolved Lower Back Pain**

A 50-year-old Caucasian woman attended for a routine full body skin check. Reticulated hyperpigmented patches on the lower back were noted incidentally. The patient was unaware of the lesions. She had used a heating pad weekly for lower back pain for at least six months, but the pain had resolved several months before her visit, and she had spontaneously stopped using the pad. No symptoms accompanied the lesions. This is the most clinically benign presentation in the series: lowest frequency of use (weekly), shortest exposure window (six months minimum), spontaneous pain resolution before diagnosis, and patient unaware of any skin change. The condition was caught entirely by chance during a routine check.

*The detail most likely to matter later is:* the exposure had already ceased, and the lesion was residual — meaning this case demonstrates the persistence of EAI morphology beyond the active heat-exposure window, raising the question of how long lesions linger after the cause is removed.

---

**Case C4 — 28M, Type 1 Diabetes, Gastroparesis**

A 28-year-old man with Type 1 diabetes and gastroparesis presented with vomiting, abdominal pain, and hyperglycaemia. Examination revealed erythematous, reticulated, non-blanchable macular pigmentation on the abdomen — characteristic of EAI. He had been applying hot water bottles for chronic abdominal pain from gastroparesis. During admission, the lesion began to fade, providing an in-vivo natural experiment: removal of the heat source (hospitalisation eliminated access to the hot water bottle) produced visible early resolution. He was the only male in the series and the youngest patient. Gastroparesis-driven pain is characteristically severe and difficult to control pharmacologically, making non-pharmacological heat application an understandable coping strategy in this condition.

*The detail most likely to matter later is:* he was young and male, two features atypical of EAI demography — the pain severity of gastroparesis appears to override the usual age-sex profile of the condition.

---

**Case C5 — 33F, Deep Endometriosis (Confirmed Surgically)**

A 33-year-old nulliparous woman presented with dysmenorrhea since age 18, progressive worsening, and chronic pelvic pain for two years, refractory to NSAIDs. She had adopted continuous heating pad use (4–5 h daily, including during sleep) to manage pain. EAI affecting the lower abdomen and upper thighs was present for 10 months. Biopsy confirmed EAI (thin epidermis, perivascular infiltrate, fragmented elastic fibres on Van-Gieson staining). Imaging and surgical staging revealed deep endometriosis (ENZIAN A2B2C2FA): bilateral ovarian endometriomas, adenomyosis, obliterated pouch of Douglas, rectal involvement. Laparoscopic excision resolved pain; heat-seeking behaviour ceased; pigmentation ameliorated with topical hydroquinone and retinoids. This is the only case in which EAI served as a cutaneous clue to an undiagnosed internal condition — the skin finding was the visible marker of a gynaecological diagnosis not yet made.

*The detail most likely to matter later is:* the endometriosis was not diagnosed at the time the EAI developed — the skin lesion preceded the gynaecological diagnosis, making EAI a potential cutaneous sentinel for underlying pelvic pathology in this context.

## Layer 2 — Group Analysis (Zoom Out)

---

### (a) Shared features (present in ≥ 2/3 of cases)

Present in 4–5 of 5 cases:

- **Asymptomatic lesion** — the EAI itself caused no pain or discomfort in any case (C1, C2, C3, C4, C5)
- **Reticulated hyperpigmented morphology** — lace-like or net-like brown/reddish-brown patches in all cases
- **Distribution maps precisely to heat source placement** — back (C1), lower abdomen/thighs (C2, C5), lower back (C3), abdomen (C4)
- **Female sex** — 4 of 5 patients female (C1, C2, C3, C5); C4 is the sole male
- **Underlying chronic pain as the direct driver of heat use** — pain was the reason in every case; EAI was the incidental dermatological consequence
- **Benign resolution following cessation of heat source** — confirmed resolution or documented improvement in all cases where follow-up was available (C1, C4, C5)

---

### (b) Central tendency — the "typical" case

The central tendency across this case set is a middle-aged woman with chronic pain from an identifiable anatomical cause (gynaecological, musculoskeletal, or visceral) who uses a heating pad or hot water bottle daily for months to years for pain relief. The lesion is asymptomatic, reticulated, and hyperpigmented, and it is typically noted incidentally — either by the patient's partner, during a routine examination, or when another clinician examines the patient for an unrelated reason. The heat source maps anatomically to the lesion site. Cessation of heat exposure is the primary treatment, and lesions generally resolve or significantly improve within months to years. The condition is primarily clinical in diagnosis; biopsy is reserved for atypical or non-resolving lesions.

---

### (c) Range and clusters

**By exposure duration:** from 6 months weekly (C3) to 3 years daily (C2), with a visible cluster of 6–12 months of daily or near-daily use (C1, C4, C5).

**By age:** 28 (C4) → 33 (C5) → 50 (C3) → 52 (C2) → 76 (C1). A visible younger cluster (C4, C5: 28–33, both with visceral pain driving extreme heat use) and an older cluster (C1, C2, C3: 50–76, with more heterogeneous causes).

**By diagnostic circumstance:** Two subclusters emerge:
- *Incidental/proxy diagnosis* — EAI noted by someone other than the patient, with the patient either unaware (C3) or unable to give history (C2), or noticed by a partner (C1)
- *Clinically pursued diagnosis* — EAI worked up because of the severity of the underlying disease and because clinicians were alert to it (C4, C5)

**By underlying cause:** Musculoskeletal/idiopathic (C1, C3), gynaecological (C2, C5), visceral/metabolic (C4).

---

### (d) Outlier flag

**C5** (33F, deep endometriosis) stands structurally apart because EAI preceded the diagnosis of the underlying condition. In all other cases, the underlying diagnosis was known before or at the time EAI was identified. In C5, the skin finding was the alert that a serious pelvic condition had not yet been formally established — inverting the usual diagnostic sequence and elevating EAI from a passive skin marker to an active clinical signal.

## Layer 3 — Synthesis (Connect and Explain the Variance)

---

**C1 — 76F, intractable pain (passive exposure escalation)**

Against the group central tendency of a patient who actively and deliberately applies heat for pain, C1 deviates in a critical respect: her exposure escalation was passive, driven by functional incapacity rather than behavioural choice. She did not choose to lie on the heating pad for six hours — she was unable to leave it. The mechanism driving her deviation from the group is therefore *functional decline acting as a force multiplier on exposure duration*: the pain that caused the EAI was the same pain that prevented the behaviour modification that would have limited the EAI. The Layer 1 prediction — that passivity of exposure would matter — was correct. Discussion implication: when EAI appears in an elderly or bedridden patient, the distribution and severity of the lesion may function as a surrogate marker of the degree of functional limitation, not merely the patient's pain management behaviour.

---

**C2 — 52F, uterine fibroids (diagnosis without history)**

C2 aligns with the group central tendency on every clinical variable — gynaecological pain, daily prolonged heating pad use, lower abdominal distribution — but deviates structurally on the diagnostic dimension. The diagnosis was made without a patient history, purely by pattern recognition, making this the only case in the series where the clinical gestalt of EAI had to stand entirely alone. The mechanism of this deviation is coincidental neurological compromise (brain abscess, post-craniotomy delirium) that removed the patient's ability to provide a history at the moment of consultation. The Layer 1 prediction — that proxy-obtained history would be the decisive feature — was confirmed. The actual exposure duration (3 years, >8 h/day) proved even more extreme than other cases once obtained. Discussion implication: EAI has sufficient morphological specificity to be diagnosed without a patient history, which has practical importance in cognitively impaired or incapacitated patients — the skin pattern itself justifies a high index of suspicion and a targeted history obtained from carers.

---

**C3 — 50F, resolved pain (residual lesion after exposure cessation)**

C3 sits below the group central tendency in every measure of exposure severity: shortest duration (6 months), lowest frequency (weekly, not daily), and — uniquely — the heat source had already been discontinued before the diagnosis was made. The lesion is a residual, not an active, finding. The mechanism driving C3's deviation from the group is *spontaneous pain resolution eliminating the behaviour before clinical recognition occurred*, producing a temporal dissociation between cause, effect, and diagnosis. The Layer 1 prediction — that lesion persistence beyond active exposure would be the telling feature — was confirmed. Discussion implication: EAI may remain visible for months after the causative behaviour has ceased, meaning clinicians who see a characteristic reticulated pattern should consider resolved as well as ongoing heat exposure; the absence of current behaviour does not exclude the diagnosis.

---

**C4 — 28M, diabetic gastroparesis (demographic outlier, pain-severity driver)**

C4 is the single male in the series and the youngest patient by a margin of five years, placing him structurally outside the group central tendency of middle-aged women. The mechanism driving both demographic deviations is *disease severity overriding demographic predictors of EAI*: gastroparesis is among the most refractory pain syndromes in young patients, and the severity of visceral pain appears to generate heat-seeking behaviour irrespective of the usual age and sex distribution of the condition. The Layer 1 prediction — that pain severity would override demography — was correct, but the additional insight is that the in-hospital fading of the lesion provided a natural experiment confirming causal attribution. Discussion implication: the male sex and young age of a patient with EAI should not reduce clinical suspicion — it should instead prompt a direct enquiry into the severity of the underlying pain, which in C4's case was a signal of poorly controlled gastroparesis, a condition with significant management implications.

---

**C5 — 33F, deep endometriosis (inverted diagnostic sequence)**

C5 conforms to the group central tendency in morphology, distribution, behaviour, and trigger mechanism. Its structural deviation is temporal and diagnostic: EAI preceded the confirmed diagnosis of deep endometriosis by months. In every other case, the underlying condition was known; the EAI was incidental. In C5, the EAI was the visible alarm that drew clinical attention to a disorder the patient herself had normalised over 15 years of dysmenorrhea. The mechanism is *chronic pain normalisation combined with inadequate prior workup*, which allowed severe pelvic disease to progress untreated while the patient managed symptoms with heat — until the heat left its mark on the skin. The Layer 1 prediction — that EAI would precede gynaecological diagnosis — was the core finding. Discussion implication: in a reproductive-age woman with EAI in a pelvic distribution and a history of dysmenorrhea or chronic pelvic pain, EAI should prompt systematic gynaecological evaluation rather than being treated as a standalone dermatological finding, because the skin lesion may be the first externally visible sign of significant internal pathology.

---

### Discussion Paragraph

The most important finding that emerges from the pattern of deviations across this case set is not the clinical heterogeneity of EAI's underlying causes — that is expected. It is the *diagnostic function* that EAI performs in each case, which varies systematically and reveals something the average alone cannot. In the typical EAI case, the skin finding is passive: it trails behind a known diagnosis and a known behaviour. But across these five cases, EAI occupies at least three distinct diagnostic roles: a marker of functional incapacity (C1), a diagnostic anchor when history is unavailable (C2), and a temporal sentinel that precedes diagnosis of the underlying condition (C5). The cases that deviate most sharply from the group central tendency — C4 by demography, C5 by diagnostic timing — share a common mechanism: the severity of the underlying condition drove pain management behaviour to an extreme that left an externally visible mark, and that mark arrived before the condition itself was formally established. The clinical teaching that emerges is not the morphology of EAI, which is well described, but its *diagnostic timing*: a systematic clinical response to EAI should include not only cessation of the heat source but a structured enquiry into whether the pain driving the behaviour has itself been adequately investigated — because in the most important case in this series, the skin was the first available clue that it had not.

## Step 22.5 — Variance Interrogation

**C1 (passive exposure):** Alternative explanation — the cape-like distribution could be attributed to a specific heating pad model or application technique rather than to bedridden status. Distinguishing evidence: whether the patient was ambulatory during the exposure period. Missing variable: functional status ratings or mobility documentation over the 12-month exposure period.

**C4 (young male, gastroparesis):** Alternative explanation — the male sex might reflect reporting bias or the specific high-pain-severity niche of gastroparesis rather than a true demographic exception to EAI epidemiology. Distinguishing evidence: population-level male:female EAI ratios stratified by underlying diagnosis. Missing variable: pain severity scoring (e.g. VAS) at the time of heat use.

**C5 (inverted diagnostic sequence):** Alternative explanation — the endometriosis may have been clinically suspected earlier but not formally investigated, making the delay diagnostic rather than causal. Distinguishing evidence: prior consultation records and any previous pelvic ultrasound or gynaecology referral. Missing variable: timeline of prior medical contacts for dysmenorrhea before the EAI presentation.
