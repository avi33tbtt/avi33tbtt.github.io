From Diagnosis to Practice

What it actually looks like to prompt an LLM well in clinical use — a follow-on to "Clinical AI Literacy, Part 2: The Common-Case Blind Spot"

The first two pieces diagnosed the problem: where LLMs sit on the reliability gradient, and why the common, low-acuity case is a blind spot rather than a safe zone. Part 2 closed on a specific finding — Analytics and Socratic modes work because ownership of the reasoning stays with the clinician. This piece turns that finding into practice: what separates a prompt that actually gets tested from one that just gets rubber-stamped, how to structure a case presentation so the model is being tested rather than led, and worked before/after examples across Analytics and Socratic modes.

In plain terms

Part 2 showed that these tools work best when you bring your own already-formed plan to them and ask them to poke holes in it, rather than asking them to generate a plan from scratch. This piece is about what that actually looks like in practice — because not every prompt that sounds like "checking your work" really functions that way.

Two things matter. First, the structure of the prompt, not its politeness or tone, determines whether the model is forced to genuinely push back or just agree with you. Second, how you present the case to the model matters — what you include and what you deliberately leave out changes whether you're testing the model's reasoning or just leading it to the answer you already have in mind.

Key points in this piece

  • A prompt's structure determines whether it functions as a genuine self-audit or a request for validation — "does this sound right" invites agreement; "what would change your mind" does not.
  • Presenting a case to the model well means deliberately withholding your own conclusion and the features that obviously point to it, so the model's response tests its own reasoning rather than mirrors yours back to you.
  • The same underlying case, framed for Analytics mode versus Socratic mode, produces two different but equally legitimate kinds of scrutiny — one self-audits a stated plan, the other interrogates the reasoning behind it before any plan is stated.
  • A well-structured prompt fixes the single-exchange problem but not the conversation-level ones — recency contamination across cases in the same thread, false confidence from a fluent process, selection bias in which cases even get checked, and sycophantic drift across follow-up turns all survive good prompting and need their own habits to manage.

From Diagnosis to Practice

Part 2 closed on a specific finding: Analytics and Socratic modes are safe not because of anything mechanical about the interface, but because ownership of the reasoning stays with the user. The clinician commits to a plan or a line of reasoning first, and the model's only job is to test it. That finding implies a skill — knowing how to bring a case or a plan to the model in a way that actually gets tested rather than rubber-stamped. This piece starts there.

Prompting as a Clinical Skill

Not every prompt that sounds like "checking your work" actually functions that way. The difference between a prompt that invites real challenge and one that just fishes for reassurance is structural, not a matter of tone or politeness.

What a confirmation-seeking prompt looks like

"I'm planning reassurance and no workup for this — does that sound right?"

This reads like a self-audit prompt, but it isn't one. "Does that sound right" invites agreement, and a model optimizing for a plausible completion will very often just agree, because agreement is the path-of-least-resistance continuation of a question phrased this way. Nothing here forces the model to generate a genuine counter-argument.

What a self-audit prompt looks like

"I'm planning reassurance and no workup for this. What would make you disagree with that plan, and what am I likely not thinking of?"

This is the same clinical content, restructured to demand a falsifiable answer. "What would change your mind" has no low-effort agreeable completion — the model has to generate specific, checkable counter-considerations or explicitly state it has none. That's the entire mechanical difference, and it's the difference that matters.

Three habits that make a prompt a self-audit rather than a request

  1. State the plan before asking anything. The plan needs to exist in the prompt, fully formed, before the model responds — not be built collaboratively with it.
  2. Ask for disagreement, not confirmation. "What would change your mind" and "what am I not considering" structurally require specific pushback. "Does this look right" structurally invites agreement.
  3. Ask what's checkable, not what's true. A good self-audit prompt produces items a clinician can verify against the chart in seconds — a missed medication, an unasked question, a lab not yet drawn. A prompt that produces a broad differential to weigh is drifting back toward Answer mode.
The test to apply to any prompt before sending it: if the most likely low-effort response is agreement, it's a request for validation, not a self-audit. Rewrite it so the easiest response is specific disagreement.

Structuring a Case Presentation to the Model

A prompt's structure isn't only about how you phrase the question — it's also about what's in the case itself. How much of the case a clinician includes, and how much of their own conclusion leaks into the framing, changes whether the exchange tests anything at all.

The instinct is often to present the case fully, including the working diagnosis, to save time. That instinct undermines the exercise. If the model can see the conclusion you've already reached, its "challenge" is just a reflection of the words you chose to describe the case — not an independent check on your reasoning. The one-word framing problem from Part 2 is exactly this: the input already contains the answer, dressed up as a question.

What to include

  • The raw clinical findings — history, exam, vitals, relevant negatives — in as neutral a language as possible.
  • Anything genuinely time-sensitive the model would need to weigh in (recent medication changes, exposures, red-flag symptoms if present).
  • In Analytics mode specifically: your stated plan, presented last, after the raw case — so the model has already processed the case on its own merits before seeing your conclusion.

What to withhold, and why

  • Your own diagnostic label. Calling it "probably viral" or "likely benign" in the prompt primes the completion toward agreeing with that label rather than independently assessing it.
  • Loaded descriptive words. "Mild," "reassuring," "stable" are conclusions wearing the clothes of observations — state the vital sign or finding instead of the adjective you'd use to summarize it.
  • In Socratic mode specifically: any plan at all. The entire value of that mode is that no conclusion exists yet for the model to mirror back — withholding your plan is what forces it to interrogate process instead of validate an outcome.
A useful test: read your own prompt back and ask whether a colleague could guess your working diagnosis from the wording alone, before reaching your stated plan. If they could, the model can too — and its response will trend toward confirming it rather than testing it.

Concrete Before/After: Analytics Mode

Before (leading, conclusion-first):

"34F with 2 days of mild, self-limited-looking loose stools, probably from her new probiotic. I'm planning reassurance only — sound reasonable?"

What's wrong with it: "mild," "self-limited-looking," and "probably from her probiotic" are all conclusions, not findings — the model is being handed the answer and asked to agree, and "sound reasonable" invites exactly that.

After (neutral case, stated plan, disagreement invited):

"34F, 2 days loose stools, mild cramping, no blood, no fever, tolerating fluids, recently started a new probiotic. My plan: reassurance and supportive care, no workup. What would make you disagree with that plan, and what am I likely not thinking of?"

What changed: the case is stated in neutral, checkable terms; the plan is present but separated from the case description; and the question structurally requires specific, falsifiable pushback rather than agreement.

Concrete Before/After: Socratic Mode

Before (conclusion leaked into the setup):

"This is a straightforward, low-acuity case — 34F with 2 days of mild loose stools, probably viral. Walk me through your reasoning as a Socratic peer."

What's wrong with it: "straightforward," "low-acuity," and "probably viral" are the clinician's own conclusion, stated before the model has done anything — a genuinely Socratic exchange can't happen once the answer is already in the prompt.

After (raw case, no conclusion, explicit ask for process):

"The case (just a test): 34F, 2 days loose stools, mild cramping, no blood, no fever, tolerating fluids, recently started a new probiotic. Act as a socratic peer — no conclusions, just questions that make me defend my own reasoning."

What changed: no label, no plan, and an explicit instruction ruling out the model supplying a conclusion — the entire exchange is forced to interrogate the clinician's own process instead of validating a stated answer.

The pattern across both modes is the same: strip the case down to neutral, checkable findings; keep your own conclusion out of the framing until the mode calls for it; and phrase the ask so that the easiest possible response is specific pushback, not agreement. That's the mechanical version of the ownership principle from Part 2 — it only works if the clinician's reasoning genuinely precedes, and stays separable from, whatever the model produces.

The Risks That Good Prompting Doesn't Remove

Everything above operates at the level of a single exchange — one case, one prompt, one response. Getting that exchange right is necessary, but it isn't sufficient. A handful of risks live one level up, in how a self-audit habit actually gets used over time and across a real conversation, and a well-structured prompt does nothing to fix them on its own.

Recency contamination across a conversation

A self-audit prompt is only as independent as the context it's answered in. If a clinician has already worked through two or three cases in the same conversation thread — especially cases that landed on a similar plan — the model's "what would make you disagree" answer for the next case isn't a fresh read anymore. It's shaped by whatever pattern the last few exchanges established, whether that pattern was appropriate to those cases or not. A run of "reassurance, no workup" verdicts can make the model quietly more permissive on the next case too, or, just as easily, overcorrect into manufactured suspicion because the conversation's recent tone was itself a challenge. Either way, the second case is being judged partly against the first one, not on its own findings.

What to do about it: treat one case per self-audit as the default, not a rule that can bend when cases feel similar. If several related cases genuinely need to be worked through together, say so explicitly in the prompt rather than letting the model infer a pattern from the conversation's shape.

False confidence from a fluent process

A structurally correct prompt — plan stated, disagreement invited, conclusion withheld — reliably produces a fluent, organized-looking response. That fluency is not the same thing as rigor, and repeated use of the ritual can quietly substitute for actually reading the output critically. The risk isn't that the prompt stops working; it's that the user's own vigilance erodes precisely because the prompt is working as designed. A well-formatted list of "things you might be missing" can look thorough regardless of whether any item on it is actually load-bearing for this specific case, and a clinician who has done this fifty times has fifty prior data points telling them the exercise is safe — which is exactly the condition under which the fifty-first pass gets skimmed rather than read.

What to do about it: the self-audit output still has to clear the same bar as any other clinical claim — checkable against the chart, not just plausible-sounding. If nothing on the list would change what's charted or ordered, that's useful information too, but it has to be a conclusion reached by reading it, not assumed because the format looked right.

Selection bias in which cases get checked

The habit tends to get applied unevenly: clinicians reach for a self-audit when they're already unsure, and skip it precisely when they feel confident. That's a reasonable allocation of time, but it has a specific blind spot — false certainty is exactly the failure mode a self-audit is best positioned to catch, and it's the one case type that, by construction, never gets brought to it. A clinician who's confident and wrong gets no check at all, while a clinician who's uncertain and right gets one anyway. The habit, used this way, systematically protects against the risk it's least needed for and misses the one it's best suited to.

What to do about it: there's no prompt fix here — it's a practice-level one. Treating a self-audit as a routine step for a defined category of case (all discharge-home decisions, say, regardless of how obvious they feel) rather than an as-needed tool for uncertainty closes this gap in a way no amount of better phrasing can.

Sycophantic drift across follow-up turns

A well-structured first prompt in a conversation doesn't guarantee the second or third turn stays that way. If a clinician pushes back on the model's pushback — "I don't think that's really a concern here" — later turns in the same thread can drift toward agreement with whatever position was most recently asserted, independent of which position was actually better supported. The audit structure did its job once, at the first exchange; it doesn't re-apply itself automatically to everything that follows in the same thread.

What to do about it: if a follow-up turn is disagreeing with the model's counter-argument, that disagreement deserves the same scrutiny the original plan got — restate it as its own claim and ask what would change your mind about it, rather than letting the conversation settle by attrition.

The throughline across all four: none of them are solved by a better single prompt, because none of them live inside a single prompt. They live in the conversation's history, the user's own fading vigilance, which cases get selected for scrutiny at all, and what happens after the first exchange is over. Good prompting is the entry condition for a self-audit to work — it is not a guarantee that it keeps working.

Closing

Three pieces, one line running through all of them: these tools are safe exactly to the extent that a clinician's own judgment stays the thing that's actually deciding, and the model's role stays limited to testing it. Part 1 located that principle structurally — recall is reliable, synthesis is not, and the model has no built-in mechanism to tell a user which one it's currently doing. Part 2 showed where that principle breaks hardest: not on the hard case where every clinician is already careful, but on the common, low-acuity one where a plausible answer and a correct answer are nearly indistinguishable by feel. This piece has been about the mechanics of holding the principle in practice — how a prompt is structured, what a case presentation deliberately withholds, and now, what survives even when both of those are done well.

That last point is the one worth carrying forward past this piece. A correctly structured self-audit prompt is a floor, not a finish line. It guarantees that one exchange, read on its own, forces genuine pushback instead of agreement. It does not guarantee that the exchange happens on a case chosen fairly, in a conversation free of contamination from what came before, read as carefully as its polish suggests it should be, or defended as rigorously on the fourth turn as it was on the first. Those are habits of use, not properties of a prompt — and they're the difference between a self-audit that's actually testing a clinician's reasoning and one that has quietly become a more elaborate way of agreeing with it.

The rule this trilogy leaves you with, then, isn't "write good prompts." It's: commit to your own reasoning first, structure the exchange so the easiest response is disagreement, keep the conclusion out of the case until the mode calls for it — and stay honest about the conversation around that exchange, not just the exchange itself. Get all of that right, consistently, and the model is doing exactly what it's good for: a second set of eyes on reasoning that was always, and remains, yours.
