The Case

Fed into the Navigator exactly once. Each cycle below re-reads this same case fresh, then hands its output to the next cycle — one resolution layer at a time, per the protocol's own depth-control rule.

5-year-old girl, diagnosed with Type 1 Diabetes 4 months ago. On a basal-bolus regimen (long-acting insulin at bedtime, rapid-acting with meals), managed at home by parents; attends kindergarten 5 days/week. Reasonable glycemic control on paper (last HbA1c mildly elevated), but the parents report two hypoglycemic episodes in the last month — one at school (managed by staff with juice, no glucagon used), one overnight (child was found sweaty and mildly confused, glucose 48 mg/dL). Mother does most of the day-to-day dosing; father travels for work. The school has a written care plan but the teacher has expressed discomfort about being responsible for insulin dosing decisions at lunch. Family recently moved cities; new pediatric endocrinology follow-up is booked but 6 weeks away.

Minimal High-Yield Interventions

  • Contact the current care team now (not at the 6-week mark) specifically about the overnight low and CGM/monitoring options — this is the fastest-moving phase boundary.
  • Arrange hands-on glucagon training for the specific school staff present at lunch, not just a document handoff.
  • Have a direct, blame-free conversation with the teacher about their discomfort, with an explicit "escalate, don't guess" rule.
  • Write a simple backup dosing reference for the father or another caregiver, so the household isn't a single point of failure.

Zoom-In — Bifurcation Tactics: Overnight Detection

[ZOOM IN: BIFURCATION TACTICS]

Dropping the system map. Focusing on one node only: how the family closes the overnight-detection gap before it repeats.

Expected outcome: a second overnight low is caught early enough — by monitoring, not by luck — that it never reaches the confusion/sweating stage seen last time.

Red flag: the family (understandably) treats the first event as a one-off and simply adjusts the bedtime dose slightly, without changing how overnight glucose is actually observed — leaving the detection gap itself untouched. A subtler trap: assuming a new city's pharmacy or insurance logistics for a CGM will "sort itself out" over six weeks, quietly turning an urgent fix into a slow one.

Safety preparation (circuit breaker):

  • Raise the overnight low with the current prescribing clinician this week — by phone or message if the in-person visit is six weeks out — rather than waiting for the scheduled appointment.
  • In the interim, agree on a concrete overnight-check routine (a specific time, a specific person) rather than leaving it implicit.
  • Treat CGM access as a logistics problem to start now, not a topic for the eventual specialist visit — insurance and pharmacy steps often take longer than the clinical decision itself.

Why this is the real leverage point: the insulin dosing math itself is largely guideline-driven and already being managed correctly enough to keep HbA1c near target. The genuine discretionary leverage sitting with this family right now is entirely in how fast the detection gap closes — because that is the one piece guidelines can't automate; it depends on this specific family's access, city, and support system.

What this is, and isn't

This is a worked application of one blog's proposed cognitive framework, run across four simulated agent passes, for a single illustrative case. It is not a clinical protocol, not a substitute for the child's actual pediatric endocrinology team, and not a source of dosing guidance — no dose, ratio, or medication instruction should be taken from it. As the source piece itself argues, an AI-generated map like this one is a draft with no bedside eyes, useful only as a sparring partner for practicing which details actually matter — never as an answer key.
