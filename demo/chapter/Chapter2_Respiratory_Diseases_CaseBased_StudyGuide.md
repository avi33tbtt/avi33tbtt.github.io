# Chapter 2: Respiratory Diseases — Case-Based Study Guide
### Pneumonia · COPD · Asthma · TB · Pleural Effusion · Lung Carcinoma · PE · ILD

This guide uses 8 clinical cases to cover every topic in Chapter 2. Each case has a vignette, a set of clinical thinking points, clinical pearls, and a Socratic Q&A block to test reasoning.

---

## Case 1 — Community-Acquired Pneumonia
**Covers:** Pneumonia (typical/atypical, CAP vs HAP) + Management of pneumonia

### Vignette
A 62-year-old smoker presents with 3 days of fever, productive cough, and right-sided pleuritic chest pain. RR 24/min, SpO₂ 91% on room air, HR 102, BP 118/76, Temp 38.9°C, no confusion. Chest X-ray shows right lower lobe consolidation.

### Thinking Points
1. CAP is defined as pneumonia acquired outside hospital or within 48 hours of admission; HAP is pneumonia developing ≥48 hours after admission and implies a different (often resistant) organism profile.
2. Severity is risk-stratified with **CURB-65**: Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, RR ≥30, BP (SBP <90 or DBP ≤60), Age ≥65. Each criterion = 1 point.
3. This patient scores low on CURB-65 (no confusion, RR <30, BP normal, age <65) — but hypoxia (SpO₂ 91%) is an independent reason for admission regardless of the score.
4. Lobar consolidation with high fever and rigors points toward typical bacterial organisms (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*); a dry cough with extrapulmonary features (headache, myalgia, GI symptoms) suggests atypical organisms (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila*, *Legionella*), which often show patchy/interstitial infiltrates instead.
5. Empirical antibiotics scale with severity: low severity → amoxicillin; moderate → amoxicillin-clavulanate plus a macrolide; severe → IV beta-lactam plus macrolide, with broader cover (anti-pseudomonal) considered in HAP.
6. Supportive care includes oxygen titrated to target saturation, IV fluids, and analgesia for pleuritic pain (which can otherwise limit breathing and predispose to atelectasis).
7. Failure to improve by 48–72 hours should prompt reassessment for empyema, resistant organism, wrong diagnosis (e.g., PE, malignancy), or immunocompromise.

### Clinical Pearls
- CURB-65 guides *site of care*, not the entirety of clinical judgement — hypoxia, comorbidities, and social factors matter too.
- Atypical pneumonia classically causes a "walking pneumonia" picture — patient looks better than the X-ray.
- Procalcitonin trends can help decide when it is safe to stop antibiotics.
- Always reassess severity scores serially — a patient can deteriorate after the initial assessment.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why is hypoxia an admission criterion even when CURB-65 is low?**
A: CURB-65 predicts mortality risk from systemic illness, but it does not directly measure oxygenation. A hypoxic patient needs oxygen therapy and monitoring that cannot safely be done as an outpatient, independent of their mortality score.

**Q2: What clinical clue would make you suspect Legionella over pneumococcal pneumonia?**
A: Legionella often presents with high fever, relative bradycardia, confusion, GI symptoms (diarrhoea), deranged liver function, and hyponatremia, alongside a history of exposure to contaminated water sources (air conditioning, hot tubs).

**Q3: Why combine a beta-lactam with a macrolide in moderate-to-severe CAP?**
A: The beta-lactam covers typical bacteria (pneumococcus, H. influenzae) while the macrolide adds cover for atypical organisms and has additional anti-inflammatory/immunomodulatory effects — important since the causative organism is often not known at presentation.

**Q4: What does "treatment failure" at 72 hours make you think about?**
A: Reconsider whether this is a complication (parapneumonic effusion/empyema, lung abscess), a resistant or unusual organism, an incorrect diagnosis (PE, heart failure, malignancy), or an immunocompromised host requiring broader work-up.

## Case 2 — Pulmonary Tuberculosis
**Covers:** Pulmonary TB (diagnosis) + Anti-TB treatment (HRZE, DOTS, MDR-TB)

### Vignette
A 28-year-old woman presents with 3 weeks of cough, evening low-grade fever, night sweats, 4 kg weight loss, and one episode of streaky hemoptysis. Chest X-ray shows a cavitary lesion in the right upper lobe.

### Thinking Points
1. Upper lobe cavitation in a previously sensitized adult is the classic pattern of **post-primary (reactivation) TB**, as opposed to primary TB, which more often shows hilar adenopathy and mid/lower zone infiltrates in children or the immunosuppressed.
2. First-line diagnostics: sputum smear for AFB (2–3 samples), **GeneXpert MTB/RIF** (rapid PCR-based test that also flags rifampicin resistance as a proxy for MDR-TB), and culture (slowest, but the gold standard for sensitivity and drug susceptibility testing).
3. GeneXpert has largely replaced smear as the preferred initial test where available because it is faster and gives resistance information at the same time.
4. Standard first-line treatment is the **HRZE** regimen: Isoniazid, Rifampicin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol for a 2-month intensive phase, followed by Isoniazid + Rifampicin for a 4-month continuation phase (total 6 months for fully sensitive disease).
5. Key drug toxicities to monitor: Isoniazid (peripheral neuropathy — co-prescribe pyridoxine/vitamin B6; hepatotoxicity), Rifampicin (hepatotoxicity, orange body fluid discoloration, induces CYP450 — reduces efficacy of OCPs, warfarin, some antiretrovirals), Pyrazinamide (hepatotoxicity, hyperuricemia/gout), Ethambutol (optic neuritis — check baseline and periodic visual acuity and red-green colour vision).
6. **DOTS** (Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course) ensures adherence and is central to preventing acquired drug resistance.
7. **MDR-TB** is resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; it requires longer regimens with second-line agents and is suspected in treatment failure, relapse, or contact with a known MDR-TB case.
8. Treatment response is monitored with repeat sputum smear/culture at 2 months, 5 months, and at the end of treatment.

### Clinical Pearls
- Always give pyridoxine with isoniazid to prevent peripheral neuropathy.
- A positive sputum smear at 2 months ("smear non-converter") should prompt evaluation for drug resistance or poor adherence.
- Rifampicin's orange-red body fluid discoloration is harmless but should be explained to patients in advance to avoid alarm.
- All TB patients should be offered HIV testing — co-infection changes the clinical picture and prognosis.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why does GeneXpert testing for rifampicin resistance matter so much clinically?**
A: Rifampicin resistance is a strong surrogate marker for MDR-TB because isoniazid and rifampicin resistance very frequently co-occur; detecting it early allows the clinician to start an appropriate second-line regimen instead of a regimen that is likely to fail.

**Q2: A patient on anti-TB therapy develops blurred vision and reduced colour discrimination. What do you do?**
A: Suspect ethambutol-induced optic neuritis. Stop ethambutol promptly and refer for ophthalmologic assessment — this toxicity is usually reversible if caught early but can become permanent if the drug is continued.

**Q3: Why is DOTS emphasized over simply prescribing the regimen?**
A: TB treatment courses are long (6+ months) and incomplete or erratic adherence is the major driver of relapse and acquired drug resistance; direct observation improves completion rates and protects both the individual patient and public health.

**Q4: How would primary TB differ radiologically from this patient's presentation?**
A: Primary TB more often shows a Ghon focus with hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy (the Ghon-lymph node complex = Ranke complex) typically in a child or immunosuppressed host, rather than the apical cavitation seen with reactivation disease in an immunocompetent adult.

## Case 3 — COPD Exacerbation
**Covers:** COPD (diagnosis, GOLD classification, exacerbation management)

### Vignette
A 65-year-old man with a 40 pack-year smoking history and known COPD presents with 3 days of worsening breathlessness, increased sputum volume, and increased sputum purulence.

### Thinking Points
1. COPD is diagnosed spirometrically by a **post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio <0.70**, confirming fixed (largely irreversible) airflow obstruction.
2. **GOLD staging** grades severity by FEV1 % predicted: mild (≥80%), moderate (50–79%), severe (30–49%), very severe (<30%); the newer GOLD ABE grouping adds symptom burden (mMRC/CAT score) and exacerbation history to guide therapy choice.
3. An exacerbation is diagnosed clinically using the **Anthonisen criteria**: increased dyspnoea, increased sputum volume, and increased sputum purulence — this patient has all three (a "type 1" exacerbation), which is the strongest indication for antibiotics.
4. Acute management: short-acting bronchodilators (SABA ± SAMA via nebulizer), systemic corticosteroids (short course), antibiotics when ≥2 Anthonisen criteria are present or sputum is purulent, and controlled oxygen therapy.
5. Oxygen targets in COPD are deliberately conservative (SpO₂ 88–92%) because over-oxygenation can worsen hypercapnia in chronically CO₂-retaining patients (loss of hypoxic respiratory drive, V/Q mismatch worsening, Haldane effect).
6. Non-invasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP) is indicated for respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35 with rising PaCO₂) despite optimal medical therapy, and reduces mortality and intubation rates in this setting.
7. Long-term management beyond the acute exacerbation: smoking cessation (the single most effective disease-modifying intervention), long-acting bronchodilators (LABA/LAMA), inhaled corticosteroids in frequent exacerbators (especially with blood eosinophilia), pulmonary rehabilitation, and vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal).

### Clinical Pearls
- Target SpO₂ 88–92% in acute COPD exacerbation, not the usual 94–98% — hyperoxia can precipitate dangerous hypercapnic respiratory failure.
- The Anthonisen criteria are the practical bedside trigger for antibiotic use in exacerbations.
- NIV is one of the few interventions in COPD shown to directly reduce mortality during an exacerbation with acidosis.
- Smoking cessation slows FEV1 decline more than any pharmacological therapy.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why can giving high-flow oxygen to a COPD patient be dangerous?**
A: Chronically hypercapnic COPD patients may rely partly on hypoxic drive for ventilation; over-correcting oxygenation can blunt this drive, worsen V/Q mismatching, and increase CO₂ retention via the Haldane effect, precipitating CO₂ narcosis.

**Q2: What arterial blood gas finding tells you this patient needs NIV rather than just nebulizers and steroids?**
A: A respiratory acidosis — pH <7.35 with an elevated PaCO₂ — despite optimal initial medical therapy is the key trigger for starting NIV.

**Q3: How does the GOLD ABE grouping change management decisions compared to FEV1 alone?**
A: FEV1 % predicted tells you the degree of airflow limitation, but treatment escalation (e.g., adding LABA/LAMA combination, or ICS) is actually driven more by symptom burden and exacerbation frequency — two patients with the same FEV1 can need very different treatment intensity.

**Q4: Why might this patient also benefit from inhaled corticosteroids long-term?**
A: Frequent exacerbators, particularly those with peripheral blood eosinophilia, derive more benefit from add-on ICS in reducing future exacerbation frequency, though ICS is not first-line for all COPD patients due to pneumonia risk.

## Case 4 — Acute Severe Asthma
**Covers:** Bronchial asthma (GINA, stepwise management, acute severe asthma)

### Vignette
A 22-year-old woman with known asthma presents with acute breathlessness and wheeze. She is unable to complete sentences, is using accessory muscles, and her peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) is 35% of predicted.

### Thinking Points
1. Recognize severity bands: **acute severe asthma** features include PEFR 33–50% predicted, RR >25/min, HR >110/min, and inability to complete sentences in one breath — this patient meets several of these criteria.
2. **Life-threatening asthma** features include PEFR <33%, SpO₂ <92%, a silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, hypotension, or altered consciousness — these mandate immediate escalation.
3. Long-term (chronic) management follows the **GINA stepwise approach**, increasingly favoring as-needed or regular low-dose ICS-formoterol over SABA-only relief, escalating through steps based on symptom control and exacerbation risk.
4. Acute management of severe asthma: high-flow oxygen, nebulized salbutamol with ipratropium bromide, systemic corticosteroids (oral or IV), and — if not responding — IV magnesium sulfate as an adjunct bronchodilator.
5. Arterial blood gas interpretation is counterintuitive here: tachypnoeic asthmatics typically blow off CO₂ and are hypocapnic; a **normal or rising PaCO₂** in a severely breathless asthmatic is an ominous sign of fatigue and impending respiratory failure, not reassurance.
6. Escalation pathway: ICU referral and consideration of ventilatory support if the patient deteriorates despite maximal nebulized and systemic therapy.
7. Distinguishing asthma from COPD: asthma typically shows greater reversibility on spirometry, onset at a younger age, and association with atopy/allergic triggers, compared to the fixed obstruction of COPD.

### Clinical Pearls
- A "silent chest" on auscultation is a life-threatening sign of minimal air movement, not improvement.
- Rising PaCO₂ in an asthmatic in extremis signals exhaustion and should prompt urgent escalation, including possible intubation.
- Sedatives should be avoided in acute severe asthma as they can suppress respiratory drive further.
- IV magnesium sulfate is reserved for severe/life-threatening asthma not responding to initial bronchodilator and steroid therapy.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why is a "silent chest" more worrying than loud wheezing?**
A: Wheeze requires turbulent airflow; in near-complete airway obstruction there is too little airflow to generate sound, so silence reflects very severe obstruction rather than resolution.

**Q2: This patient's initial ABG shows a normal PaCO₂. Why is that concerning rather than reassuring?**
A: An asthmatic in acute distress should be tachypnoeic and hypocapnic from hyperventilation; a PaCO₂ that has "normalized" suggests the patient is tiring and losing the ability to sustain the high respiratory effort, signalling impending respiratory failure.

**Q3: What is the role of ipratropium bromide in addition to salbutamol?**
A: Ipratropium (an anticholinergic) provides additive bronchodilation through a different pathway (blocking vagally-mediated bronchoconstriction) and has been shown to improve outcomes when combined with beta-agonists in severe asthma exacerbations.

**Q4: Why would you avoid sedating an anxious, breathless asthmatic patient?**
A: Sedatives can depress the respiratory centre and reduce the patient's ability to compensate for airway obstruction, increasing the risk of respiratory arrest.

## Case 5 — Pleural Effusion
**Covers:** Pleural effusion (transudate vs exudate, Light's criteria, causes)

### Vignette
A 55-year-old man presents with progressive dyspnoea. Examination reveals decreased breath sounds and stony dullness to percussion at the right base. Chest X-ray shows blunting of the right costophrenic angle.

### Thinking Points
1. The first diagnostic step for a new effusion of unclear cause is diagnostic **thoracentesis**, sending fluid for protein, LDH, glucose, pH, cell count/differential, Gram stain/culture, and cytology as indicated.
2. **Light's criteria** classify the fluid as an exudate if any one of the following is met: pleural fluid protein/serum protein >0.5, pleural fluid LDH/serum LDH >0.6, or pleural fluid LDH greater than two-thirds of the upper limit of normal serum LDH.
3. Transudates arise from systemic pressure/oncotic imbalances ("failure" states): heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.
4. Exudates arise from local pleural/lung pathology causing increased capillary permeability: infection (parapneumonic effusion/empyema), malignancy, tuberculosis, pulmonary embolism, and connective tissue disease.
5. Additional fluid clues: low glucose and low pH suggest empyema, complicated parapneumonic effusion, malignancy, or rheumatoid effusion; high adenosine deaminase (ADA) supports tuberculous pleuritis in endemic settings; lymphocyte-predominant exudates suggest TB or malignancy, whereas neutrophil-predominant exudates suggest acute bacterial infection.
6. A discordant picture can occur in heart failure patients on diuretics — diuresis can concentrate the pleural fluid protein/LDH enough to mimic an exudate by Light's criteria despite a transudative underlying cause; serum-to-pleural-fluid albumin gradient (>1.2 g/dL favours transudate) can help resolve this.
7. Management depends on the cause and the fluid characteristics: large or symptomatic effusions may need therapeutic drainage; frank pus, positive Gram stain, or pH <7.2 in a parapneumonic effusion indicates empyema requiring chest tube drainage.

### Clinical Pearls
- Light's criteria: protein ratio >0.5 OR LDH ratio >0.6 OR pleural LDH > ⅔ upper limit of normal serum LDH = exudate.
- "Failure states" (heart, liver, kidney) cause transudates; local pleuropulmonary disease causes exudates.
- A very low pleural fluid pH or glucose in a parapneumonic effusion is a red flag for empyema needing drainage.
- High pleural ADA is a useful low-cost clue to tuberculous pleuritis in high-burden settings.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why does heart failure typically cause a transudate rather than an exudate?**
A: Elevated hydrostatic pressure from heart failure forces low-protein fluid across an otherwise normal, non-inflamed pleural membrane, so the fluid resembles a simple ultrafiltrate rather than a protein-rich inflammatory exudate.

**Q2: A heart failure patient on long-term furosemide has a pleural fluid LDH ratio of 0.65. Does this rule out a transudative cause?**
A: Not necessarily — diuretic therapy can concentrate pleural fluid protein and LDH enough to falsely cross into the exudate range by Light's criteria; calculating the serum-pleural fluid albumin gradient (>1.2 g/dL suggests transudate) helps clarify the picture in this situation.

**Q3: What pleural fluid findings would make you urgently call for chest tube drainage rather than just antibiotics?**
A: Frank pus, a positive Gram stain/culture, or a pleural fluid pH <7.2 in the setting of a parapneumonic effusion indicate empyema or a complicated effusion that requires drainage in addition to antibiotics.

**Q4: Why is pleural fluid ADA useful in some settings but not universally relied upon?**
A: It is a sensitive and inexpensive marker for tuberculous pleuritis in high TB-prevalence populations, but its specificity falls in low-prevalence settings or in the presence of other lymphocyte-rich exudates (e.g., lymphoma), so it should be interpreted alongside other clinical and laboratory data.

## Case 6 — Lung Carcinoma
**Covers:** Lung carcinoma (types, paraneoplastic syndromes, staging, palliative care)

### Vignette
A 68-year-old heavy smoker presents with hemoptysis, weight loss, hoarseness of voice, and is found to have hyponatremia on routine bloods. CT chest shows a central lung mass with mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

### Thinking Points
1. Lung cancer is broadly divided into **non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)** — adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, large cell carcinoma — and **small cell lung cancer (SCLC)**; this distinction drives treatment, since early NSCLC may be surgically resectable while SCLC is typically treated with chemotherapy/radiotherapy due to early systemic spread.
2. **Hoarseness of voice** in this context suggests recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy from mediastinal nodal or tumour involvement (left recurrent laryngeal nerve is affected more often given its longer intrathoracic course around the aortic arch).
3. **Paraneoplastic syndromes** to recognize: SIADH and ectopic ACTH/Cushing's syndrome classically with SCLC; hypercalcaemia (via PTHrP) more classically with squamous cell carcinoma; Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome with SCLC; hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy (clubbing, periostitis) with NSCLC.
4. This patient's hyponatremia raises suspicion for SIADH, which should prompt evaluation of serum/urine osmolality and sodium to confirm, and supports a search for SCLC.
5. Staging: NSCLC uses the **TNM system** to guide resectability and prognosis; SCLC is more simply staged as **limited-stage** (confined to one hemithorax, treatable with curative-intent chemoradiotherapy) versus **extensive-stage** (metastatic, treated primarily with systemic chemotherapy).
6. Diagnostic workup: CT chest for initial characterization, bronchoscopy with biopsy or EBUS-guided sampling of mediastinal nodes for tissue diagnosis and staging, and PET-CT to detect distant metastases.
7. Palliative care principles become central when curative treatment is not feasible: symptom control (pain, dyspnoea, cough), management of complications (malignant pleural effusion, superior vena cava obstruction, bone metastases), and early integration of palliative care alongside oncological treatment.

### Clinical Pearls
- New hyponatremia in a smoker with a lung mass should raise suspicion for SCLC-associated SIADH.
- An apical (Pancoast) tumour can cause Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) via sympathetic chain involvement.
- Hoarseness from recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy implies mediastinal nodal disease — it is a staging clue, not just a local symptom.
- SCLC is highly chemo/radiosensitive initially but has a high relapse rate; NSCLC's curability depends heavily on stage at diagnosis.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why does hoarseness matter beyond being an uncomfortable symptom?**
A: It implies recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement, usually from mediastinal lymph node or tumour extension, which is a marker of locoregional spread and therefore affects staging and resectability.

**Q2: How would you confirm that this patient's hyponatremia is from SIADH and not another cause?**
A: Confirm euvolemia clinically, then demonstrate low serum osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality not maximally dilute, urine sodium typically >20–40 mmol/L), after excluding other causes such as adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, or diuretic use.

**Q3: Why is the limited vs extensive staging system used for SCLC instead of full TNM staging?**
A: SCLC behaves more like a systemic disease at presentation in most patients, so the practical clinical question is whether disease is confined enough to one hemithorax to be encompassed in a curative-intent radiotherapy field (limited stage) or has spread beyond that (extensive stage), which more directly determines treatment approach than detailed anatomic TNM staging.

**Q4: What would make you suspect a Pancoast tumour rather than a more central lung cancer?**
A: An apical location on imaging combined with Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) and/or shoulder/arm pain from brachial plexus involvement points toward an apical (Pancoast) tumour invading the sympathetic chain and surrounding structures.

## Case 7 — Pulmonary Embolism
**Covers:** Pulmonary embolism (Wells score, D-dimer, CTPA, anticoagulation, thrombolysis)

### Vignette
A 45-year-old woman, recently returned from a long-haul flight, presents with sudden-onset pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness. HR 112, BP 122/80, SpO₂ 93% on room air.

### Thinking Points
1. Risk factors for venous thromboembolism include prolonged immobility (e.g., long-haul travel), recent surgery, malignancy, pregnancy, oestrogen-containing contraception/HRT, and inherited or acquired thrombophilia.
2. The **Wells score** estimates clinical probability of PE using items such as clinical signs of DVT, heart rate >100, recent immobilization or surgery, prior DVT/PE, hemoptysis, active malignancy, and whether an alternative diagnosis is less likely than PE.
3. Diagnostic pathway: in patients with low/moderate clinical probability, a sensitive **D-dimer** test is used to rule out PE if negative; in patients with high clinical probability, or a positive D-dimer, proceed directly to **CTPA** (CT pulmonary angiography), the imaging gold standard.
4. A **V/Q scan** is a useful alternative to CTPA when CT contrast is contraindicated (e.g., significant renal impairment, contrast allergy) or in pregnancy, where it can reduce maternal breast radiation exposure.
5. Once PE is confirmed, risk-stratify by haemodynamic stability, evidence of right ventricular strain (on echocardiography or CT), and biomarkers (troponin, BNP) to classify as massive (haemodynamically unstable), submassive (RV strain but stable), or non-massive PE.
6. Management: anticoagulation (LMWH, unfractionated heparin, or a direct oral anticoagulant) is first-line for most patients; systemic thrombolysis is reserved for massive PE with shock or severe hypotension; an IVC filter is considered when anticoagulation is contraindicated.
7. D-dimer is far more useful for *ruling out* PE in low-probability patients than for ruling it in, since it is sensitive but non-specific (also elevated in infection, pregnancy, malignancy, recent surgery, and advancing age).

### Clinical Pearls
- A negative D-dimer in a patient with low clinical probability effectively excludes PE without needing imaging.
- Massive PE = haemodynamic instability (shock/hypotension), and is the key trigger for considering thrombolysis.
- The classic ECG finding S1Q3T3 is described in PE but is neither sensitive nor specific; sinus tachycardia is the most common ECG finding.
- D-dimer is less useful in pregnancy because it is often elevated physiologically regardless of clot presence.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why would you not simply order a D-dimer on every patient with chest pain to "rule out" PE?**
A: D-dimer has poor specificity — many conditions unrelated to thromboembolism (infection, recent surgery, pregnancy, malignancy, older age) elevate it — so it is only useful as a rule-out test in patients with a low pre-test (clinical) probability; in high-probability patients, a negative D-dimer does not reliably exclude PE and imaging should proceed regardless.

**Q2: This patient becomes hypotensive shortly after arrival. How does that change your management?**
A: Hypotension/shock indicates a massive PE causing right ventricular failure; this is an indication to consider systemic thrombolysis (or catheter-directed therapy) in addition to supportive haemodynamic management, rather than anticoagulation alone.

**Q3: Why might a V/Q scan be preferred over CTPA in a pregnant patient?**
A: V/Q scanning, particularly a perfusion-only protocol, can deliver a lower radiation dose to maternal breast tissue compared with CTPA, while still providing adequate diagnostic sensitivity for PE in this population.

**Q4: What does right ventricular strain on echo tell you about a haemodynamically stable patient with confirmed PE?**
A: It identifies "submassive" PE — the patient is stable now but has evidence of right heart strain from the embolic burden, which carries a higher risk of deterioration and may warrant closer monitoring (e.g., ICU-level observation) even without an indication for thrombolysis.

## Case 8 — Interstitial Lung Disease
**Covers:** ILD (IPF, sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, HRCT patterns)

### Vignette
A 60-year-old man presents with 18 months of progressive exertional dyspnoea and a dry cough. Examination reveals fine, bibasilar, end-inspiratory ("Velcro") crackles and finger clubbing.

### Thinking Points
1. The clinical picture of chronic dry cough, progressive dyspnoea, bibasilar fine crackles, and clubbing is classic for **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)**.
2. HRCT is central to diagnosis: IPF shows a **usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)** pattern — subpleural and basal-predominant reticulation with honeycombing and minimal ground-glass change — often allowing a confident diagnosis without biopsy when the pattern is classic.
3. **Sarcoidosis** typically presents differently: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (often found incidentally), non-caseating granulomas on biopsy, and can affect multiple systems (skin — erythema nodosum; eyes — uveitis; cardiac involvement); it may also be asymptomatic.
4. **Hypersensitivity pneumonitis** should be suspected when there is a history of antigen exposure (birds, mouldy hay, certain occupational dusts); HRCT often shows ground-glass opacities with air-trapping (mosaic pattern) rather than the basal fibrosis of IPF.
5. Pulmonary function tests in ILD typically show a **restrictive pattern**: reduced FVC and TLC, a preserved or even increased FEV1/FVC ratio, and reduced **DLCO** — DLCO reduction is often the earliest and most sensitive functional abnormality.
6. Management differs by aetiology: IPF is treated with antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib), supportive care, and consideration of lung transplantation in eligible patients — importantly, high-dose immunosuppression is *not* beneficial in IPF (unlike many other inflammatory ILDs) and may cause harm.
7. Sarcoidosis is managed with corticosteroids when there is significant or progressive organ involvement; hypersensitivity pneumonitis is managed primarily by antigen avoidance, with corticosteroids for acute/subacute flares.

### Clinical Pearls
- Clubbing + bibasilar "Velcro" crackles + restrictive PFTs should make you think of IPF.
- DLCO is frequently the most sensitive (and earliest abnormal) PFT parameter in ILD, often dropping before FVC does.
- Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on chest X-ray is the classic clue to sarcoidosis.
- Unlike most inflammatory ILDs, IPF does *not* respond to (and may be harmed by) high-dose steroids/immunosuppressants — this is a frequently tested distinction.

### Socratic Q&A
**Q1: Why does this patient's clinical and radiological picture point more toward IPF than sarcoidosis?**
A: The chronic, slowly progressive course over 18 months, bibasilar fine crackles, clubbing, and (presumed) HRCT pattern of basal subpleural reticulation/honeycombing are typical of IPF, whereas sarcoidosis more often presents with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, a more variable course, and frequently extrapulmonary features.

**Q2: Why is DLCO often more useful than FVC for picking up early ILD?**
A: DLCO reflects the gas-exchanging surface area and integrity of the alveolar-capillary interface, which is affected very early by interstitial thickening and fibrosis, often before lung volumes (FVC) have measurably fallen.

**Q3: A resident wants to start high-dose steroids for this patient's "ILD." Why should you pause before agreeing?**
A: If this is IPF specifically, high-dose immunosuppression has not been shown to provide benefit and may increase the risk of harm (as shown in trial data leading to revised practice); steroids are appropriate for many other ILDs (e.g., sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis flares) but not as a default for IPF, so confirming the specific ILD subtype first is essential.

**Q4: What single historical question would most efficiently help distinguish hypersensitivity pneumonitis from IPF here?**
A: Ask about a specific antigen exposure history — birds/feather bedding, mouldy hay, occupational dust/chemical exposure, or other organic antigens — since a clear temporal relationship to exposure (and possible improvement away from it) strongly favours hypersensitivity pneumonitis over IPF.
