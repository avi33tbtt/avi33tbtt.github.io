Step 11.1

The Patient's Inner Question List

Questions this patient is most likely carrying silently — organised by the five domains of patient information need.

1 · "What is happening to my body?" — Disease understanding
Why am I suddenly struggling to breathe when my problem was always my foot?
Patients rarely understand how a foot wound can affect the lungs; the causal chain (infection → clotting → embolism) is medically non-intuitive, so they feel confused and alarmed.
Has my diabetes done this — is this my fault?
Many patients internalise guilt about long-standing diabetes complications, yet fear being judged by clinicians if they voice it.
What exactly is osteomyelitis — does it mean my bone is dying?
The word "bone infection" sounds catastrophic; patients often imagine rapid, irreversible destruction and are too frightened to clarify.
2 · "What will happen to me?" — Prognosis & trajectory
Am I going to lose my foot?
Amputation is a deeply feared outcome for diabetic foot disease patients; they frequently avoid asking because they are not sure they can handle the answer.
Is the clot in my lungs life-threatening? Could I die from this?
Fear of death from PE is high but patients hesitate to ask because the question feels "too dramatic" or they fear alarming their family.
How long will I need to stay in hospital?
Concerns about income, caregiving duties at home, and social obligations make duration of admission an urgent but rarely voiced priority.
3 · "What do I need to do?" — Self-management
What should I eat to help my blood sugar stop going so high — or so low?
Wide glucose swings are distressing; patients sense something is wrong with their diet or insulin but don't know where to start and fear appearing non-compliant.
Do I need to inject insulin myself, and what do I do if the dose seems wrong?
Insulin regimen changes are intimidating; patients worry about getting it wrong at home but may not admit this to avoid seeming incompetent.
How do I look after my other foot to stop the same thing happening?
Patients with one ulcer know the other foot is at risk, but daily foot care is rarely explained concretely and they feel unsure who to ask.
4 · "What can go wrong?" — Worry & safety
The blood thinner I'm getting — can it cause me to bleed internally?
Anticoagulant therapy is frightening; patients hear about bleeding risk but don't know how to weigh it, so the worry sits unaddressed.
What signs of danger should I watch for so I can call for help in time?
Patients want a personalised warning list but assume the team will proactively give them one — they rarely receive it clearly before discharge.
5 · "What does this mean for my life?" — Functional impact
Will I be able to walk normally again?
Mobility and independence are central to self-image; patients fear but rarely voice concerns about permanent disability from foot disease.
Can I go back to work, and if so, when?
Financial and occupational concerns are urgent but patients suppress them in clinical encounters, believing they are not "medical" questions.
Who will help me at home when I am discharged, and am I safe to be alone?
Social isolation is common but patients are embarrassed to reveal limited support networks, fearing it will complicate or delay discharge.
Step 11.2

Plain-Language Explainer

Written at approximately a Grade 8 reading level. Medical terms are defined in brackets on first use.

(a) What is happening in your body — using an analogy

Diabetes, when it is not well-controlled for a long time, slowly damages the blood vessels and nerves in the feet. Think of those blood vessels as irrigation pipes. Years of high sugar gradually corrode the pipes, reducing the water pressure (blood flow) reaching the toes. Nerves stop sending pain signals correctly, so a sore can develop without you even noticing. Without good blood flow, the body cannot fight infection effectively, so a wound on your toe became infected, spread to the bone (this is called osteomyelitis — a bone infection), and created a pocket of pus called an abscess.

A serious infection also puts your blood into a "sticky" state — it clots more easily than normal. One of those clots may have broken off and travelled through the bloodstream to land in the blood vessels of your lungs. This is called a pulmonary embolism (PE) — "pulmonary" means lung, and "embolism" means a travelling blockage. Like a kink in a garden hose, the clot reduces blood flow through the lungs, making it harder to get enough oxygen into the body. That is why you are short of breath and need the oxygen mask.

(c) What your treatments are doing and why they matter

  • Oxygen via nasal cannula: Gives your body extra oxygen while the clot is being treated, preventing organ damage from low oxygen levels.
  • Enoxaparin (blood thinner injection): Prevents the existing clot from growing larger and stops new clots forming. It does not dissolve the current clot immediately — your body's own clot-busting system does that over weeks — but it gives your system a chance to do so safely.
  • CT Pulmonary Angiogram (CTPA) scan: A special scan using dye to take detailed pictures of the blood vessels in your lungs. It will confirm whether a clot is present and show how large it is, guiding further treatment decisions.
  • IV antibiotics: Strong antibiotics delivered directly into the vein reach the bone more effectively than tablets, fighting the infection at its source.
  • Basal-bolus insulin regimen: A twice-daily background insulin (basal) plus small doses at meal times (bolus) mimics how a healthy pancreas works, keeping your sugar far more stable than the current sliding-scale approach and supporting wound healing.

Step 11.4

Medication Literacy Check

Medication Patient Question Plain-Language Answer Why This Question Matters
Enoxaparin
(Low-molecular-weight heparin injection)		 Will this injection make me bleed everywhere — even inside? Enoxaparin reduces your blood's clotting ability, so you may bruise more easily or take longer to stop bleeding from a small cut. However, serious internal bleeding is uncommon at the correct dose and with careful monitoring. Tell the team immediately if you notice unusual bruising, blood in your urine, or black stools. Fear of bleeding is the single biggest barrier to anticoagulant adherence; an informed patient will monitor themselves rather than secretly stopping the medication.
Enoxaparin (continued) Do I need to give this injection to myself at home? If your doctor decides you need to continue this medication at home, the nursing team will teach you — or a family member — how to inject it safely under the skin of the abdomen. It is simpler than it sounds, and a nurse will practice with you before you leave. Patients feel anxiety about self-injection; early counselling reduces non-adherence after discharge.
Basal-Bolus Insulin
(e.g., Glargine + rapid-acting)		 I'm already on insulin — how is this different from what I was doing before? A sliding scale only gives insulin when your sugar is already high — it is reactive. A basal-bolus plan gives a steady background dose at night plus a small dose before each meal, keeping your sugar in a healthier range throughout the day and helping your wound heal faster. Patients may not continue the new regimen after discharge if they don't understand why it is better than their previous one.
Basal-Bolus Insulin (continued) Can I get dangerously low blood sugar (a hypo) from this? Yes — low blood sugar (called hypoglycaemia) is a risk with insulin, especially if you skip a meal. You should always eat around the time of your meal-time dose. Carry glucose tablets or a sugary drink. Signs of a low include shakiness, sweating, and confusion — treat immediately with sugar and tell your team. Hypoglycaemia fear causes patients to under-dose insulin, perpetuating poor control and delayed healing.
IV Antibiotics
(broad-spectrum, bone-penetrating)		 Will the antibiotics make me feel sick to my stomach? Some antibiotics can cause nausea, loose stools, or a rash. Tell your nurse straight away if this happens — there are anti-nausea medicines and, if needed, the antibiotic can be reviewed. Never stop an antibiotic course early because you feel better; bone infections need the full course to clear completely. Early antibiotic discontinuation is the most common cause of treatment failure in osteomyelitis; patients need to understand why the full course matters.
Step 11.5

Red-Flag Awareness Guide

  Seek emergency care immediately (go to hospital now)

  • Sudden severe shortness of breath — especially if worse than on admission, or if you feel faint or your lips/fingers go blue.Teaching note: Suggests worsening PE, haemodynamic compromise, or right heart strain — a clinical emergency requiring immediate re-evaluation.
  • Coughing up blood, or blood in your stools / urine (black, tarry, or red stools).Teaching note: May signal major anticoagulant-related bleeding — GI haemorrhage or haemoptysis from infarction — requiring anticoagulant reversal.
  • Sudden confusion, severe headache, or difficulty speaking.Teaching note: Possible intracranial bleed on anticoagulation, or septic encephalopathy from uncontrolled infection — both time-critical emergencies.
  • Rapidly spreading redness, heat, or blackening of the foot or leg, especially if accompanied by high fever and shaking chills.Teaching note: Signs of necrotising fasciitis or septic progression — surgical emergency; delay in debridement dramatically worsens outcome.
  • Blood sugar below 70 mg/dL with shakiness, confusion, or loss of consciousness despite sugar intake.Teaching note: Severe hypoglycaemia with neuroglycopenia — risk of seizure and permanent neurological damage; requires IV dextrose.

  Call your doctor within 24 hours (do not wait)

  • Breathlessness that is gradually getting worse over a day, even if it doesn't feel like an emergency yet.Teaching note: Gradual worsening may indicate clot extension or inadequate anticoagulation — warrants dose adjustment or repeat imaging.
  • Foot wound discharge that increases, changes colour to green or brown, or develops a very bad smell.Teaching note: Suggests polymicrobial progression or antibiotic failure; may require surgical reassessment and microbiological review.
  • Blood sugar consistently above 300 mg/dL on more than two readings despite taking insulin as instructed.Teaching note: Indicates poor glycaemic control with risk of hyperosmolar state; impairs immune function and wound healing — regimen adjustment needed urgently.
  • Pain, swelling, or warmth developing in the other leg.Teaching note: New DVT — hypercoagulable state from infection increases bilateral risk; needs Doppler confirmation and possible dose escalation.

  Monitor at home (watch and record)

  • Blood sugar readings: Check before each meal and at bedtime. Record in a notebook. Aim to keep readings between 80 and 180 mg/dL.
  • Oxygen saturation (SpO₂): If given a home pulse oximeter, readings below 94% at rest should prompt you to seek urgent review.
  • Wound appearance: Check the dressing daily. Note any bleeding through the dressing, new swelling, or redness spreading beyond the wound edges.
  • Temperature: If you feel hot and shivery, take your temperature. A reading above 38°C (100.4°F) warrants a call to your doctor.
Step 11.6

Lifestyle, Diet & Daily Living

🥗 Diet & Nutrition

Focus on low-glycaemic-index foods (lentils, wholegrains, non-starchy vegetables). Avoid sugary drinks, white rice in large quantities, and sweetened snacks. Eat regular meals at consistent times to work with the new insulin regimen.

⚑ Individualise: portion size and carbohydrate targets depend on weight and kidney function — dietitian referral recommended.

🏃 Physical Activity

Complete rest from weight-bearing on the right foot until the wound and bone infection are under control. Light upper-body movement in bed is encouraged to reduce DVT risk. No walking on the ulcerated foot until cleared by the surgical team.

⚑ Individualise: mobility plan depends on surgical debridement outcome and wound healing status.

😴 Sleep & Rest

Prioritise sleep — the body heals most effectively during rest. Keep the foot elevated on a pillow while in bed to reduce swelling. Discomfort and hospital noise can disrupt sleep; ask the nurse about positioning aids.

💼 Work & Occupation

Return to work is not appropriate until the PE is treated, the wound is healing, and glucose is stable. Timeline is likely several weeks to months depending on surgical outcome and anticoagulation duration.

⚑ Individualise: occupation type (standing vs. seated) significantly affects return-to-work timing.

🚗 Driving

Not advisable during hospital admission. After discharge, driving on anticoagulants is generally permitted once stable, but the patient should check with her doctor if she takes high-dose insulin, as significant hypoglycaemia poses a driving risk.

⚑ Individualise: local road-authority guidance on insulin-treated diabetes and driving applies.

🍺 Alcohol

Avoid during the acute admission. After discharge, alcohol lowers blood sugar unpredictably, especially with insulin, and can interact with some antibiotics (e.g., metronidazole). Moderation is advised once clinically stable.

🧹 Foot Monitoring at Home

Daily inspection of both feet using a mirror if needed. Look for new cuts, redness, blisters, or changes in skin colour. Never walk barefoot. Keep feet clean and dry. Moisturise the skin but avoid between the toes.

📊 Home Glucose Monitoring

A glucometer and logbook are essential. Blood glucose should be checked 4 times daily (before meals and bedtime) until the regimen is stable. Record readings and bring the logbook to every clinic visit.

⚑ Individualise: if continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is accessible, this significantly reduces the burden of finger-prick testing.
Step 11.7

The Unasked Question — Patient Insight Mining

Insight 01

The foot you can't feel is more dangerous than the one that hurts

What it isDiabetic neuropathy means nerve damage has probably reduced sensation in both feet. This patient may have no warning pain from a new wound on the left foot — yet that foot is equally at risk. Many patients believe that "if it doesn't hurt, it's fine." The opposite is true in diabetic neuropathy: silence is the danger signal.
Why missedPain is the body's intuitive alarm system; patients naturally use pain as their primary health monitor and don't think to check for wounds that cause no discomfort.
How to introduceDuring the foot-care counselling session, say: "Can you feel me touching this part of your foot right now?" — then demonstrate the sensation deficit and directly link it to why daily visual inspection matters more than waiting for pain.
Insight 02

Stopping the blood thinner early because you "feel better" is one of the most dangerous things you can do

What it isMost patients feel significantly better within a few days of anticoagulation. The natural human instinct is to conclude the clot has gone and to stop the injection — especially if it is painful or expensive. In reality, the clot dissolves slowly over weeks, and stopping early allows it to re-expand or for a new clot to form, with risk of sudden death.
Why missedPatients equate symptom resolution with disease cure — a logical but dangerous heuristic for PE, where the underlying hypercoagulable state (from active infection) persists independently of symptoms.
How to introduceAt the point of prescribing: "You are going to feel much better on this medicine within a day or two — but I need to tell you that feeling better does not mean the clot has gone. If you stop the injections early, the clot can come back, sometimes much more seriously. The full course is non-negotiable."
Insight 03

Getting the infection under control is just as important as treating the clot — possibly more so

What it isThe underlying driver of both the PE and the glycaemic chaos in this patient is the active bone infection. As long as the osteomyelitis remains uncontrolled, the body stays in an inflammatory, hypercoagulable, and insulin-resistant state. Blood thinners manage the PE; only surgical source control and antibiotics remove the root cause. Many patients focus entirely on the more dramatic diagnosis (PE) and deprioritise wound care and surgical follow-up after discharge.
Why missedThe breathing difficulty is the frightening, visible crisis; the foot wound, present for a month, has become normalised background noise for both patient and family.
How to introduceExplicitly name the causal chain: "Your breathing problem started because of your foot — if we fix the breathing but leave the foot infection untreated, the clot can come back. Every wound dressing, every antibiotic dose, every surgical review appointment is also protecting your lungs."

Step 11.10

Critical Awareness Debrief — Patient Education Lens

1 · Health literacy assumptions
The AI assumed basic literacy, the ability to self-monitor blood glucose, and access to a glucometer. It also assumed the patient understands what a "clot" means. In practice, 12 years of diabetes does not guarantee health literacy — the severity of the current crisis suggests possible gaps in self-management education that should have been addressed years earlier. Verify before assuming familiarity with any monitoring device.
2 · Cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic factors
The clinical input gives no information about language, cultural health beliefs, or financial capacity. In many contexts, insulin cost, injection stigma, and distrust of surgical intervention are significant barriers that would fundamentally alter how information should be framed and prioritised. The family's caregiving capacity (one working adult, no home nurse mentioned) is a real socioeconomic constraint that could compromise post-discharge safety.
3 · Where the AI output may be overconfident or incomplete
The module assumes the PE diagnosis is confirmed — it has not yet been. The CTPA is still pending. All patient education on PE should be delivered conditionally ("we suspect a clot and are treating for it while we confirm"). Additionally, antibiotic choice and duration for osteomyelitis have not been specified; the medication table covers the principle but cannot give the patient accurate specific instructions.
4 · What AI analysis cannot tell you
No AI module can tell you how this patient feels emotionally about the possibility of losing her foot, how much support she has at home, what her previous experience of healthcare has been, whether she trusts you, or what her personal goals and priorities are. The most important next step is to sit with the patient (and family, with her consent) and ask: "What is worrying you most right now, and what do you most want to understand before we go further?" Her answer will tell you more than any generated output can.
