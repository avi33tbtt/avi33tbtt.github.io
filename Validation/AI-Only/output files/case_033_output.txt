DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 33: 62F, chronic kidney disease on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) with hypertension, presenting with pedal edema, decreased urine output, and SOB
Date: 2026-08-17
Learner: N/A
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Pedal edema, decreased urinary output, and shortness of breath, all x4 days.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: This symptom triad in an older patient with a multi-year hypertension history should immediately activate the pattern for fluid-overload state secondary to renal impairment (cardiorenal or primary renal failure with volume retention) — the presenting complaint alone, before any further data, should raise chronic kidney disease as a leading hypothesis rather than a purely cardiac one, given the combination of oliguria with edema.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. 7 years ago: giddiness and fever, diagnosed as hypertension by an RMP and started on atenolol, with no renal workup documented at that time: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: A first presentation of "giddiness and fever" leading straight to a hypertension diagnosis without apparent renal function testing is a significant missed decision point in retrospect — undiagnosed CKD can itself cause secondary hypertension, and this initial encounter represents the earliest point at which renal disease could have been identified, years before the eventual 2021 diagnosis.
  2b. 1 year back: pedal edema, facial puffiness, and abdominal distension attributed by the same RMP to "hypertension" and treated by continuing antihypertensives alone: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: This constellation (edema + puffiness + ascites-type distension) is a much stronger signal for a nephrotic-range or fluid-retentive renal process than for hypertension alone, and represents a second, more prominent missed decision point where earlier renal-specific investigation (urinalysis, creatinine) could have identified CKD roughly a year before the eventual diagnosis.
  2c. 4 months back (April 2021): SOB grade 4, pedal edema, abdominal distension, facial puffiness — ultrasound at Suryapet reveals underlying kidney disease, patient referred to this institute: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: This is the point at which the diagnostic ambiguity of the preceding years was finally closed by objective imaging, confirming kidney disease as the unifying explanation for the edema/puffiness/distension pattern that had been misattributed to hypertension for roughly a year.
  2d. Decreased appetite x4 months: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: Anorexia in this timeframe is consistent with uremia from progressive/advanced CKD, but could also reflect a separate process (e.g., depression, GI pathology); in the context of the concurrent renal diagnosis, uremic anorexia is the more probable explanation, though the case does not explicitly state this reasoning.
  2e. No K/C/O DM, CAD, Asthma, TB, Epilepsy: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Absence of diabetes is notable and somewhat atypical, since diabetic nephropathy is the most common cause of CKD in this age group — its absence should sharpen the search for the actual underlying etiology of this patient's CKD (e.g., long-standing/undertreated hypertension itself, chronic interstitial disease, or an undiagnosed glomerular process), which the case never explicitly names.
  Missing history: No documented cause/etiology of the CKD is ever established (hypertensive nephrosclerosis is implied by the "CKD... with HTN" diagnosis line, but this is not explicitly reasoned through against other causes); no history of NSAID use, herbal/alternative medicine use, or other nephrotoxin exposure; no family history of renal disease; no clarification of how long the patient has actually been on maintenance hemodialysis before this admission, despite the diagnosis being "CKD on MHD."

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Series of events during dialysis: BP 210/130, treated with Nicardia and Arkamin without response, then Lasix 80mg + labetalol 10mg (BP to 170/110), then further labetalol (BP to 160/110), improving to 140/90 over half an hour, then nitroglycerin given, patient stabilized: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: A hypertensive emergency occurring during dialysis in a patient with known fluid overload is a classic complication of intradialytic volume/pressure shifts in CKD, and the stepwise escalation (calcium channel blocker → central alpha-agonist → loop diuretic + beta blocker → nitrate) reflects real-time analytical (System 2) titration against a moving target — this is the most acute and highest-stakes decision sequence in the entire case.
  3b. SOB with SpO2 80%, BiPAP applied raising SpO2 to 90%, ABG showing mild acidosis, stabilized after 2 hours BiPAP then transitioned to oxygen: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Severe hypoxia in a fluid-overloaded CKD patient during a hypertensive crisis is most consistent with acute pulmonary edema (flash pulmonary edema from a hypertensive emergency plus volume overload), and the mild acidosis on ABG is consistent with a combination of respiratory compromise and the patient's underlying uremic metabolic acidosis — though the case does not explicitly separate these two acid-base contributors.
  3c. On admission-day examination: afebrile, BP 140/90, PR 84, RR 22, SpO2 94% RA, GRBS 102, CVS S1S2+, RS bilateral air entry+, abdomen soft/non-tender, bowel sounds+: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: A stable, largely unremarkable general and systemic exam after the acute dialysis-related crisis had resolved supports that the earlier hypertensive emergency and hypoxia were acute, reversible events superimposed on the chronic CKD/HTN baseline, rather than pointing to a new competing diagnosis.
  System 1 nodes: pedal edema + facial puffiness + decreased urine output → volume-overloaded CKD (rapid, appropriately supported pattern match).
  System 2 nodes: the intradialytic BP crisis management (sequential drug titration against serial BP readings) and the BiPAP/ABG-guided respiratory management both required active analytical reasoning under time pressure, which the case documents step-by-step but does not narrate as an explicit reasoning process.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Serology negative, RT-PCR negative: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: These appropriately exclude transfusion-relevant bloodborne infections (relevant given the patient received 3 blood transfusions) and active COVID-19 infection as a contributor to the respiratory presentation, narrowing the SOB etiology back toward the fluid-overload/pulmonary-edema explanation.
  4b. Three blood transfusions (28/9, 6/10, 16/10/21) and three hemodialysis sessions (21/9, 6/10, 11/10/21) documented, but no corresponding hemoglobin values, renal function trends (creatinine, urea, potassium), or other lab numbers are given in the extracted text: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: Repeated transfusions strongly imply a significant anemia (expected and common in CKD from erythropoietin deficiency), and this is indirectly supported by the treatment list (Livogen, Orofer XT — both iron supplements), but the case never documents the actual hemoglobin values or the rationale for choosing transfusion versus erythropoietin/iron therapy alone — this is a substantial gap given how central it is to the treatment plan.
  4c. Images referenced in the post (ECG/labs/other reports) are not transcribed as text and their specific values are therefore not available in the extracted source: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: The blog references investigation images without accompanying transcribed values (e.g., presumed renal function panel, complete blood count, chest X-ray), meaning several of the most important objective data points for a CKD case (creatinine trend, potassium, hemoglobin) are not verifiable from the text alone.
  Unused/unclear results: The specific antibiotics started on Day 4 (Augmentin, Levofloxacin) imply a presumed infective trigger (e.g., pneumonia or a respiratory/urinary infection) for the deterioration, but no explicit infective workup (chest X-ray findings, sputum/urine culture, white cell count, procalcitonin) or stated rationale for starting antibiotics is given in the text — a decision point generated (antibiotics started) without its triggering evidence being shown.
  Missing investigations: No documented serum creatinine/eGFR values despite the primary diagnosis being CKD; no potassium level despite the relevance of hyperkalemia risk in a dialysis patient; no chest imaging findings described in text form; no parathyroid hormone or calcium/phosphate panel despite Bio D3 (vitamin D analog) being part of the treatment plan, which would normally be guided by such values.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Chronic Kidney Disease on Maintenance Hemodialysis (MHD) with Hypertension.
  At what point reached: The provisional diagnosis is stated after the admission history and examination, prior to the day-by-day treatment course being detailed — it does not appear to be revised further across the 9 documented treatment days, aside from the empiric addition of antibiotics and nebulization from Day 3/4 onward.
  Closure type: Analytical closure, built over the preceding months (RMP visits → Suryapet ultrasound → institute referral) rather than reached acutely in this single encounter — this presentation represents an acute decompensation (uremic/fluid-overload crisis with hypertensive emergency and hypoxia) superimposed on an already-established chronic diagnosis.
  Open branches at closure: The specific trigger for this particular decompensation episode is never explicitly named — candidates left implicit include a missed/inadequate dialysis session, dietary/fluid indiscretion, an intercurrent respiratory infection (suggested by the empiric antibiotic and nebulization additions from Day 3-4), or progression of the underlying renal disease itself; none of these is explicitly selected or excluded in the documented reasoning.
  Synthesis insight: The case is a clear example of a longstanding, previously-established diagnosis (CKD with HTN) being carried forward without being re-interrogated at this admission — appropriate for a known dialysis patient, but the acute precipitant of this specific admission (why now, why this decompensation) is never made explicit, which is a gap in the reasoning chain that a learner should be prompted to fill.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 2a/2b — The original RMP anchored on "hypertension" as the explanation for giddiness/fever and later for edema/facial puffiness/abdominal distension over a period of roughly 6 years, repeatedly attributing progressively more renal-specific symptoms to the same initial diagnosis rather than re-evaluating — a clear and clinically significant anchoring failure that delayed CKD diagnosis by an extended period.
  Availability: Node 2e — the absence of diabetes (the most common and readily "available" cause of CKD) may make it harder for a clinician to quickly settle on an etiology, and the case documentation does not show an explicit search for the actual cause (e.g., longstanding hypertensive nephrosclerosis vs. a primary glomerular disease), possibly because "CKD with HTN" was accepted as a sufficient label without further etiologic workup.
  Premature closure: Node 5 — the working diagnosis of "CKD on MHD with HTN" is carried through the entire admission without an explicit statement of the acute decompensation's trigger, which is a form of closure on the chronic label without fully working through the acute presentation's specific cause.
  Framing: Node 1 — the blog's own title ("62 year old female with CKD on MHD") frames the reader immediately with the diagnosis already established, consistent with how this case is presented as a known, longitudinally-followed dialysis patient rather than a new diagnostic puzzle.
  Search satisficing/Commission bias: Node 4c — antibiotics (Augmentin, Levofloxacin) and nebulization were added from Day 3-4 without documented supporting evidence (culture results, imaging findings) in the extracted text, which could reflect either appropriate empiric treatment based on unlisted clinical findings, or a satisficing pattern of treating a plausible infective contributor without confirming it — the source text does not allow this to be distinguished.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2a/2b — the repeated, multi-year misattribution of increasingly renal-specific symptoms (giddiness/fever, then edema/facial puffiness/abdominal distension) to "hypertension" by the treating RMP, without renal function testing.
  Why: This is the single point in the case with the greatest transferable teaching value: whenever new or progressive symptoms (especially edema, puffiness, or distension) arise in a patient with an existing diagnosis, the reasoning pathway should explicitly re-test the working diagnosis against the new findings rather than folding them into the pre-existing label. Earlier recognition here — as much as five to six years earlier — could have identified this patient's CKD at a much earlier, potentially more modifiable stage, changing the entire subsequent disease trajectory.
