DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case_016 (22F, blurring of vision → found to have malignant
hypertension and markedly elevated renal function, now CKD on maintenance hemodialysis)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 22F student (MPC graduate, preparing for physics PG entrance exam), heavy menstrual
  bleeding 5 days before her exam (treated by RMP), followed the next day by non-progressive
  blurring of vision present throughout the day, eventually traced over ~2-3 weeks to malignant
  hypertension (BP 220/100) and markedly elevated urea/creatinine (~16, units not specified),
  now on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD)
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Missed (retrospectively identifiable)
  Insight: In a 22-year-old presenting with new visual symptoms, "blurring of vision" should
  trigger fundoscopic examination and blood pressure measurement as a near-reflexive first step
  given how effectively and cheaply these can identify hypertensive retinopathy — the actual
  diagnostic path took multiple ophthalmology visits across two cities before a BP reading was
  finally obtained, representing a significant delay in reaching an easily obtainable, high-yield
  data point (this is a textbook "missed" decision point — recognized only in retrospect from the
  case's own timeline).

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Heavy menstrual bleeding 5 days before the exam, treated by RMP with unspecified medication,
      symptoms relieved: Decision Point: Missed
      Insight: Heavy menstrual bleeding immediately preceding new-onset visual symptoms and
      (eventually discovered) severe hypertension and renal failure raises the question of
      whether this was itself an early manifestation of the same underlying process (e.g.,
      uremic platelet dysfunction or a coagulation abnormality secondary to advancing renal
      failure) rather than an unrelated gynecological event — this causal link is never
      explicitly explored in the text.
  2b. Grandmother's death delaying ophthalmology follow-up by 2-3 days: Decision Point: No
      Insight: A documented but non-clinical (psychosocial/logistical) reason for care delay;
      relevant to the case timeline but not itself a reasoning decision point.
  2c. First ophthalmologist (unspecified location) found "swelling of veins in the eye, water
      accumulation in the eye, and anemia," and recommended a specialist in Hyderabad — but the
      family instead went to a different city (Guntur) due to having a known family doctor there:
      Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: The first ophthalmologist's findings — venous engorgement/swelling and fluid
      accumulation in the eye — are textbook descriptions of papilledema and retinal edema,
      classic findings of malignant/hypertensive retinopathy, and this was the earliest point in
      the documented timeline where the underlying diagnosis could plausibly have been suspected;
      the decision to seek care via a personal/logistical connection (family doctor in a
      different city) rather than the recommended referral pathway is a system-level decision
      point that measurably delayed diagnosis, though this reflects patient/family circumstance
      rather than a clinical reasoning failure per se.
  2d. Second ophthalmologist (Guntur) unable to interpret the eye findings, but incidentally
      checked BP 2-3 times and found it consistently ~220/100: Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: This is the actual turning-point decision in the case — a clinician who could not
      characterize the retinal findings nonetheless took the sensible step of checking vital
      signs, and this simple action (not a specialized test) is what finally uncovered the
      life-threatening hypertension; this underscores that basic vital sign measurement can be
      more diagnostically decisive than specialist-level pattern recognition when the specialist
      is looking at an unfamiliar picture.
  2e. No H/o similar complaints in the past; no known DM/HTN/CVA/CAD/TB/asthma; regular menstrual
      cycles otherwise (3/28, no clots): Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: Absence of any prior diagnosed hypertension confirms this is either truly new-onset
      or previously undetected/asymptomatic hypertension that had already caused substantial
      silent renal damage by the time of this presentation — consistent with how CKD from
      longstanding unrecognized hypertension often first declares itself via an acute
      hypertensive-emergency complication (here, hypertensive retinopathy).
  Single most important history: 2d (the incidental BP check by the second ophthalmologist) —
  this single vital sign measurement, not any imaging or lab test, was the actual decision point
  that unlocked the correct diagnosis.
  Missing history: No documented family history of renal or hypertensive disease; no prior BP
  readings on record from any earlier routine visit (e.g., during the menorrhagia consultation)
  that might establish how long the hypertension had been present; no detail on the RMP medication
  given for menorrhagia (relevant to excluding a drug-related contribution to either the bleeding
  or the subsequent picture).

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Pallor present; no icterus/clubbing/cyanosis/lymphadenopathy/pedal edema:
      Decision Point: Bifurcating
      Insight: Pallor without pedal edema is consistent with the anemia of chronic kidney disease
      (reduced erythropoietin) at a stage before significant fluid overload became clinically
      apparent — this pattern is consistent with, though not specific to, an advanced but not yet
      grossly decompensated CKD picture at the time of examination.
  System 1 nodes: Once BP 220/100 and urea/creatinine ~16 were known, the pattern (severe
  hypertension + markedly elevated renal function + hypertensive retinopathy) would very quickly
  and correctly pattern-match to malignant hypertension causing (or caused by) renal failure —
  this rapid recognition, once the key data existed, is appropriate System 1 reasoning.
  System 2 nodes: The deliberate analytical work in this case happened at the presentation-seeking
  stage, not at the clinical-reasoning stage — i.e., the "hard part" of this case was obtaining
  the right measurement (BP) rather than interpreting it once obtained, which is somewhat unusual
  and worth flagging as a distinct category of diagnostic delay (access/measurement delay vs.
  reasoning delay).
  Unfollowed finding: The case doesn't specify whether a formal fundoscopic exam (documenting
  papilledema/hemorrhages/exudates — grade of hypertensive retinopathy) was performed at KIMS on
  this admission to correlate with and confirm the two outside ophthalmologists' impressions.

  Missing investigations (as documented in text): No stated formal fundoscopic grading, no stated

  renal failure or vice versa fundamentally changes patient counseling and future management.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: CKD on maintenance hemodialysis (title diagnosis), with malignant
  hypertension and hypertensive retinopathy as the presenting/discovering features
  Point of closure: Reached pragmatically at the point of severe BP + severely elevated renal
  function, prompting emergency dialysis initiation — the case moves directly from "diagnosis
  discovered" to "treatment started" without an explicit intermediate synthesis step being
  narrated.
  Classification of leap: Forced closure — the severity of the findings (malignant hypertension,
  markedly elevated creatinine) necessitated immediate action (central line, dialysis) rather
  than allowing time for a more complete etiological workup before treatment began; this is
  clinically appropriate given the acuity, but it means the underlying question of primary cause
  (chronic glomerulonephritis presenting late vs. primary/essential hypertension causing
  nephrosclerosis vs. another underlying renal disease) was never definitively closed in the
  visible record — the patient is left with a descriptive diagnosis (CKD on MHD) rather than an
  etiological one.
  Open branches at closure: The primary etiology of this patient's renal failure — whether a
  primary glomerular disease, a secondary hypertensive nephrosclerosis, or another cause — remains
  unresolved in this text. Given the patient's young age (22), this etiological question has
  substantial implications (recurrence risk in a future transplant, genetic/familial screening
  considerations, disease-specific management) that a purely symptomatic/dialysis-focused pathway
  does not address.
  Synthesis insight: This case's most important lesson is procedural rather than pathophysiological
  — the diagnostic delay here was driven by care-access decisions (choosing a familiar doctor over
  a recommended specialist referral) and by relying on specialist pattern-recognition (ophthalmology)
  in a domain where a basic vital sign (BP) was actually the decisive test, rather than by any
  failure of clinical reasoning once the right data was available.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 2c — The first ophthalmologist's uncertain interpretation ("swelling of veins…
  water accumulation… anemia") without an immediate BP check suggests the specialist may have
  anchored within their own domain (ophthalmological pathology) rather than considering a systemic
  cause producing eye findings.
  Availability: Not clearly present in this text.
  Premature closure: Not present — if anything, the opposite problem (delayed closure) is the
  issue in this case, not premature closure.
  Framing: Node 2c — The family's choice to seek care via a known personal connection (family
  doctor in Guntur) rather than following the recommended specialist referral (Hyderabad) is a
  patient/family-level framing decision that shaped and delayed the diagnostic pathway — worth
  naming as a system-level bias-adjacent factor even though it isn't a clinician cognitive bias
  per se.
  Search satisficing: Node 2c — The first ophthalmologist, upon recognizing findings outside their
  interpretive comfort zone, referred appropriately (a good practice) but did not personally check
  a vital sign that would have been immediately informative; this may reflect a scope-of-practice/
  role-based satisficing ("this is outside my domain, refer onward") rather than pursuing an easy
  cross-disciplinary check within their own visit.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2c/2d (the ophthalmology visits preceding the incidental BP discovery)
  Why: This case's highest-yield lesson is not about differential diagnosis reasoning but about
  the value of basic vital sign measurement at any point of contact with the healthcare system,
  regardless of specialty. A 22-year-old with new visual symptoms was seen by two ophthalmologists
  before a blood pressure reading was obtained; the finding that ultimately explained everything
  (BP 220/100) required no specialized equipment or test. The transferable, generalizable
  principle: "When any specialist encounters unexplained findings outside their expected pattern
  library, checking basic vital signs before referring onward can shortcut a diagnostic delay of
  days to weeks" — a low-cost, high-yield habit that this case shows was nearly skipped entirely.
