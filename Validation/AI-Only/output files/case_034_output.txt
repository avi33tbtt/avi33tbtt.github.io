DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 34: 15F with easy fatiguability and giddiness x5-6 months, iron deficiency anemia
Date: 2026-08-17
Learner: N/A
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Easy fatiguability x5-6 months with giddiness on exertion (lasting 2-3 min, relieved by rest); generalized weakness present.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: Subacute, exertion-related fatigue and giddiness in an adolescent female should immediately activate anemia as the leading pattern-library entry — particularly nutritional (iron-deficiency) anemia given the age and sex — but the presenting complaint alone should also prompt active screening for a menstrual or other chronic blood-loss source before the differential is allowed to narrow further.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Fever 10 days back, low-grade, lasted 2-3 days, resolved with medication: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: A brief, self-limited febrile illness distinct in time from the 5-6 month fatigue history is unlikely to be the primary driver of the chronic symptom complex, appropriately narrowing away an acute infective process as the main explanation.
  2b. No pedal edema, no SOB, no bleeding per rectum, no dark-coloured stools, no altered bowel habits, no weight loss: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: This systematic negative review lowers the probability of a cardiac contributor (no edema/SOB despite significant anemia symptoms) and of an occult GI bleeding source (no melena/hematochezia) or a malignancy-associated weight-loss pattern, helping to keep the differential narrowed toward a nutritional or gynecological source of blood/iron loss rather than a GI or systemic disease process.
  2c. History of blood transfusion 6 years ago "due to less Hb": Decision Point: Missed — Insight: A prior transfusion for low hemoglobin at age ~9 is a highly significant historical clue suggesting this may be a chronic or recurrent anemia rather than a new problem — the case does not explore what the cause of that earlier anemia was, whether it was ever formally worked up, or whether it represents the same underlying process (e.g., chronic dietary insufficiency, or an unrecognized bleeding tendency) recurring now.
  2d. Dietary history: mixed diet, good appetite, eats fruits, 3 meals/day, no pica: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: A reportedly adequate mixed diet with no pica somewhat weakens a pure dietary-insufficiency explanation for iron deficiency, and should prompt more active consideration of ongoing blood loss (most likely menstrual, given the history below) as the dominant driver rather than diet alone — though self-reported diet histories in adolescents can under-capture actual iron intake and bioavailability (as the source's own discussion section notes, non-heme iron absorption is low and diet-dependent).
  2e. Menstrual history: menarche at 12, initially regular (5/30, 2 pads/day, no clots, no dysmenorrhea), but irregular cycles since 1 year with bleeding for 5 days (no clots, no menorrhagia stated): Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: The onset of menstrual irregularity roughly coinciding with the timeframe of worsening fatigue is the single most important historical clue in this case — irregular, prolonged, or heavier-than-usual menstrual bleeding is a very plausible ongoing iron-loss mechanism in an adolescent, and this decision point deserved more explicit quantification (e.g., total bleeding days per cycle, pad-soaking frequency during the irregular episodes) than the case documents.
  Missing history: No explicit quantification of blood loss volume during the irregular cycles (number of pads/day during those episodes, presence of flooding); no family history of anemia or bleeding disorders; no history of parasitic infection or GI symptoms (relevant given hookworm as a common cause of iron-deficiency anemia in some settings); no clarification of whether the prior anemia at age 9 was ever formally diagnosed as iron-deficiency or another type.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Pallor ++, koilonychia +: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Pallor combined with koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails) is a classic, fairly specific combination pointing toward chronic iron-deficiency anemia specifically (rather than anemia of another type), since koilonychia is a relatively iron-deficiency-specific sign rather than a generic anemia finding.
  3b. Vitals stable (afebrile, BP 110/70, PR 84, RR 18); no other stigmata (implied by absence of documentation) of chronic disease, bleeding, or malignancy: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Normal vitals and the absence of other described abnormal findings support a chronic, compensated anemia rather than an acute or hemodynamically significant process, consistent with the long symptom duration.
  3c. CVS S1S2 normal, abdomen soft/non-tender with bowel sounds present, CNS unremarkable: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Normal cardiac, abdominal, and neurological findings further argue against a structural cardiac cause for the giddiness (e.g., high-output failure from severe uncompensated anemia would more likely show tachycardia or a flow murmur), and the unremarkable abdominal exam does not suggest an organomegaly-associated hematologic process (e.g., hemolytic anemia with splenomegaly).
  System 1 nodes: pallor + koilonychia + adolescent female → iron-deficiency anemia (rapid, well-supported pattern match).
  System 2 nodes: reconciling the "good appetite, mixed diet, no pica" history with an iron-deficiency diagnosis required analytical reasoning about blood-loss (particularly menstrual) contribution to iron balance — this reasoning is implicit in the case (menstrual history was taken and is relevant) but never explicitly stated as the etiologic explanation in the diagnosis or discussion.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Hemogram/peripheral smear referenced via images (not transcribed as text) leading to the stated diagnosis of "Microcytic hypochromic anemia": Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: A microcytic hypochromic picture is consistent with iron-deficiency anemia but is not itself diagnostic — it is also seen in thalassemia trait/disease and anemia of chronic disease — and the case does not document the specific red-cell indices (MCV, MCH, RDW) or a peripheral smear description that would help distinguish these possibilities from simple iron deficiency.
  4b. No iron studies (serum ferritin, serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation) documented in the extracted text despite the diagnosis being labeled specifically "iron deficiency anemia": Decision Point: Missed — Insight: A definitive iron-deficiency-anemia diagnosis is normally confirmed by a low ferritin (or low serum iron with high TIBC), not by red-cell morphology alone; without these values documented, the diagnosis rests on the combination of microcytic hypochromic indices, koilonychia, and clinical context, which is a reasonable presumptive approach but leaves thalassemia trait formally unexcluded.
  4c. No hemoglobin electrophoresis or family/ethnic-background-based thalassemia screening documented: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: Given that this patient had a prior transfusion at age 9 for low hemoglobin, and is now again anemic with a microcytic hypochromic picture, a hemoglobinopathy (thalassemia trait, or even thalassemia intermedia going unrecognized) is a real alternative or contributing explanation that the case does not appear to have actively investigated or excluded.
  Unused results: The specific hemoglobin value and red cell indices are referenced only via embedded images, not transcribed as text, so their exact values are not verifiable from the source as provided.
  Missing investigations: Serum ferritin/iron studies; hemoglobin electrophoresis (especially relevant given the recurrent-anemia history); stool examination for occult blood or ova/parasites (relevant given India-specific epidemiology of hookworm-related iron-deficiency anemia, which the case's own cited discussion references indirectly); a menstrual-blood-loss quantification tool or gynecologic referral given the irregular cycles.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Microcytic hypochromic anemia — Iron Deficiency Anemia.
  At what point reached: After history, examination, and the (image-only, untranscribed) hemogram/smear results, without documented iron-study confirmation.
  Closure type: Pattern-recognition closure (System 1) — the combination of adolescent female, pallor, koilonychia, and a microcytic hypochromic blood picture is strongly suggestive of iron-deficiency anemia and this is a clinically reasonable working diagnosis, but it is reached without the confirmatory iron studies that would fully validate it over the main alternative (thalassemia trait), making this a case of appropriately-directed but not fully closed analytical reasoning.
  Alternatives open at closure: Thalassemia trait/minor (not excluded — and arguably raised in probability by the recurrent-anemia/prior-transfusion history); ongoing menstrual blood loss as the specific mechanism of iron deficiency (plausible and clinically important, but never explicitly named as the presumed cause in the diagnosis line); intestinal parasitic infection as a contributing cause (not investigated).
  Synthesis insight: The single-drug treatment plan (oral iron replacement alone) is the correct first step for either simple dietary iron deficiency or menstrual-loss-driven iron deficiency, and will also serve a partial diagnostic function (as the case's own cited discussion notes: response to iron replacement is one of the two defining criteria for a nutritional anemia) — but the case does not explicitly plan a follow-up hemoglobin/reticulocyte check to confirm treatment response, which would be the natural next diagnostic step to retrospectively confirm iron deficiency over a hemoglobinopathy.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 3a — Pallor and koilonychia likely anchored the team quickly and appropriately to iron-deficiency anemia; this anchoring appears clinically justified given the specificity of koilonychia for this diagnosis, though it may have reduced the perceived need to pursue iron studies or hemoglobin electrophoresis before finalizing the label.
  Availability: Node 1 — iron-deficiency anemia is the most common and readily "available" cause of anemia in an adolescent female in this population, which is statistically reasonable but may have made a less common but relevant alternative (thalassemia trait, especially given the recurrent-anemia history) less likely to be actively considered.
  Premature closure: Node 4b/4c — the diagnosis was finalized without the standard confirmatory iron-study workup or exclusion of hemoglobinopathy, representing a mild premature closure given the recurrent nature of this patient's anemia (prior transfusion 6 years ago).
  Framing: Node 1 — the blog's title itself pre-frames the case around fatigue/giddiness without disclosing the eventual diagnosis, which is actually a relatively unbiased framing compared to many of the other cases in this corpus (which often reveal the diagnosis in the title).
  Search satisficing: Node 4a — once a microcytic hypochromic smear was obtained and matched the expected pattern for iron deficiency, further workup (iron studies, hemoglobin electrophoresis) appears to have stopped, consistent with search satisficing once a plausible-fitting answer was found.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2c/4c — the history of a prior blood transfusion for low hemoglobin 6 years ago, and its implications for the current diagnostic workup.
  Why: This is the single piece of history in the case most likely to change the diagnostic approach if given proper weight: a recurrent anemia history in an adolescent, especially one requiring transfusion at a young age, should prompt explicit consideration of an inherited hemoglobinopathy (thalassemia trait) alongside — not instead of — iron deficiency, since the two are not mutually exclusive and can coexist. The transferable teaching principle: whenever a patient with a "new" episode of a common diagnosis (like iron-deficiency anemia) has a history of a prior similar episode, the reasoning pathway should explicitly ask whether this is truly a new/recurrent nutritional problem or the unmasking of an underlying, previously uninvestigated condition.
