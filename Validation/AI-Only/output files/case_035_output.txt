DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 35: 32M with epigastric pain, vomiting, fever, and hypotension, chronic heavy alcohol use, provisional septic shock
Date: 2026-08-17
Learner: N/A
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Sudden-onset epigastric pain (throbbing, non-radiating), 2 episodes of vomiting (non-bilious, projectile, food content), giddiness, and fever x1 day.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: Acute epigastric pain with vomiting and fever in a heavy chronic alcohol user should immediately activate two major competing pattern-library entries — acute pancreatitis/alcoholic gastritis (a toxic-metabolic/inflammatory process) versus an intra-abdominal infective or perforation-related process leading to sepsis — and the co-occurring fever and eventual hypotension mean this node cannot be safely closed toward a benign "alcoholic gastritis" explanation without urgent further workup.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. High-grade morning fever with chills and rigor, no diurnal variation, not associated with cold/cough/SOB/headache: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Chills and rigor accompanying fever are a classic marker of bacteremia/systemic infection (as opposed to a purely viral or inflammatory low-grade fever), and this detail meaningfully raises the pretest probability of an infective source underlying the abdominal presentation, supporting the eventual "septic shock" label.
  2b. Last binge of alcohol yesterday, with a 20-year history of regular heavy drinking (half quarter to 1 bottle, 4 times/day): Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: This degree and duration of alcohol use is a major risk factor for both acute pancreatitis and alcoholic gastritis/hepatitis, and the temporal proximity of the last binge to symptom onset strongly supports an alcohol-triggered acute process as the initiating event, even if it later evolved into a septic complication.
  2c. Loss of appetite (personal history) and chronic tobacco (Kaini) use x5 years: Decision Point: Closing (partial) — Insight: These are compatible with, but not specific to, a chronic alcohol/tobacco-related GI process; they add background risk without independently narrowing the acute differential.
  2d. Not a known case of DM, HTN, CAD, TB; no chemo/radiation, blood transfusion, or prior surgery; childhood asthma with cold/food-triggered exacerbations, on inhaler: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Absence of major chronic comorbidities narrows away several alternative explanations for hypotension and altered state (e.g., diabetic ketoacidosis, cardiac ischemia), keeping the differential focused on an acute abdominal/infective-toxic process; the asthma history is noted but does not appear directly relevant to the current presentation.
  2e. Father has history of diabetes (family history): Decision Point: Missed — Insight: This is documented but not used in the visible reasoning; it has low direct relevance to this acute presentation but could matter for the patient's own future metabolic risk, particularly relevant if pancreatitis recurs or becomes chronic.
  Missing history: No explicit timeline of exactly how many hours elapsed between symptom onset, presentation to the outside MP (medical practitioner), and arrival/transfer to this facility with hypotension already present; no documented urine output history before admission; no explicit description of stool character (diarrhea, blood) that would help localize a possible infective/GI source of sepsis.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Cold extremities, feeble pulse, BP 70/40 (after IV fluids were already started at the outside facility), fluid resuscitation initiated: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Cold extremities with a feeble pulse and profound hypotension despite fluids already having been given is the single most critical finding in this case, indicating a shock state with continued hemodynamic instability — this combination is highly consistent with septic (or, less likely given the clinical context, hypovolemic/distributive from another cause) shock and is the decision point that most directly drove the "septic shock" diagnosis and aggressive treatment.
  3b. Mild dehydration; temp 99.4F, pulse 60bpm low volume, RR 12, BP 70/40, SpO2 97%, GRBS 96: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: A relatively low pulse rate (60bpm) in the setting of profound hypotension is somewhat atypical for classic septic shock (which more often produces compensatory tachycardia) and could suggest an element of vagally-mediated or medication-related bradycardia, or simply an early/blunted compensatory response — this discordance between pulse rate and blood pressure is a decision point that the case documentation does not explicitly address.
  3c. Abdomen — obese habitus, tenderness in epigastric and hypochondrial region, no palpable mass, no free fluid, spleen/liver not palpable: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Epigastric and hypochondrial tenderness without a palpable mass or clinically detectable free fluid is consistent with acute pancreatitis or gastritis/cholecystitis-type pathology, but a normal abdominal exam does not exclude early or evolving pancreatic necrosis or a retroperitoneal process, both of which can be clinically silent early on despite eventually driving sepsis-like physiology.
  3d. CNS intact, GCS 15/15, no focal deficit, no cerebellar signs (finger-nose, knee-heel intact): Decision Point: Closing — Insight: A fully intact neurological exam despite profound hypotension is reassuring and argues against a primary CNS event (e.g., stroke or intracranial hemorrhage) as the cause of the giddiness, supporting a hemodynamic (shock-related) rather than neurological explanation for that symptom.
  System 1 nodes: heavy alcohol use + epigastric pain + vomiting → alcohol-related pancreatitis/gastritis (rapid pattern match).
  System 2 nodes: reconciling the profound hypotension and cold extremities with a presumed abdominal/pancreatic source required active analytical reasoning to arrive at "septic shock" as the unifying diagnosis — this reasoning is implicit in the final diagnosis but not explicitly narrated step-by-step in the case text (e.g., no explicit statement of a lactate value, blood culture results, or a specific presumed infective source).

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Imaging referenced (initial investigation image, ultrasound report, and serial images on 21/08, 22/08, 23/08) but not transcribed as text: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: All investigation results in this case are embedded as images only, with no transcribed lab values, ultrasound findings, or serial trends in text form — this is a substantial data gap for a case where the working diagnosis (septic shock, in a patient with alcohol-related abdominal pain) would normally be anchored by specific findings such as serum lipase/amylase (for pancreatitis), white cell count and lactate (for sepsis severity), blood cultures (for source identification), and ultrasound findings (for gallstones, pancreatic changes, or free fluid).
  4b. No documented explicit source of sepsis (e.g., no stated finding of cholangitis, perforation, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, or another confirmed infective focus) despite the "septic shock" diagnosis: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: A diagnosis of septic shock implies a presumed or confirmed infective source, but the case text does not explicitly state what that source was determined to be — this is an important unresolved decision point, since the antibiotic choice (ceftriaxone plus metronidazole) suggests an intra-abdominal or biliary source was presumed, but this presumption is not made explicit in the visible reasoning.
  4c. Serial daily images (21/08, 22/08, 23/08) suggest ongoing monitoring/trending of some parameter (likely labs or vitals) but content is not accessible in text form: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: The existence of serial investigations implies the clinical team was actively tracking the patient's trajectory and response to treatment, which is good practice, but this DRM cannot assess whether the patient was improving, worsening, or plateauing without the underlying data.
  Unused/unclear results: All investigation results are effectively "unused" from this DRM's perspective, since they are referenced only as images without accompanying transcribed values.
  Missing investigations (as documented in text): Serum lipase/amylase; complete blood count and lactate; blood and other relevant cultures; liver function tests (relevant given chronic heavy alcohol use); coagulation profile (relevant given hypotension and possible evolving organ dysfunction).

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Septic shock.
  At what point reached: After presentation with hypotension unresponsive to initial fluids at the outside facility, and following transfer/admission workup at this institute — the diagnosis appears in the case as the final provisional diagnosis alongside the treatment plan, without an explicit intermediate discussion connecting the abdominal pain/vomiting/alcohol-use history to the eventual "septic shock" label.
  Closure type: Likely analytical closure driven by hemodynamic findings (severe hypotension, cold extremities, feeble pulse) meeting clinical criteria for shock, combined with fever/chills/rigor supporting a septic (rather than purely hypovolemic or cardiogenic) etiology — though the case documentation does not explicitly walk through this reasoning, making it difficult to fully distinguish analytical from pattern-recognition closure.
  Open branches at closure: Acute pancreatitis (strongly suggested by the alcohol history, epigastric pain, and hypochondrial tenderness) is never explicitly named as a diagnosis or explicitly ruled in/out despite being the most likely alcohol-related initiating process; the specific infective source for "septic shock" is never named; alcoholic gastritis/hepatitis as an alternative or contributing process is not explicitly discussed.
  Synthesis insight: The treatment plan (broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gram-negative and anaerobic organisms, aggressive fluid resuscitation, proton-pump inhibitor, antiemetic, thiamine/vitamin supplementation) is a reasonable and fairly standard approach for a hypotensive patient with a presumed intra-abdominal infective source in a chronic alcoholic — but the case never explicitly closes the loop on whether pancreatitis was confirmed/excluded as either the trigger for or a separate process alongside the septic shock, which is the single largest gap in the visible reasoning chain.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 1 — the alcohol history and epigastric pain may have anchored the initial outside-facility assessment toward a gastritis/pancreatitis frame, potentially contributing to a delay in recognizing the severity of the hypotension (BP 70/40) as representing shock requiring more urgent, source-directed management.
  Availability: Node 5 — "septic shock" may have become the working diagnosis in part because it is a well-recognized, readily "available" clinical syndrome once profound hypotension with fever is observed, potentially at the expense of a more specific, mechanistically grounded diagnosis (e.g., septic complication of confirmed necrotizing pancreatitis) that would better guide source control.
  Premature closure: Node 4b — the diagnosis of "septic shock" was applied without an explicit documented source, which risks treating the syndrome (shock) without having fully closed the loop on its underlying cause — a form of premature closure at the etiologic (rather than syndromic) level.
  Framing: Node 1 — this case's title and framing ("General Medicine case") do not pre-bias the reader toward a specific diagnosis, unlike several other cases in this corpus whose titles reveal the diagnosis outright — a relatively unbiased documentation style here.
  Search satisficing: Node 4 — once the syndrome-level diagnosis of "septic shock" was reached and matched the hemodynamic picture, the search for the specific underlying source (pancreatitis vs. cholangitis vs. another intra-abdominal process) does not appear to have been pursued to explicit closure in the visible text, consistent with search satisficing once a broadly-fitting syndrome label was found.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3c/4b — the epigastric/hypochondrial tenderness in a heavy chronic alcohol user, and the unresolved question of whether acute pancreatitis was the specific trigger for this septic-shock presentation.
  Why: This is the single point in the case with the greatest transferable teaching value: "septic shock" is a syndromic diagnosis, not an etiology, and in a patient with this alcohol history and this examination pattern, the reasoning pathway should have explicitly worked through acute pancreatitis (with or without infected necrosis) as the leading candidate source before or alongside naming septic shock as the final diagnosis. The general principle: whenever a syndromic diagnosis (shock, sepsis, delirium, etc.) is reached, the reasoning map should be pushed one level deeper to identify — or explicitly state the search for — the specific anatomic or microbiologic source, since source control (not just supportive shock management) is often what determines outcome.
