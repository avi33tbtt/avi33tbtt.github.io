DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case_018 (54M, chest pain + SOB + productive cough x10d, with
constitutional symptoms x15-20d, provisional dx pneumonia ?secondary to pulmonary TB)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 54M, left-sided pricking chest pain x10d (sudden onset, non-radiating, exertional),
  SOB Grade 3 x10d, cough with yellow non-blood-stained non-foul-smelling expectoration x10d,
  fever x10d (high-grade, intermittent, with chills/rigors and body aches)
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: A 10-day history of pleuritic chest pain, significant dyspnea (Grade 3), and purulent
  cough with fever is a classic community-acquired pneumonia semantic pattern — but the
  additional history of weight loss and evening temperature rise over a longer window (15-20
  days, i.e., predating the acute 10-day illness) is the critical qualifier that should split the
  differential early between straightforward bacterial pneumonia and a pneumonia occurring on a
  background of a more chronic, constitutional-symptom-driving process (classically pulmonary
  TB) — this longer constitutional timeline embedded within the HPI is easy to under-weight if
  the 10-day acute complaint is read as the whole story.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Weight loss and evening rise of temperature since 15-20 days (i.e., predating and
      outlasting the 10-day acute complaint): Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: This is the single most diagnostically important item in the entire history — the
      classic triad-adjacent pattern of evening pyrexia and weight loss, present for longer than
      the acute presenting complaint, is a strong independent pointer toward tuberculosis as an
      underlying process rather than a purely acute bacterial pneumonia, and appropriately appears
      to have driven the "?secondary to Koch's" provisional diagnosis.
  2b. No known DM/HTN/CVA/asthma/epilepsy/TB (i.e., no PRIOR diagnosed TB): Decision Point:
      Closing (partial)
      Insight: Absence of a prior TB diagnosis doesn't exclude currently active, previously
      undiagnosed TB — this is a "no known case of" limitation (reflects prior diagnosis status,
      not actual screening), and given the constitutional symptom pattern in 2a, this history item
      should not be read as meaningfully lowering TB suspicion.
  2c. No palpitations, PND, burning micturition, loose stools, decreased urine output:
      Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: These negatives reduce concern for a cardiac (PND) or urinary source contributing
      to the SOB/systemic symptoms, keeping the differential appropriately centered on the
      respiratory system.
  Single most important history: 2a — the 15-20 day constitutional symptom window, longer than
  the acute complaint itself, is what should (and appears to) drive suspicion toward a
  TB-associated process rather than simple bacterial pneumonia.
  Missing history: No documented TB contact history (household or occupational exposure), no
  documented prior chest X-ray or PPD/IGRA testing, no smoking history, no occupational exposure
  history (e.g., silica, biomass fuel) that would be relevant risk-factor information for both TB
  and other chronic lung processes.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. No pallor/icterus/cyanosis/clubbing/lymphadenopathy/koilonychia/generalized edema:
      Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: Absence of clubbing and lymphadenopathy somewhat reduces (though doesn't exclude)
      concern for a more chronic/advanced process such as bronchiectasis or malignancy; these are
      appropriately unremarkable negatives for a 10-20 day illness.
  3b. Left suprascapular findings: decreased vocal fremitus, resonant percussion (not dull),
      reduced breath sounds, no added sounds: Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: This examination pattern — decreased fremitus and reduced breath sounds but
      PRESERVED resonance (not dull) — is an important and somewhat atypical combination. Classic
      lobar consolidation (bacterial pneumonia) typically produces increased fremitus and dull
      percussion over the consolidated area; here, fremitus is decreased and percussion remains
      resonant, a pattern more suggestive of early/patchy disease, pleural thickening, or
      cavitary change (as can occur with upper-lobe TB) rather than dense lobar consolidation —
      this exam pattern itself provides supportive evidence for the TB-associated hypothesis and
      is a finding that deserved more explicit discussion in the write-up than it received.
  3c. Trachea central, chest expansion 4cm, no local rise of temperature or tenderness:
      Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: Central trachea and preserved chest expansion argue against a large pleural
      effusion or significant volume loss, consistent with a localized upper-lobe process rather
      than extensive lung involvement.
  System 1 nodes: Left-sided chest findings + productive cough + fever rapidly pattern-matched to
  "pneumonia" as the presenting syndrome.
  System 2 nodes: Integrating the atypical exam pattern (decreased fremitus + resonant, not dull,
  percussion — 3b) with the constitutional symptom timeline (2a) to arrive at "pneumonia secondary
