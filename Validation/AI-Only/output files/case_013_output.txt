DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case_013 (31M water supplier, acute L UL+LL numbness/weakness)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 31M, sudden-onset left upper and lower limb numbness (7pm) followed within 5 minutes
  by left lower limb weakness progressing to left upper limb weakness, while driving a bike;
  transient dysarthria (~15 min, resolved) during the event
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: Sudden-onset unilateral (left-sided) numbness and weakness with transient dysarthria
  in a 31-year-old is an immediate stroke-alarm presentation regardless of age — the semantic
  qualifier set (sudden onset, focal, unilateral, progressive over minutes, with speech
  involvement) should activate "acute stroke" in an experienced clinician's pattern library
  before any risk-factor history is even taken; age alone should not lower suspicion given how
  clearly focal and abrupt this presentation is.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. No headache, fever, ear discharge, visual symptoms, head injury, involuntary movements:
      Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: Absence of headache/fever reduces likelihood of an infective or hemorrhagic
      process with raised ICP features, and absence of preceding trauma reduces likelihood of a
      traumatic vascular dissection as an obvious trigger, though it doesn't exclude dissection
      outright.
  2b. Family history of DM and HTN x10 years (patient himself not a known case of either):
      Decision Point: Missed
      Insight: A strong family history of DM/HTN in a 31-year-old presenting with stroke-like
      symptoms should prompt active screening for undiagnosed metabolic disease in the patient
      himself (the exam later reveals BMI 41.2 and acanthosis nigricans — see 3a — which the
      history alone should have flagged as high pre-test probability for this before exam even
      began).
  2c. Alcohol 500ml alternate days x18 years, smoking 4-5 cigarettes/day x18 years:
      Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: Two decades of regular alcohol and tobacco use in a 31-year-old constitutes
      substantial premature cardiovascular risk loading, materially raising pre-test probability
      for an ischemic vascular event even before considering metabolic syndrome features.
  Single most important history: 2c (alcohol + smoking since age 13, i.e., 18 years by age 31) —
  most should have anchored the differential toward a vascular/atherosclerotic mechanism from the
  outset, given how unusual it is for a stroke syndrome this age without such risk loading.
  Missing history: No explicit prior lipid profile or glucose testing history; no documentation
  of whether the patient had ever been screened for diabetes/dyslipidemia despite strong family
  history and current obesity; no clear account of exact symptom timeline duration before
  hospital arrival (private hospital first, then transferred) that would affect thrombolysis
  eligibility window discussion — this is a significant omission for an acute stroke case.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Height 167cm, weight 115kg, BMI 41.2 (Class III obesity), truncal obesity, acanthosis
      nigricans, "hesitation cuts" (presumably self-harm scars, though not addressed in text):
      Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: Acanthosis nigricans plus severe truncal obesity is a strong clinical marker of
      insulin resistance, retrospectively validating the family-history-driven suspicion from 2b
      — this patient very likely has undiagnosed metabolic syndrome/pre-diabetes, a major stroke
      risk factor, discovered on exam rather than screened for proactively.
  3b. Motor exam — left-sided weakness graded 4-/5 across shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee
      (pyramidal/diffuse pattern), right side full power 5/5; left-sided hypotonia; reflexes:
      biceps/triceps/supinator/knee/ankle all recorded as "-" bilaterally, plantar flexor
      bilaterally: Decision Point: Bifurcating
      Insight: The combination of left hypotonia with reduced power but preserved cranial nerve
      function and a flexor (not extensor/Babinski) plantar response is somewhat atypical for a
      classic acute upper motor neuron stroke picture (which more often shows hypertonia/
      hyperreflexia evolving over time, or an initially flaccid "spinal shock"-like phase that
      resolves) — this exam pattern is consistent with a very early post-ictal/acute stroke phase
      before spasticity develops, but the DRM should flag this as a point requiring explicit
      reasoning rather than an automatic assumption, since diffusely absent reflexes bilaterally
      (including on the unaffected right side) is not classically explained by a unilateral basal
      ganglia infarct alone.
  3c. Cerebellar exam normal, sensory exam normal, higher mental functions normal, cranial nerves
      intact: Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: Normal cerebellar and sensory exam alongside a pure motor deficit pattern narrows
      the localization toward a pure motor hemiparesis syndrome, classically associated with a
      lacunar infarct involving the posterior limb of the internal capsule or adjacent basal
      ganglia structures — consistent with the eventual caudate/lentiform nucleus localization.
  System 1 nodes: Sudden focal left-sided weakness in the context of transient dysarthria rapidly
  pattern-matched to acute stroke.
  System 2 nodes: Reconciling the bilaterally absent deep tendon reflexes (3b) against a
  unilateral basal ganglia lesion — a finding that doesn't cleanly fit the localization and
  deserved explicit discussion that the text doesn't provide.
  Unfollowed finding: "Hesitation cuts" noted on exam (a term suggesting self-harm scarring) are
  documented but never mentioned again, explained, or explored in the history — this is a
  significant unfollowed finding with potential relevance to the patient's psychosocial history
  that the case simply leaves hanging.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Investigation report images (labeled 0-7, likely bloods/ECG/CT-MRI): Decision Point:
      Cannot classify — Insight: Values not extractable from text; presumably include glucose,
      lipid profile, and the initial imaging that led to the final localization, but cannot be
      verified here.
  4b. Final diagnosis states: acute infarct in body of right caudate nucleus and right lentiform
      nucleus (stated in prose): Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: A right-sided basal ganglia infarct correctly correlates with the observed
      left-sided weakness (contralateral motor pathway involvement), and anatomically fits the
      pure-motor, sensory-sparing pattern seen on exam (3c) — the localization is internally
      consistent with the clinical picture, which is reassuring, though the actual imaging report
      values/sequences are not visible in text to independently verify.
  Unused/unverifiable results: Presumed glucose/HbA1c and lipid panel (highly relevant given 3a)
  are not stated as values in text despite the "trunkal obesity" being explicitly named
  alongside the stroke diagnosis in the provisional diagnosis line.
  Missing investigations (as documented in text): No stated carotid Doppler or echocardiogram
  result to assess embolic source, despite the patient's age making a search for an embolic or
  hypercoagulable etiology (rather than assuming pure atherosclerotic small-vessel disease)
  clinically important in a 31-year-old.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Acute ischemic stroke with acute infarct in body of right caudate
  nucleus and right lentiform nucleus (provisional diagnosis also explicitly named "trunkal
  obesity" as a co-diagnosis)
  Point of closure: After imaging (implied — the localization is stated as the final diagnosis
  following the investigation section)
  Classification of leap: Analytical closure (System 2) — the pure motor, contralateral,
  sensory-sparing pattern on exam correctly predicted a basal ganglia lacunar-type localization,
  and the final imaging is stated to correlate with that prediction; this is a well-reasoned
  closure as far as anatomical localization goes.
  Open branches at closure: The etiological question — WHY a 31-year-old had this stroke — is
  not closed. The provisional diagnosis names "trunkal obesity" alongside the stroke, correctly
  flagging metabolic risk, but there is no stated glucose/HbA1c/lipid confirmation, no carotid or
  cardioembolic workup, and no explicit statement connecting the risk factors to a mechanism
  (small-vessel lacunar disease from chronic hypertension/diabetes vs. large-vessel
  atherosclerosis vs. cardioembolic vs. hypercoagulable state) — for a stroke this young, that
  etiological branch should be explicitly closed, not left implicit.
  Synthesis insight: The anatomical/localization reasoning is strong and well-supported by the
  exam, but the etiological reasoning — the part most relevant to secondary prevention and most
  educationally important given the patient's young age — is the part left unclosed in this
  write-up.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 1 — The dramatic, unambiguous stroke presentation likely anchored the entire
  team correctly and quickly toward "acute ischemic stroke" — appropriately, given the acuity —
  but this may have left less deliberate attention for the underlying "why," since the acute
  syndrome so clearly explained the presenting complaint.
  Availability: Not clearly present in this text.
  Premature closure: Node 5 (etiology) — The etiological workup for a young stroke patient
  appears incomplete in the text (no stated hypercoagulable/cardioembolic screen), which risks
  premature closure on "risk-factor-related atherosclerotic stroke" without confirming that
  mechanism or excluding alternatives appropriate for this age group.
  Framing: Node 1 — The referral framing ("water supplier by occupation... while driving bike")
  centers the dramatic functional incident, which appropriately drove urgent triage, but may have
  deprioritized the metabolic-risk-factor workup relative to the acute neurological workup.
  Search satisficing: Node 4 — Once the localization (caudate/lentiform infarct) explained the
  exam findings, the search for the underlying cause (young-stroke workup) does not appear to
  have continued in the visible text.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 5 (etiological workup for a young stroke patient)
  Why: The single most transferable lesson from this case is that in stroke under age 45,
  localization is necessary but not sufficient — the etiological search (metabolic screen,
  cardioembolic source, hypercoagulable workup) is its own decision point that must be explicitly
  closed, not assumed from risk-factor presence alone. This patient has strong pointers (obesity,
  acanthosis nigricans, family history, smoking, alcohol) toward a metabolic/atherosclerotic
  mechanism, but pointers are not the same as a confirmed mechanism, and the case as written
  doesn't show that confirmation happening.
