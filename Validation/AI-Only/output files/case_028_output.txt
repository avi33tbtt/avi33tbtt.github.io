DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — 65-year-old man with chronic backache, prior undiagnosed neurological decline, and acute fever/cough with aspiration risk (compressive myelopathy, acute left temporal infarct, vascular dementia)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Lower backache x5 years, on a background of an unevaluated neurological event 1.5 years ago and progressive functional/cognitive decline since, now presenting acutely with 1 week of fever, productive cough with swallowed sputum, shortness of breath after coughing, decreased intake, dysphagia, and drooling.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: This case layers three distinct temporal problems that must be held separately before integration: (1) a 5-year chronic backache, (2) an 18-month-old neurological event with incompletely investigated "?infarcts" that was never followed up due to COVID lockdown, and (3) an acute 1-week febrile respiratory/swallowing illness — the acute presentation is very likely a complication (aspiration pneumonia given dysphagia/drooling/swallowed sputum) of the chronic, undertreated neurological process rather than a new unrelated illness, and the map should trace this causal chain explicitly.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Depression with low mood and excessive crying for 4 months, 2 years ago, following his mother's death: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: This is documented as a purely psychological reaction to bereavement, but given the subsequent neurological and cognitive decline, it is also worth holding open as a possible very early prodrome of the neurocognitive process (post-stroke or vascular-related mood change can precede overt motor findings), rather than assuming it was purely reactive — the text does not revisit this possibility.
  2b. Sudden difficulty rising from squatting 1.5 years ago, prompting hospital visit; no slurred speech, falls, giddiness, involuntary movements, or bowel/bladder incontinence at that time; MRI showed "?infarcts"; attenders were told "?joint disease ?clot in brain" and advised review in 15 days, which did not happen due to COVID lockdown: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This is the single most consequential missed decision point in the entire case — an abnormal MRI with a documented plan for urgent follow-up was never actioned, and the patient's function progressively declined (from walking with a stick, to difficulty rising from sitting, memory loss, and loss of expressive/conversational ability) over the subsequent 18 months without re-evaluation; this represents a real-world system-level "Missed" decision point (a follow-up plan generated but never executed) rather than a clinical reasoning error, and the map flags it as the origin of the current disability.
  2c. Progressive decline over 18 months: walking with support, difficulty rising from sitting, memory loss, inability to participate in active conversation, inability to express feelings, no sleep disturbance: Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: This progression pattern (mixed motor decline plus cognitive/expressive decline) is consistent with either an ongoing multi-infarct process (stepwise or gradually cumulative vascular injury) or a mixed vascular-plus-degenerative dementia, and the eventual differential ("vascular dementia — ?multi-infarct dementia") reflects this appropriately, though the map notes that without the 15-day follow-up MRI that was never done, the actual trajectory (single event with static deficit vs. ongoing silent infarcts) cannot be distinguished from history alone.
  2d. Acute 1-week illness: high-grade fever with chills/rigors, productive cough with swallowed (unexpectorated) sputum and associated shortness of breath after coughing, decreased food/liquid intake, difficulty swallowing, drooling of food and saliva: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: The combination of dysphagia, drooling, and inability to expectorate sputum (having to swallow it) in a patient with a pre-existing neurological deficit is a classic aspiration-risk constellation, and the fever/productive cough almost certainly represents aspiration pneumonia — this should be the leading explanation for the acute presentation, secondary to the chronic bulbar/pseudobulbar dysfunction from the underlying cerebrovascular process.
  2e. Known diabetic x5 years on Glimepiride, known hypertensive x10 years on cilnidipine, chronic heavy smoker (1.5 packs of beedis/day, now reduced to 3-4/day) for 50 years, occasional alcohol use: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: This cardiovascular risk profile (long-standing diabetes, hypertension, and a very heavy chronic smoking history) strongly supports cerebrovascular disease as the unifying mechanism for both the 1.5-year-old event and any new/ongoing infarcts, consistent with the eventual diagnosis.
  Single most important history feature: 2b (the unactioned 15-day follow-up after an abnormal MRI 1.5 years ago) — it is the point in the case's timeline where a system-level rather than purely cognitive breakdown allowed a partially characterised neurological process to progress unchecked to its current severity.
  Missing history: No documented details of what the original "?infarcts" MRI actually showed (location, number, acuity) beyond the attenders' secondhand account; no documented swallowing assessment or speech-language pathology involvement despite dysphagia being a presenting complaint; no clear timeline of exactly when the facial asymmetry (right facial hemiparesis, found on this admission's exam) first appeared relative to the 1.5-year-old event or the current acute illness.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Not oriented to time, place, or person; slurred speech; right facial hemiparesis (deviation of mouth to left) with intact gag reflex: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Disorientation combined with a new-documented facial hemiparesis supports an acute-on-chronic cerebrovascular event (consistent with the "acute infarct in left anterior temporal lobe" component of the diagnosis, given left-hemisphere lesions classically produce right-sided facial/limb findings), though the map notes the disorientation could also reflect delirium superimposed on dementia from the concurrent acute infection.
  3b. Motor exam: increased tone in both upper limbs with normal tone in both lower limbs; power 3/5 upper limbs bilaterally, 2/5 lower limbs bilaterally (improving to 3/5 by Day 2/3/2021); reflexes 3+ throughout upper and knee, 2+ (later 3+) at ankles, bilateral extensor plantar responses: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Bilateral increased tone, bilateral hyperreflexia, and bilateral extensor (Babinski) plantar responses together constitute a genuine bilateral upper motor neuron pattern — this is the key examination finding supporting the "compressive myelopathy" component of the diagnosis (a cord-level process would produce exactly this bilateral UMN pattern below the lesion), and is distinct from and complementary to the lateralising facial finding, which localises separately to the brain.
  3c. Sensory and cerebellar examination "could not be assessed as patient is not oriented": Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: This is an honest and appropriately documented limitation rather than a fabricated normal finding, but it means the map cannot confirm a sensory level (which would be the single most useful localising sign for the compressive myelopathy hypothesis) from clinical examination alone — the whole-spine MRI screening becomes the only way to confirm or refute cord compression and its level, making that investigation especially load-bearing for this case.
  3d. Neck rigidity recorded ambiguously as "-+" (a formatting/transcription ambiguity, not a clear positive or negative) with Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs explicitly negative: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: This is a genuine textual ambiguity (unlike a simple omission) — the notation does not clearly indicate whether neck rigidity was present or absent, and the map flags this rather than guessing in either direction; the auditor should check the source page directly for the intended meaning.
  3e. GRBS 361 mg/dl on Day 28/2 (markedly elevated), later reducing to 175 mg/dl by 1/3: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Significant hyperglycaemia during the acute illness (in a patient with pre-existing but presumably controlled diabetes) is consistent with a stress/infective hyperglycaemic response secondary to the pneumonia, and its correction alongside clinical improvement (fever and cough subsiding) supports the aspiration pneumonia as the acute driver of this admission's instability.
  System 1 vs System 2: The combination of prior neurological history, new facial asymmetry, and bilateral UMN signs likely triggered System 1 pattern recognition toward a combined structural brain-and-cord process fairly efficiently, well reflected in the appropriately multi-part provisional diagnosis. Distinguishing the acute infective (aspiration pneumonia) driver from the chronic neurological substrate required deliberate System 2 integration, which the treatment plan (antibiotics, glycaemic control, positioning to prevent aspiration/bed sores) shows was appropriately actioned even though the text does not explicitly state "aspiration pneumonia" as a named diagnosis.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Hemogram, CUE, CBP, LFT, RFT, ECG, chest X-ray, USG abdomen, MRI brain, MRI whole spine screening — all embedded as images with no text-extractable values: Decision Point: Type Pivotal (implied) — Insight: This panel appropriately covers both the acute infective workup (CBP, chest X-ray) and the structural neurological workup (MRI brain, MRI whole spine), and the MRI whole-spine result in particular is the single most important investigation for confirming the "compressive myelopathy" component of the diagnosis given the inconclusive sensory exam (Node 3c), but no specific values or imaging findings are extractable as text from the fetched page. This is flagged as a data-availability gap, not a fabricated finding.
  4b. Empirical treatment for aspiration/infection (ceftriaxone/Monocef from Day 1, later course), plus cardiovascular secondary prevention (Ecosprin-AV, later switched/added to atorvastatin 80mg, aspirin, clopidogrel) starting from Day 3/3: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: The addition of dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel) plus high-dose statin from Day 3 onward is consistent with the team treating this as an acute ischaemic cerebrovascular event requiring secondary stroke prevention, which directly supports the "acute infarct" component of the diagnosis as a genuine, evidence-based (not merely radiological/incidental) working diagnosis.
  4c. Ryles tube (nasogastric) feeding instituted from admission, later fortified with protein powder: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Early institution of tube feeding is the correct and safe response to the documented dysphagia/drooling/aspiration risk (Node 2d), directly addressing the swallowing impairment even without an explicit formal swallow assessment documented in the text.
  Unused/unclear results: No specific values from any of the ten-plus investigation images are extractable, including the MRI whole-spine findings that would be needed to confirm the compressive myelopathy diagnosis and localise the level of compression.
  Missing investigations (per available text): No documented formal swallow assessment (e.g., bedside or videofluoroscopic), no documented inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP/procalcitonin) to support the pneumonia/aspiration diagnosis, and no documented correlation between the current MRI brain findings and the "?infarcts" seen 1.5 years ago (i.e., whether this represents progression of the same lesion(s) or new/interval infarcts).

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Compressive myelopathy; acute infarct in left anterior temporal lobe; ?spondyloarthropathy; vascular dementia — ?multi-infarct dementia. This four-part diagnosis is carried unchanged across all six documented days (27/2 through 5/3).
  Point of closure: The diagnosis is stated on Day 1 (admission) and never revised or narrowed in the visible text through Day 5/3, despite clinical improvement (fever and cough resolving, power improving from 2/5 to 3/5 in the lower limbs) — treatment intensifies (secondary stroke prevention added, bed-sore prevention added) without the diagnostic label itself being updated.
  Closure type: This is a Multi-component analytical synthesis reached early and then held stable rather than narrowed — appropriate in the sense that all four components (cord compression, acute cerebral infarct, possible spondyloarthropathy as the cause of cord compression, and an overarching vascular dementia framework) are plausible and mutually compatible given a single underlying cerebrovascular/degenerative substrate, but the case notably never states which component is thought to explain the acute presenting complaints (fever, cough, dysphagia) — that acute infective/aspiration event is treated (tube feeding, antibiotics) but never named as its own diagnosis alongside the four structural/vascular ones.
  Open branches at closure: (1) the acute aspiration pneumonia (inferred strongly from the treatment pattern) is never explicitly stated as a fifth diagnosis in its own right; (2) whether the current MRI brain findings represent a new acute event or the same process seen 1.5 years ago is not clarified; (3) "?spondyloarthropathy" as the proposed cause of the compressive myelopathy is never confirmed or refuted by any stated imaging finding; (4) the ambiguous neck rigidity notation (3d) is never clarified.
  Synthesis insight: This case shows an appropriately broad and clinically coherent structural/vascular diagnosis reached early and sensibly held constant while the acute crisis was managed — the main gap is one of labelling rather than reasoning: the team's actions (tube feeding, antibiotics, aspiration precautions) show clear recognition of an acute infective/aspiration process, but this is never elevated to an explicit diagnosis line alongside the four chronic/structural ones, leaving the "problem list" incomplete relative to what was actually being treated.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Not strongly evidenced at the level of this admission — the four-part differential is broad and appropriately multi-system; if anchoring occurred, it more plausibly happened 1.5 years earlier when the abnormal MRI's follow-up was not enforced, a system-level rather than single-clinician cognitive bias.
  Availability: Not clearly identifiable from the fetched text — insufficient information to flag with confidence.
  Premature closure: Node 5 — The four-part diagnosis, once stated on Day 1, is never revisited or refined despite six days of observation and clear clinical improvement in the acute component, and the acute aspiration/infective process is never given its own diagnostic label despite being clearly treated.
  Framing: Node 2b — The case's own narrative frames the 18-month gap in care as attributable to "COVID lockdown," which is presented as an external/systemic cause; this framing is worth naming for the auditor as a system-level rather than purely clinical-reasoning failure point, distinct from the other bias categories.
  Omission bias: Node 4 — No formal swallow assessment or inflammatory marker trend is documented despite dysphagia and infection both being central to the acute presentation.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2b (the unactioned 15-day follow-up after the original abnormal MRI, 1.5 years before this admission)
  Why: Every downstream finding in this case — the progressive motor and cognitive decline, the current bilateral UMN signs, the facial hemiparesis, and even the aspiration pneumonia precipitating this admission — traces back to a neurological process that was partially characterised (an abnormal MRI, a specific follow-up plan) and then never re-evaluated for eighteen months. The transferable lesson is systemic rather than purely cognitive: a generated follow-up plan is only as good as the mechanism ensuring it is actioned, and this case is a clear example of how a diagnostic process can be clinically sound at the point of testing yet still fail the patient entirely if the loop back to the clinician is not closed — a lesson as relevant to health-system design as to individual reasoning.
