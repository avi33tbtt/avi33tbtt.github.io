NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 23M, bilateral lower limb weakness x5 days with tingling/numbness, non-projectile non-bilious vomiting 5 days back, a fall while getting up to urinate, gluteal abscess x5 months, scrotal abscess x20 days
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: Acute-subacute bilateral lower limb weakness in a young adult with a background of chronic/recurrent abscesses (gluteal, scrotal) should immediately activate an infective/inflammatory spinal cord or cauda equina process — most importantly infective spondylodiscitis or Pott's paraplegia — rather than the primary neurological mimics (stroke, MS, GBS) that are statistically less likely given the abscess history, which is a semantic qualifier of high pre-test-probability significance for TB.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Bilateral lower limb weakness with tingling/numbness x5 days: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: Bilateral, symmetric-onset weakness with sensory symptoms splits the differential between a spinal cord/cauda equina lesion and a peripheral polyneuropathy (e.g., GBS); the presence of a preceding fall and subsequent examination findings (Node 3) are needed to resolve this branch.
  2b. Vomiting x5 days, non-projectile, non-bilious, food particle content: Decision Point: Missed (as a discriminator) — Insight: Isolated vomiting without other features (no headache, no fever documented, no neck stiffness) is nonspecific here and was not clearly tied to either a raised-intracranial-pressure process (relevant given the eventual finding of cerebral ring-enhancing lesions) or a systemic infective process; this connection is not explicitly made.
  2c. Fall while attempting to stand for urination: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: A fall precipitated by attempting to void is a red flag suggesting the weakness was already functionally significant at that point (possible early paraparesis with bladder involvement), but this was not elaborated with a formal bladder/bowel function history (continence, retention), which is essential in any suspected cord-compressive syndrome.
  2d. Gluteal abscess x5 months (operated), scrotal abscess x20 days (operated 10 days back): Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Two anatomically separate, recurrent "cold" abscesses (explicitly noted as having no signs of inflammation) over several months is the single most important piece of history pointing toward disseminated tuberculosis as the underlying process, since cold abscesses are a classic TB presentation, and this history predates the neurological symptoms by months, suggesting the infective process was already active before cord involvement began.
  Missing history: No documented fever, night sweats, or weight loss (constitutional TB symptoms), no bladder/bowel continence history despite motor findings suggesting cord involvement, no prior TB diagnosis or contact history, no HIV risk-factor history taken at the time (the author's own conclusion later suggests considering this, but no history was actually elicited), no details on how the gluteal/scrotal abscesses were diagnosed or cultured at the time of their original treatment.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Motor: LL power 2/5 (right), 0/5 (left); UL power 5/5 bilaterally; LL hypotonia bilaterally: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Asymmetric but bilateral lower-limb-only weakness with hypotonia localizes the lesion to the spinal cord (likely conus/cauda equina level) or a lower motor neuron process affecting the lumbosacral segments, and sparing of the upper limbs argues strongly against a diffuse process like GBS (which typically shows more symmetric, ascending involvement) or a high cervical/brainstem lesion.
  3b. Deep tendon reflexes: mixed picture — some reflexes exaggerated (knee 3+/2+, ankle 3+/2+, supinator 3+/2+) with ankle clonus present on the right, but LL hypotonia and profound weakness also present: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: This is a genuinely mixed/discordant examination pattern — hyperreflexia and clonus (upper motor neuron signs) coexisting with hypotonia and profound weakness (more typical of lower motor neuron or acute spinal shock) — which the source text itself labels as "hypotonia, hyporeflexia, paraplegia characteristic of lower motor neuron lesion," but the documented reflex findings (3+ knee/ankle reflexes, clonus) do not straightforwardly support a pure LMN interpretation; this internal inconsistency in the exam should be flagged rather than resolved by assumption — it may represent acute spinal cord injury with evolving reflex changes (spinal shock transitioning) rather than a clean UMN-vs-LMN classification.
  3c. Bilateral extensor plantar reflex: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Bilateral extensor (upgoing) plantars are a genuine upper motor neuron/corticospinal tract sign and support a spinal cord lesion (myelopathy) rather than a pure peripheral nerve or root-level process, which is an important finding that appears to conflict with the hypotonia and is worth explicit reconciliation.
