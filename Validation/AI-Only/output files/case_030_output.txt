NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Loss of appetite x2 days, 2 episodes of vomiting, involuntary movements of upper limbs x2 episodes (second associated with tongue bite).
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: New-onset seizure-like activity in a middle-aged chronic heavy drinker splits the differential immediately between alcohol-withdrawal seizure, breakthrough seizure in a known epileptic (irregular medication), and a metabolic/hepatic precipitant (e.g., early hepatic encephalopathy) — each with a different urgency and treatment pathway, so this node cannot be closed on the complaint alone.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Chronic alcoholic, 180ml whisky/day x20 years, only one meal/day: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Heavy, sustained daily alcohol intake with poor nutritional intake is the single strongest predictor for this presentation cluster (withdrawal seizure, alcoholic hepatitis, and malnutrition-related coagulopathy), and anchors the differential toward an alcohol-related process from the outset.
  2b. K/c/o epilepsy (multiple episodes 10 years back, irregular medication; 2 further episodes 6 months back): Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: A pre-existing seizure disorder with irregular treatment keeps "breakthrough epilepsy" alive as a competing explanation alongside alcohol-related seizure, and the two are not mutually exclusive — irregular AED use and heavy alcohol use can both lower seizure threshold simultaneously.
  2c. Not a known case of DM2, HTN, Asthma, TB, CAD: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Absence of these comorbidities narrows away several alternative causes of altered sensorium/seizure (e.g., hypoglycemic seizure in a diabetic, hypertensive encephalopathy), keeping the map focused on the alcohol/hepatic axis.
  2d. No involuntary defecation or micturition during the episodes: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: This detail is recorded but not explicitly used to reason about seizure semiology (e.g., distinguishing a generalized tonic-clonic seizure from a non-epileptic event); the case does not show this being weighed in classifying the seizure type.
  Missing history: No explicit statement of when the last alcohol drink was taken before the seizure episodes (critical for timing an alcohol-withdrawal seizure, which classically occurs 6-48 hours after last drink); no family history recorded; no documented screening question about prior liver disease diagnosis or jaundice episodes before this admission.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Icterus present; no pallor, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, cyanosis, pedal edema at admission: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Icterus without pallor at this early stage points toward a hepatocellular process (alcoholic hepatitis) rather than a hemolytic or chronic anemia-driven picture, and anchors the "hepatic" arm of the differential established by the history.
  3b. Vitals stable (BP 110/70, PR 98, RR 17, SpO2 98%), GRBS 165: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: A normal-range admission glucose of 165 mg/dl reasonably excludes hypoglycemia as the seizure trigger at this timepoint, closing that branch early, though it does not exclude a hypoglycemic episode having occurred and resolved before arrival.
  3c. CNS — conscious, coherent, cooperative, HMF intact, no focal deficit at admission: Decision Point: Closing (partial) — Insight: A normal neurological exam post-ictally supports a generalized (rather than focal-structural) process at this stage, though the later diagnosis label references "?focal seizures," which is not obviously supported by this normal early exam — a decision point the case narrative does not resolve.
  System 1 nodes: icterus + chronic alcoholism → alcoholic liver disease (rapid pattern match).
  System 2 nodes: reconciling "?focal seizures" in the diagnosis line with a normal focal neurological exam required explicit reasoning that the case does not show being done.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. HbsAg, HIV1/2, HCV non-reactive; Dengue NS1/IgM/IgG negative: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: These appropriately rule out major infectious/viral causes of hepatitis and thrombocytopenia, strengthening the case for an alcohol-driven etiology by exclusion.
  4b. PT 20, INR 1.45, APTT 39 (admission day), improving serially to PT 15, INR 1.1, APTT 30 by day 4-5, then worsening again to PT 18, INR 1.33 by day 11 and PT 18/INR unspecified further: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: A coagulopathy that improves then re-worsens is a decision point for ongoing synthetic liver dysfunction or a superimposed process (e.g., sepsis, evolving cholecystitis) rather than a single static insult — this fluctuation is not explicitly discussed as a reasoning trigger in the case log.
  4c. Urine culture: E. coli >10^5 cfu/ml (day 7), blood culture no growth: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: A significant urine culture in a patient with new/worsening delirium and fever directly supports UTI as a contributing precipitant of the delirium/encephalopathy picture, which the treatment team appropriately incorporated (oral ofloxacin started).

  Working diagnosis reached: Progressed from "Seizures under evaluation (?focal seizures) secondary to ?hepatic encephalopathy with thrombocytopenia" (day 0) → "delirium secondary to alcohol withdrawal with hepatic encephalopathy grade II with alcoholic hepatitis with thrombocytopenia" (day 3) → "acute decompensation of CLD" with cholelithiasis/cholecystitis/?obstructive jaundice/?UTI (day 5 onward), evolving toward likely autoimmune/steroid-responsive alcoholic hepatitis management (prednisolone started day 17).
