DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — 50-year-old woman with backache and progressive ascending proximal-predominant weakness of all four limbs (quadriparesis under evaluation)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Backache x20 days (recurrent, previously partially relieved then returned), inability to use all four limbs x10 days.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: A subacute proximal-predominant, ascending, symmetric quadriparesis evolving over 10 days, preceded by a 20-day backache, must be split early into at least two broad tracks: a myopathic/neuromuscular process (proximal weakness pattern) versus a structural spinal/vertebral process (bony lesion causing cord or root compromise) — the pattern of weakness itself is the key discriminating clue and should shape which investigations are prioritised first.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Backache sudden onset, gradually progressive 2 months ago, initially relieved with medication at a local hospital, then recurred 20 days ago and did not respond to medication this time: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A backache that was initially medication-responsive but became refractory on recurrence suggests a progressive structural process (e.g., an expanding vertebral lytic lesion) rather than a simple musculoskeletal strain, and the loss of response to the same treatment is itself a decision point that should have prompted earlier imaging.
  2b. Weakness progression pattern explicitly described as: proximal lower limbs → distal lower limbs → proximal upper limbs → distal upper limbs, sudden onset, over 10 days: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This specific ascending, proximal-then-distal, lower-limb-then-upper-limb progression pattern is a genuinely distinctive and highly informative feature — a symmetric proximal-predominant weakness pattern is classically associated with myopathic processes (including steroid-responsive/inflammatory myopathies or metabolic myopathies) rather than a single focal cord compressive lesion, which would more typically produce a level-based deficit rather than this centripetal/proximal pattern; this pattern is the single most diagnostically specific finding in the entire history.
  2c. Functional difficulties: combing hair, bringing food to mouth, buttoning/unbuttoning, mixing food, squatting and rising from squatting, climbing stairs, wearing footwear, tripping of toes, lifting head off pillow, rolling over and rising from bed: Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: This detailed functional inventory confirms genuine proximal girdle (shoulder and hip) weakness (combing hair, squatting) together with some distal involvement (tripping of toes, buttoning), supporting a predominantly proximal but not purely proximal pattern — slightly blurring a "pure myopathy" picture and keeping a polyradiculopathy or mixed process on the differential.
  2d. No difficulty breathing; no diurnal variation of weakness; temporary relief of symptoms for about 2 hours after tramadol injection: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Absence of diurnal variation lowers probability of myasthenia gravis specifically; the description of "relief after tramadol" is an unusual and under-explained detail — if weakness itself (not just pain) transiently improves with an opioid analgesic, this raises the possibility that pain-inhibited weakness (pseudo-weakness from a painful spinal lesion) is a greater contributor than a primary myopathic or neurological process, and this detail deserved more explicit interpretation than it appears to have received in the text.
  2e. No DM, HTN, TB, asthma, epilepsy, CAD, CVA; total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy for dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) in 2009: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Surgical menopause (bilateral oophorectomy) at a relatively young age is relevant background for accelerated bone density loss, which could compound risk for a vertebral lytic/compressive lesion, and is also occasionally relevant to autoimmune or endocrine differentials, but this connection is not explicitly explored in the text.
  Single most important history feature: 2b (the specific ascending, proximal-predominant weakness progression pattern) — it is the feature that should most directly shape the differential toward the three hypotheses eventually listed (myopathy / vertebral lytic lesion / multiple myeloma), each of which this pattern is at least partially consistent with.
  Missing history: No documented weight loss, bone pain elsewhere, or constitutional symptoms (relevant to excluding or supporting multiple myeloma); no documented family history of neuromuscular or connective tissue disease; no documented medication history (e.g., statins, which can cause myopathy) prior to this admission; no clear quantification of how much relief was obtained from tramadol on pain versus on weakness specifically.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Conscious, oriented, cooperative; cranial nerves intact; tone normal in all four limbs: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Normal tone (rather than spasticity or flaccidity) somewhat lowers probability of an acute upper motor neuron cord lesion causing this weakness, and is more consistent with either a myopathic process or a lesion that has not yet produced classic upper-motor-neuron tone changes.
  3b. Power 4-/5 in all four limb groups (proximal and distal, upper and lower, symmetric bilaterally), hand grip 100% bilaterally: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: This is a notable internal inconsistency the map must flag: the history describes a clearly proximal-predominant, functionally severe weakness pattern (unable to comb hair, squat, or roll over), yet the recorded power grading (4-/5 throughout, with normal hand grip) is mild and does not obviously distinguish proximal from distal weakness — this mismatch between the reported functional disability and the graded exam findings is not commented on or reconciled anywhere in the text and represents the most important unresolved decision point in the examination.
  3c. Reflexes: normal and symmetric throughout (biceps, triceps, supinator, knee, ankle all 2+; plantars flexor bilaterally); sensory, cerebellar, and autonomic examination all normal; spine non-tender on this and every subsequent daily exam; meningeal signs negative: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: A completely normal reflex, sensory, and cerebellar examination with preserved (flexor) plantar responses argues against an acute upper motor neuron or myelopathic process at the time of these examinations, and normal spine tenderness on repeated exams is somewhat inconsistent with the "bony lytic lesion" hypothesis, unless the lesion is one that does not produce point tenderness (e.g., certain myelomatous lesions) — this tension between the exam and one of the three listed differentials is not explicitly addressed in the text.
  3d. Vitals notable for a persistently elevated respiratory rate (26–30 cpm across the admission) despite normal SpO2 (98%) and the patient denying breathing difficulty: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: A respiratory rate in the high-20s to 30 range is objectively tachypnoeic and is not explained anywhere in the text — combined with the "single breath count monitoring" ordered from Day 1 (a specific test for respiratory muscle/neuromuscular weakness, e.g., in suspected myasthenic or Guillain-Barré-type processes), this suggests the team was clinically concerned about respiratory muscle involvement despite the patient's subjective denial of dyspnoea, but the RR finding itself is never explicitly commented on in the assessment lines.
  System 1 vs System 2: The distinctive ascending proximal-predominant weakness pattern combined with backache likely triggered System 1 pattern recognition toward a short list of "myopathy vs bony lesion vs myeloma," which is reflected accurately in the provisional diagnosis. The discrepancy between reported functional disability and graded muscle power (Node 3b), and the persistently elevated respiratory rate (Node 3d), both required deliberate System 2 follow-up that is not clearly documented as having occurred.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Extensive laboratory and imaging panel — CBP, complete urine examination, ABG, LFT, RFT, thyroid profile, serology, random blood sugar, chest X-ray, X-ray sacroiliac joint, ECG, USG abdomen, USG neck, MRI brain, MRI cervical spine with whole spine screening — all embedded as images with no text-extractable values: Decision Point: Type Pivotal (implied) — Insight: This is an appropriately broad initial work-up for a quadriparesis of unclear cause (covering metabolic, endocrine, infective, and structural causes), and the whole-spine MRI screening in particular is the key investigation that would be expected to identify or exclude a vertebral lytic lesion, but no specific values or imaging findings are extractable as text from the fetched page. This is flagged as a data-availability gap, not a fabricated finding.
  4b. Treatment includes methylprednisolone 1g IV given once on Day 15/3 only, alongside ongoing tramadol, muscle relaxant (Myoril), and B-complex vitamins throughout: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A single high-dose pulse of methylprednisolone is a treatment consistent with an inflammatory/autoimmune myopathy or a demyelinating process being considered and empirically treated, but it is administered without an explicit statement in the text of which specific diagnosis it was targeting, and it is not repeated on subsequent days, leaving its intended role and the assessment of its effect unclear from the visible text.
  Unused/unclear results: No specific values from any of the fifteen-plus investigation images are extractable; in particular, no CPK (creatine phosphokinase) value is mentioned in text despite this being the single most relevant and expected test to support or refute a myopathic process, which is one of the three listed differentials.
  Missing investigations (per available text): No documented CPK, no documented serum protein electrophoresis or Bence-Jones protein result (relevant to excluding/confirming multiple myeloma, despite it being explicitly listed as a differential), and no documented electromyography/nerve conduction study, despite these being standard next steps for a proximal-predominant weakness pattern of unclear myopathic versus neurogenic origin.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Quadriparesis under evaluation, secondary to ?myopathy ?bony (vertebral) lytic lesion ?multiple myeloma — this three-part provisional diagnosis is carried unchanged through every daily note from admission to discharge on Day 17/3.
  Point of closure: No closure occurs — the patient is discharged on 17/3/2021 with the same open, three-part differential documented on Day 1, and no final diagnosis is recorded anywhere in the visible text.
  Closure type: This is an explicitly Unresolved/Open case rather than falling into any of the four standard closure types — the map notes this is unusual: a patient with an incompletely characterised quadriparesis over 10+ days, admitted, investigated extensively, treated empirically (including a single steroid pulse), and discharged without the differential having been narrowed at all in the documented notes.
  Open branches at closure: All three original hypotheses (myopathy, vertebral lytic lesion, multiple myeloma) remain simultaneously open at discharge; additionally, the internal inconsistency between reported functional disability and graded muscle power (3b), the unexplained persistent tachypnoea (3d), and the untested CPK/protein electrophoresis are all unresolved threads.
  Synthesis insight: This case is a genuine example of a map that never reaches Step DRM.6-style synthesis within the visible documentation — the diagnostic reasoning appears to have stalled at the investigation-gathering stage, and the discharge without a narrowed diagnosis or documented follow-up plan for interpreting the pending/completed investigations (e.g., MRI whole-spine, CPK) is itself the most notable feature of this case's reasoning trajectory, worth flagging distinctly from cases that reach an explicit (even if uncertain) closure.

  Anchoring: Not strongly evidenced — the three-part differential is carried consistently without inappropriately narrowing on limited evidence, which is appropriate hedging given the unresolved data, though it also reflects the case never progressing analytically.
  Availability: Not clearly identifiable from the fetched text — insufficient information to flag with confidence.
  Premature closure: Not applicable — if anything, this case shows the opposite pattern: closure never occurs at all within the documented admission, despite ten days of hospitalisation and extensive investigations.
  Framing: Node 1 — The case title emphasises "inability to use all four limbs," which foregrounds severity but somewhat obscures the more diagnostically specific detail (the proximal-predominant, ascending progression pattern) that is the most information-rich part of the history.
  Search satisficing: Node 4 — A broad initial panel was ordered, but no CPK or serum protein electrophoresis is documented despite these being the most targeted tests for two of the three named differentials (myopathy and myeloma respectively); the search for confirmatory evidence for the specific hypotheses named in the provisional diagnosis appears not to have been completed, or the results are not reflected in the visible notes, before discharge.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3b (mismatch between severe functional disability reported in history and only mildly reduced graded muscle power, 4-/5, on formal testing)
  Why: This is the single largest unreconciled discrepancy in the entire case — a patient described as unable to comb her hair, squat, climb stairs, or roll over in bed is graded at only 4-/5 power throughout, a grade that would typically still permit most of these activities against resistance. Explicitly addressing this mismatch (Is the grading imprecise? Is pain limiting effort during testing, consistent with the tramadol-response detail in Node 2d? Is there a fluctuating or fatigable component not captured by a single-point-in-time power grade?) would be the most transferable lesson from this case: when the history and the formal examination disagree in magnitude, that disagreement itself is a decision point requiring explicit resolution, not silent coexistence in the same note.
