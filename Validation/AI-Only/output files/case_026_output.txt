DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — 45-year-old woman with left lower limb weakness, hip pain, and lower backache following recent bilateral pneumonia/sepsis admission (chronic osteomyelitis of the hip)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Weakness of the left lower limb x3 months, in a patient recently hospitalized for 20 days with bilateral pneumonia, denovo-diagnosed DM II, and anaemia requiring PRBC transfusions.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: New-onset unilateral limb weakness emerging directly on the heels of a 20-day septic/infective hospitalisation with denovo diabetes should immediately raise a post-infective or metastatic-infective musculoskeletal process (e.g., septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, or an epidural/paraspinal abscess) rather than being framed as an independent neurological or orthopaedic complaint — the temporal adjacency to the prior severe infection is the single most important framing fact for this entire case.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Prior admission: high-grade continuous fever unrelieved by medication, occasional dry cough, non-bloody vomiting and loose stools, diagnosed as bilateral pneumonia with denovo DM II and anaemia, treated with imipenem-cilastatin and amikacin for 20 days: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Treatment with two broad-spectrum, reserve-tier antibiotics (a carbapenem plus an aminoglycoside) indicates the prior infection was recognised as severe/possibly resistant, and denovo diabetes diagnosed during this septic illness creates an immunocompromised state that substantially raises the risk of haematogenous seeding of bone/joint — this is the key mechanistic link the map should trace forward into the current hip/spine complaints.
  2b. Weakness of left lower limb developed after returning home from that admission, with progressive left hip pain, crepitus on walking, and progression from walking with support to complete inability to walk or rise from bed unassisted over about a month: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A subacute, progressively worsening course of hip pain with crepitus (rather than a sudden traumatic onset) is consistent with an evolving destructive joint/bone process such as osteomyelitis or infective arthritis, and fits a post-septic haematogenous-seeding timeline beginning during or shortly after the pneumonia admission.
  2c. Lower backache x3 months (same timeframe as the limb weakness) and pitting pedal oedema x3 months extending to the knee, unrelieved by medication: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Concurrent backache raises the possibility of vertebral/paraspinal involvement (e.g., discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, or epidural abscess) as an additional or alternative site of the same post-septic process, rather than the hip being the sole focus; the persistent pedal oedema is a separate thread that could reflect hypoalbuminaemia (from chronic infection/inflammation), venous stasis from reduced mobility, or a cardiac/renal contributor, and is not mechanistically tied to the musculoskeletal picture in the text.
  2d. Blood transfusion history: 1 PRBC unit at one hospital, 2 PRBC units at another: Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: Multiple transfusions confirm the anaemia was clinically significant, consistent with a chronic infective/inflammatory process (anaemia of chronic disease) rather than a single acute bleed, supporting the overall picture of a smouldering infective process spanning the interval between the two hospitalisations.
  2e. Denovo DM II 3 months back, on Glimepiride+Metformin; no HTN, TB, asthma, epilepsy, CVA, CAD; occasional toddy use; family history of diabetes in an elder brother: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Absence of a prior TB history somewhat lowers (without excluding, given the endemic context and the eventual differential of infective arthritis) probability of tuberculous osteomyelitis, though this is not explicitly investigated or excluded in the visible text.
  Single most important history feature: 2a/2b together — the temporal chain from severe pneumonia/sepsis with denovo diabetes directly into progressive hip pain and limb weakness is the backbone of the entire diagnostic reasoning, and should have been made an explicit, named hypothesis (haematogenous osteomyelitis/septic arthritis secondary to the recent bacteraemic illness) from the earliest point in this admission.
  Missing history: No documented blood culture results or organism identification from the prior pneumonia admission that could be correlated with any organism eventually isolated from the hip/spine; no documented weight loss, night sweats, or other constitutional symptoms that would raise or lower suspicion for tuberculous versus pyogenic osteomyelitis; no clear timeline correlating exactly when the hip symptoms began relative to the antibiotic course during the prior admission (i.e., did symptoms start during treatment, suggesting inadequate source control, or only after stopping antibiotics).

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. No pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy; pedal oedema present: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Absence of lymphadenopathy somewhat lowers probability of a disseminated lymphoproliferative or widespread tuberculous process, keeping a localized pyogenic musculoskeletal infection as the leading consideration.
  3b. Motor examination: markedly reduced power specifically at the left hip (flexors/extensors/rotators/abductors/adductors ranging 1/5 to 3-/5) and left knee flexors/extensors (1/5), with pain limiting hip examination and inability to roll over in bed due to left hip pain, while proximal upper limb and other lower-limb-joint power is relatively preserved (4+/5 range) and grossly symmetric except at the hip/knee: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A pattern of severe, localized weakness/pain-limited power specifically at the left hip and knee, with preserved strength elsewhere and preserved cranial nerves/higher mental function/sensation, strongly favours a local structural/infective process causing pain-inhibited weakness (pseudoparesis) over a true neurological (e.g., myelopathic or peripheral nerve) cause of weakness — this pattern recognition is well supported by the documented exam and is consistent with the eventual diagnosis.
  3c. Reflexes: knee and ankle reflexes both absent bilaterally ("-"), while biceps/triceps are brisk (3+) and other reflexes normal: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: Bilateral absent knee and ankle reflexes are a genuinely neurological finding that is not clearly explained by a purely local hip/spine infective process and is not commented on or reconciled with the eventual musculoskeletal diagnosis in the text — this could reflect a lumbosacral radiculopathy from spinal involvement, a coincidental peripheral neuropathy (plausible given new diabetes), or another cause, and represents an unresolved thread in the map.
  3d. Spine examination: tenderness over the lumbosacral spine; later (Day 3–4) straight leg raise test positive on the left with tenderness at the left sacroiliac joint and anterior thigh: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: The evolution of exam findings over the admission — from initial LS spine tenderness to a positive SLR and SI joint/anterior thigh tenderness by Day 3 — suggests either an evolving/spreading process (e.g., extension of infection from hip to sacroiliac joint or spine) or a re-examination revealing findings not elicited on Day 1; this progression itself is a decision point that should have prompted urgent re-imaging or escalation, which the text shows did occur via the eventual orthopaedic transfer.
  3e. Umbilical hernia (3×2 cm) noted incidentally on abdominal exam: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: This is an incidental finding not connected to the primary presentation and appropriately not pursued further in the visible text, though it is worth explicitly noting as excluded from the differential rather than left silently unaddressed.
  System 1 vs System 2: The combination of recent severe sepsis, denovo diabetes, and progressive localized hip pain with crepitus likely triggered System 1 pattern recognition toward an infective bone/joint process fairly early (reflected in empirical ceftriaxone/monocef being started by Day 1–2). The unexplained bilateral absent knee/ankle reflexes required deliberate System 2 follow-up (e.g., nerve conduction studies, correlation with new diabetes or spinal imaging) that is not evidenced in the fetched text.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Progressed from a broad provisional diagnosis ("lower backache under evaluation — ? left hip chronic osteomyelitis/infective arthritis — ? lumbar disc disease, DM+") on admission and Days 1–3, to a narrowed diagnosis of "chronic osteomyelitis of the hip" by Day 4, at which point the patient was transferred to orthopaedics.
  Point of closure: The narrowing from three competing hypotheses (osteomyelitis/infective arthritis/lumbar disc disease) to a single diagnosis (chronic osteomyelitis) occurs between the Day 3 and Day 4 notes, coinciding with worsening vitals (PR rising from 78 to 102, BP rising to 130/100) and evolving exam findings (SLR+, SIJ/anterior thigh tenderness) — but the specific imaging or laboratory evidence that tipped the diagnosis toward osteomyelitis specifically (over infective arthritis or lumbar disc disease) is not shown in the visible text.
  Closure type: Analytical, appropriately arrived at over several days of observation and escalating findings (a properly Held-open map rather than a premature single-day closure), though the final evidentiary basis is not visible in this fetch — this is a case where the DRM's staged, hedged provisional diagnosis (explicitly listing three possibilities with question marks) is itself a good model of appropriately holding the map open, and the auditor should specifically check the MRI hip images for the confirmatory findings before treating "chronic osteomyelitis" as validated by this text alone.
  Open branches at closure: (1) whether infective arthritis or lumbar disc disease were formally excluded, or simply de-prioritised as the hip diagnosis became more probable; (2) the bilateral absent knee/ankle reflexes (Node 3c) are never explained or revisited; (3) the specific causative organism linking the prior pneumonia/sepsis admission to the current bone infection is never identified in the visible text; (4) the persistent pedal oedema (3 months, unrelieved) is never mechanistically explained.
  Synthesis insight: This case demonstrates a generally well-reasoned, appropriately staged diagnostic process — the team held a differential open with explicit question marks for several days while gathering imaging and response-to-treatment data before narrowing to chronic osteomyelitis — but two threads (the bilateral reflex loss and the persistent oedema) remain unintegrated into the final synthesis in the visible text, and the specific imaging/lab evidence for the final diagnosis is not confirmable from this fetch.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2a (the recent severe pneumonia/sepsis admission with denovo diabetes as the likely source event for the subsequent bone/joint infection)
  Why: Explicitly naming and pursuing a temporal/causal hypothesis — "this patient's current musculoskeletal infection most likely originated from bacteraemia during her recent severe pneumonia admission" — from the very first encounter would focus the work-up (blood culture correlation, source-control review of the prior antibiotic course) more efficiently than treating the hip/back complaints as a fresh, unconnected problem. The transferable lesson: when a patient presents with a new focal problem shortly after a major systemic infective illness, the prior illness should be treated as the leading candidate source, not merely as background history.
