DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 038: 60M, mason, involuntary left-hand tremor, burning/swelling left hand, walking difficulty and giddiness x2yrs, on background of prior CVA
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map
Map scope: Full case, presenting complaint through working diagnosis
Note: source case includes several investigation panels (CUE, CBP, electrolytes, creatinine, RBS, ECG, T3/T4/TSH) presented only as embedded images with no transcribed numeric values in the text; these are marked "value not extractable from source" rather than assumed normal.

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Involuntary movement (tremor) of left hand fingers x2yrs (intermittent, days apart); burning sensation/swelling of left hand x2yrs; questionable walking difficulty and giddiness x2yrs.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: A tremor plus a burning dysaesthesia in the same limb, in a man with known vascular disease, opens two very different tracks from the first sentence — a central/movement-disorder track (tremor, gait, giddiness) and a peripheral neurovascular or nerve-territory track (burning, swelling) — and the complaint alone should have prompted holding both open rather than reading it as one problem.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Sudden-onset, static (non-progressive) grip weakness, proximal strength preserved: Decision Point — Type: Closing — Insight: A static rather than progressive course over 2 years argues against a subacute inflammatory or neoplastic process and keeps a fixed vascular/structural insult (old infarct, root/plexus injury) more likely than a progressive neurodegenerative one.
  2b. Tremor described as simultaneously resting, kinetic, and postural: Decision Point — Type: Missed — Insight: The case narrative records all three tremor types together without flagging how unusual that combination is; a tremor confined to one type (e.g., pure resting) would point toward a parkinsonian/basal-ganglia mechanism, while a mixed picture should have triggered explicit consideration of a structural cerebellar-outflow or vascular tremor rather than being folded into "kinetic tremors under evaluation."
  2c. Paraesthesia (burning, pricking, itching) in the left upper limb, with intact sensory exam later: Decision Point — Type: Bifurcating — Insight: A sensory symptom without an objective sensory deficit points toward either a small-fibre/neuropathic-pain mechanism or a vascular/ischaemic limb process rather than a large-fibre central lesion, and this branch is not reconciled with the "CVA" framing anywhere in the note.
  2d. Facial deviation and left-UL weakness 5 months prior, occurring immediately after news of his son's fatal MI, diagnosed as bilateral MCA territory infarct and treated medically: Decision Point — Type: Pivotal (for the case as documented) — Insight: This prior stroke diagnosis becomes the anchor for everything that follows; because the acute event followed acute emotional/psychological stress, a stress-related vasospastic or non-atherothrombotic mechanism (e.g., reversible cerebral vasoconstriction, stress-cardiomyopathy-associated embolism) was at least biologically plausible and is never revisited.
  2e. Known CAD with anterior-wall MI in 2017 and prior CAG: Decision Point — Type: Closing (for cardioembolic source) — Insight: Establishes a plausible embolic source for the earlier infarct and supports a vascular explanation for current limb findings, but is not linked back to the marked inter-arm blood-pressure discrepancy found on this admission.
  2f. Occupation as a mason for decades: Decision Point — Type: Missed — Insight: Chronic repetitive upper-limb use/vibration and postural loading is a plausible contributor to a peripheral or entrapment component of the burning/weakness picture and is never explored as an occupational exposure.
  Single most important history feature: The temporal link between the son's death and the prior stroke event (2d) — it is the piece most likely to reframe the working "CVA" diagnosis if pursued, and it is not pursued.
  Missing history: No documented family history; no explicit description of gait/giddiness character (vestibular vs cerebellar vs orthostatic); no medication-adherence or antiplatelet-response history; no smoking history beyond "non-smoker" with no further vascular risk-factor detail (lipids, diabetes screening origin); no clarification of which arm was used for the historical BP readings that led to the hypertension diagnosis.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Clubbing of fingers and koilonychia of toes: Decision Point — Type: Missed — Insight: This is an unusual, unexplained combination (clubbing usually cardiopulmonary/GI, koilonychia usually iron-deficiency) that is recorded but never referenced again or worked up, despite being exactly the kind of incidental finding that should generate its own branch.
  3b. Marked inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy — 110/80 mmHg left arm vs 210/150 mmHg right arm: Decision Point — Type: Pivotal, but functionally Missed — Insight: A >20-40 mmHg systolic difference between arms is a recognised marker of subclavian/aortic arch arterial disease (e.g., subclavian stenosis, Takayasu arteritis, aortic dissection) and is the single most objectively striking finding in the entire exam; it is documented as "discrepancy in BP of both upper limbs" but never developed into a vascular hypothesis, imaging plan, or link to the left-arm symptoms it sits right next to.
  3c. Left upper-limb wasting with measured circumference asymmetry (arm 26 cm vs 29 cm; forearm 23.5 cm vs 26.5 cm) and largely 4/5 (vs 5/5) power in distal left hand/forearm muscles, sparing proximal groups: Decision Point — Type: Pivotal — Insight: A distal, asymmetric, wasting-predominant pattern without a clear proximal or global weakness argues for a lower-motor-neuron or anterior-horn/root process in that limb rather than a purely upper-motor-neuron stroke sequela, which would more typically produce a pyramidal weakness pattern.
  3d. Increased tone in the left upper limb despite the wasting/LMN-pattern weakness above: Decision Point — Type: Bifurcating — Insight: Increased tone with wasting and preserved proximal power is an internally discordant combination — it does not cleanly fit either a pure old-infarct (UMN) picture or a pure anterior-horn (LMN) picture — and the map should hold this open as evidence of a mixed or dual pathology rather than resolving it silently into "CVA."
  3e. Sensory system recorded as intact despite the patient's reported burning/pricking symptoms: Decision Point — Type: Closing (for a large-fibre sensory lesion) — Insight: A normal formal sensory exam alongside reported dysaesthesia narrows the mechanism toward small-fibre/autonomic or centrally-mediated pain rather than a dorsal-column or root-level sensory loss.
  System 1 nodes (likely pattern-matched): recognition of BP as "hypertension" and tremor as "kinetic tremor" without further probabilistic reasoning.
  System 2 nodes (deliberate analysis required but under-used): the inter-arm BP discrepancy (3b) and the tone–wasting discordance (3d) both needed explicit analytical reasoning that the note does not show being done.
  Findings listed but not followed up: 3a (clubbing/koilonychia) and 3b (BP discrepancy) both meet this criterion.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: "K/C/O CVA B/L MCA territory infarct + CAD + HTN with kinetic tremors under evaluation."
  Point of closure: Documented at admission, apparently carried forward from the prior stroke diagnosis 5 months earlier, with the current tremor appended as an unresolved add-on rather than integrated into a single explanatory hypothesis.
  Closure type: Forced/label closure — the "diagnosis" is explicitly left open ("tremors under evaluation") while empiric treatment (tetrabenazine, pregabalin) was started anyway; this is neither a clean pattern-recognition closure nor an analytical closure, since no single mechanism is offered to unify the distal wasting, BP asymmetry, mixed tremor, and prior stroke.
  Open branches at closure: Hirayama disease (raised explicitly by the discussant, then left unresolved because of atypical age); subclavian/large-vessel arterial disease (never explicitly raised despite 3b); entrapment/peripheral nerve process at the wrist or forearm (never raised); small-fibre neuropathy explaining the burning/pricking (implied by treatment with pregabalin but never named as a diagnosis).
  Synthesis insight: The team treated the tremor and the pain symptomatically (tetrabenazine, pregabalin) without resolving which of at least three competing anatomical explanations (central/old-infarct, anterior-horn/root, or vascular-limb) accounted for the distal wasting and weakness, effectively closing the chart without closing the differential.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring — Node 2d/5: The prior "CVA, B/L MCA territory infarct" label from 5 months earlier anchors the entire current presentation, so a new distal, asymmetric, LMN-pattern limb problem and a striking BP asymmetry are folded into the old stroke diagnosis rather than prompting a fresh differential.
  Availability — Node 5: The discussant raises Hirayama disease specifically (a memorable, named entity fitting the distal-wasting pattern) but then dismisses it on age alone without pursuing the actual diagnostic test (flexion MRI) that would confirm or refute it, suggesting the alternative was considered because it was salient rather than systematically worked up.
  Premature closure — Node 5: A working label ("under evaluation") was recorded and tetrabenazine/pregabalin started before subclavian imaging or EMG/NCS — tests that could discriminate the competing hypotheses — were obtained.
  Framing effect — Node 1/2d: The framing of the patient as a known stroke and hypertension patient, reinforced by the emotionally salient story of the stress-triggered prior event, appears to have shaped how the team read every subsequent finding as a stroke sequela rather than as potentially independent new pathology.
  Search satisficing — Node 3b/4b: Once the BP was labelled "discrepancy...on both upper limbs" and hypertension medication was continued, the search stopped — no vascular workup followed a finding that, on its own, is unusual enough to warrant one.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3b — the >100 mmHg systolic blood-pressure discrepancy between the two upper limbs.
  Why: This is the single most objectively abnormal, easily reproducible finding in the entire case, sits directly beside the symptomatic left arm, and was recorded without triggering any vascular hypothesis, imaging referral, or discussion. Earlier recognition of inter-arm BP discrepancy as a decision point — rather than a passing observation — would have opened a vascular-limb branch (subclavian stenosis/occlusion, arteritis) that could unify the burning, swelling, and possibly the tremor and gait complaints far more parsimoniously than the current "old stroke plus tremor under evaluation" framing.
