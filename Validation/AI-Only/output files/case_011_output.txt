DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case_011 (41M, high-coloured urine x2y, pain abdomen x1wk)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 41M, high-coloured urine since 2 years (intermittent, on/off weekly x2 episodes since
  2019), pain abdomen since 1 week (diffuse, continuous, squeezing, aggravated by eating)
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: Two complaints of very different chronicity presented together — a 2-year urinary
  symptom and a 1-week abdominal pain — should be triaged as two possibly-separate problems
  rather than one narrative from the outset; the case presentation implicitly treats them as one
  story, but the semantic qualifier mismatch (years vs. days) is itself a decision point that
  should prompt asking whether a chronic renal/urinary process and an acute pancreatic/biliary
  process are coincidental, causally linked, or both markers of a shared systemic driver
  (alcohol).

NODE 2 — History
  2a. 2015: high-coloured urine, told "ureteric calculi" by private hospital, no records:
      Decision Point: Missed
      Insight: An unverified, records-free diagnosis from 6 years prior should not be accepted
      at face value — recurrent high-coloured urine over years is at least as consistent with
      chronic glomerular disease (hematuria/proteinuria) as with recurrent stone disease, and the
      absence of any documented imaging or stone-passage history is a gap that should have
      prompted independent re-evaluation rather than carrying forward the old label.
  2b. 2019: HTN diagnosed, medication taken only 1 week, then again poorly adherent in 2020:
      Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: Poorly-controlled hypertension for ~2 years in a patient with 2 years of
      intermittent high-coloured urine is a strong combined signal for underlying chronic kidney
      disease (hypertension both as cause and consequence of renal pathology) — this link is
      present in the history but not explicitly reasoned through in the write-up until the final
      diagnosis line.
  2c. Cholecystectomy 1 year back: Decision Point: Bifurcating
      Insight: Directly relevant to the current abdominal pain — post-cholecystectomy patients
      can develop recurrent biliary-type pain (retained stone, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction) or
      gallstone-pancreatitis-related pancreatitis from missed/recurrent choledocholithiasis; this
      surgical history is highly pertinent to the "acute pancreatitis" provisional diagnosis and
      should have been foregrounded rather than listed neutrally in past history.
  2d. Alcohol 120ml/day x1 year (per text; "alcoholic since 7 years" also stated — internally
      inconsistent duration): Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: Regardless of the exact duration ambiguity, significant alcohol intake is the
      single strongest risk factor for acute pancreatitis in this case and should anchor the
      pain-abdomen differential toward alcohol-related pancreatitis, especially combined with the
      post-cholecystectomy history (2c) as a competing/complementary mechanism.
  2e. Hospitalized 6 months ago for the same abdominal pain complaint: Decision Point: Missed
      Insight: A prior hospitalization for an apparently identical pain presentation 6 months ago
      is a recurrence pattern that should raise the question of whether this is recurrent acute
      pancreatitis (raising concern for chronic pancreatitis evolution) rather than a first
      episode — this is not addressed in the synthesis.
  Single most important history: 2b (poorly-treated hypertension over 2 years, temporally
  overlapping the urinary symptom) — most directly foreshadows the eventual chronic
  glomerulonephritis diagnosis and was available from the first history-taking.
  Missing history: No documented prior 24-hour urine protein, renal ultrasound, or renal function
  trend from 2015–2020 despite 6 years of relevant symptoms; no clarification of the
  alcohol-duration inconsistency (1 year vs. 7 years in the same note); no family history of
  renal disease.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. PR 110, BP 180/120, afebrile, GRBS 150: Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: A markedly elevated blood pressure (180/120, hypertensive urgency range) at
      presentation, in a patient with known poorly-treated HTN, is itself a decision point that
      should trigger renal function assessment as a priority alongside the pancreatitis workup —
      severe uncontrolled hypertension and unexplained chronic hematuria together are a classic
      combination pointing toward underlying renal parenchymal disease.
  3b. Abdomen obese, lap cholecystectomy scar, tenderness in epigastrium/right & left
      hypochondrium, guarding in epigastrium and right hypochondrium, no rigidity:
      Decision Point: Closing
      Insight: The tenderness/guarding pattern (epigastric + bilateral hypochondrial) without
      rigidity is consistent with acute pancreatitis and reasonably closes toward that diagnosis
      for the abdominal pain component, though the right hypochondrial component also keeps a
      biliary/post-cholecystectomy source plausible given 2c.
  System 1 nodes: Alcohol history + epigastric pain pattern-matched quickly to "acute
  pancreatitis" (illness script match).
  System 2 nodes: The BP of 180/120 in context of chronic urinary symptoms required deliberate
  connection-making toward a renal diagnosis — this connection is not made explicit until Day 4.
  Unfollowed finding: The severely elevated BP at admission (180/120) was not immediately
  followed by an urgent renal workup in the documented Day 0–3 treatment, which focused
  exclusively on pancreatitis management (IVF, analgesia, antiemetics) — renal-directed workup
  (CUE) appears only on Day 4.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Investigation images (3 files near "Provisional Diagnosis," image-only): Decision Point:
      Cannot classify — Insight: Presumably includes serum amylase/lipase (needed to formally
      support "acute pancreatitis") and initial renal function tests, but values are not
      extractable from text.
  4b. Day 4 CUE — RBC: plenty, Albumin: +++, Pus cells: plenty: Decision Point: Pivotal
      Insight: This is the most decisive documented finding in the whole case — heavy hematuria
      combined with heavy (+++) proteinuria is a strong pattern for glomerular disease (as
      opposed to simple UTI or stone disease, which would not typically produce +++ albuminuria)
      and directly supports the eventual chronic glomerulonephritis diagnosis; this result arrives
      four days into admission, considerably after the abnormal BP (3a) that should have prompted
      earlier renal-focused testing.
  4c. Day 5 — burning micturition slightly decreased, hematuria present; empiric nitrofurantoin
      and telmisartan started Day 4: Decision Point: Bifurcating
      Insight: Nitrofurantoin was started empirically alongside the CUE findings, treating the
      pus cells as a presumed UTI, while the +++ albuminuria and RBCs were labeled "urinary
      tract" in the Day 4 assessment without yet distinguishing infective UTI from glomerular
      hematuria/proteinuria — these are different problems requiring different confirmatory
      tests (urine culture vs. renal biopsy/further nephrology workup), and the text doesn't show
      that distinction being made before treatment started.
  Unused/unverifiable results: Image-only investigations cannot be checked against the "acute
  pancreatitis" diagnostic label (amylase/lipase values not visible in text).
  Missing investigations (as documented in text): No stated urine culture to confirm/refute UTI
  before starting empiric nitrofurantoin; no stated serum creatinine trend across the 5 days
  despite the eventual CKD-spectrum diagnosis; no renal ultrasound result described in prose.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Acute pancreatitis (resolved) with chronic glomerulonephritis
  Point of closure: Final diagnosis line, after Day 5 — evolved from the Day 0 "acute
  pancreatitis" and Day 4 "acute pancreatitis with urinary tract [infection]" labels.
  Classification of leap: Mixed — the pancreatitis component was pattern-recognition closure
  (System 1) from presentation (alcohol + epigastric pain + guarding), while the renal component
  moved from an initial UTI framing (Day 4 assessment: "urinary tract") to "chronic
  glomerulonephritis" by the final diagnosis line without an explicit intermediate step shown in
  the text — this final relabeling is not narrated as a reasoning move (e.g., "given +++
  albuminuria and plenty RBCs alongside 6 years of symptoms and 2 years of uncontrolled HTN, this
  is more consistent with glomerular disease than isolated UTI"), it simply appears as the closing
  diagnosis.
  Open branches at closure: Whether there is a concurrent UTI in addition to the glomerulonephritis
  is never resolved (nitrofurantoin was given, but no culture result confirms or refutes true
  infection); the specific type/etiology of the chronic glomerulonephritis (post-infectious,
  IgA nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, etc.) is not addressed at all.
  Synthesis insight: The relabeling from "urinary tract [infection]" (Day 4) to "chronic
  glomerulonephritis" (final) is the case's most important — and least explained — reasoning
  transition. It's plausibly correct given the heavy proteinuria, but the write-up does not show
  the discriminating logic, which is exactly the kind of silent diagnostic pivot a reasoning map
  is meant to surface.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 1/3 — The alcohol history and epigastric pain likely anchored the entire team
  toward "acute pancreatitis" as the primary problem for the first 3-4 days, plausibly delaying
  attention to the markedly abnormal admission BP (180/120) and the 2-year urinary symptom
  history until Day 4.
  Availability: Not clearly present in this text.
  Premature closure: Node 4c — Treating the Day 4 pus cells as UTI and starting empiric
  nitrofurantoin without a culture, while the more striking +++ albuminuria and plentiful RBCs
  were present in the same sample, risks anchoring on the "infection" explanation because it is
  more common and more immediately actionable, rather than pursuing the glomerular pattern that
  the final diagnosis ultimately settled on.
  Framing: Node 1 — The chief complaint order (urine symptom listed first, but pain abdomen
  driving the admission) may have framed pain abdomen as the primary problem and urine symptoms
  as background, when the reverse chronology (2 years vs. 1 week) suggests the urinary/renal
  problem may be the more fundamental underlying condition.
  Search satisficing: Node 4 — Once "acute pancreatitis" explained the presenting pain and
  symptomatically improved by Day 3 ("pain subsided"), the case history doesn't show continued
  active investigation into the cause of the chronic urinary symptoms beyond the Day 4 CUE — the
  etiological workup for the glomerulonephritis (its type/cause) appears to stop at the CUE.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3a / 4b (severely elevated admission BP → delayed renal workup)
  Why: The patient arrived with BP 180/120 and a documented 2-year history of intermittent
  high-coloured urine, yet the renal-focused investigation (CUE showing +++ albuminuria and
  plentiful RBCs) wasn't obtained until Day 4 of a 5-day admission. For a learner, the
  transferable lesson is that a markedly abnormal vital sign (severe hypertension) occurring
  alongside a chronic, unexplained symptom (years-long hematuria) should trigger same-day
  targeted workup for the chronic problem, not just management of the acute presenting complaint
  — treating the "loudest" problem first is natural but can delay recognition of the
  more consequential underlying diagnosis (chronic glomerulonephritis, here found incidentally
  rather than sought).
