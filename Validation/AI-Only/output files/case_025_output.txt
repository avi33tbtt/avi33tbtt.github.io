DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — 21-year-old man with vomiting, loose stools, and epigastric pain, with dark/blood-tinged stools (acute gastroenteritis)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Vomiting x1 week, loose stools x2 days, pain abdomen x2 days.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: A week-long vomiting illness that only develops loose stools and abdominal pain in the last 2 days should be actively questioned as either a single evolving infective gastroenteritis or two distinct/sequential processes (e.g., an initial upper-GI insult followed by a separate lower-GI event) rather than assumed to be one continuous illness from day one.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Started working as a waiter at a local restaurant 1 month ago; married 1 month back: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: New occupational food handling/exposure is a plausible source of an infective gastroenteritis (occupational or dietary exposure) but is not explicitly connected to the illness onset in the text; it is documented as background without being used as a decision point.
  2b. Admitted 2 years ago for "fever with thrombocytopenia" (2 days, local hospital): Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: A past episode of fever with thrombocytopenia (in a dengue-endemic setting, as suggested by other cases in this same case series) raises the question of whether this patient has an underlying susceptibility to recurrent thrombocytopenic febrile illness, and whether the current presentation could represent a similar process — this history is documented but not referenced again when interpreting the current bleeding-associated stool changes.
  2c. Vomiting since 1 week, associated with home-made fried rice consumption, escalating from 4 non-projectile watery/food-containing episodes (first 2 days, non-blood-tinged) to 7–8 episodes/day in subsequent days: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: The temporal link to fried rice and the escalating frequency pattern is classic for infective gastroenteritis (and specifically raises Bacillus cereus food poisoning as a classic teaching association with reheated/stored rice), giving a plausible dietary trigger and expected short-incubation course.
  2d. Pain abdomen x2 days, initially peri-umbilical and burning, migrating to epigastric burning pain by the morning of presentation: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: A shift from peri-umbilical to epigastric pain over a short interval could reflect either ongoing gastritis/gastroenteritis-related irritation or an evolving separate process (e.g., peptic ulcer disease with possible bleeding, given the subsequent stool findings) — this migration pattern is a genuine decision point that the text does not explicitly interpret.
  2e. Loose stools x2 days — initially watery, small-volume, frequent — progressing since the morning of presentation to dark-coloured stools (~7 episodes), occasionally blood-tinged, without tenesmus: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: The evolution from watery diarrhoea to dark, occasionally blood-tinged stools is the single most clinically significant feature in the history — dark/blood-tinged stool in the setting of epigastric pain raises active or recent upper or lower GI bleeding as a real possibility, not merely a marker of infective colitis, and this should have been an explicit trigger for urgent haemoglobin/haematocrit trending and consideration of endoscopy, distinct from the routine gastroenteritis work-up.
  Single most important history feature: 2e (progression to dark, blood-tinged stools) — it is the feature that most changes the acuity and required work-up of this case, shifting it from "probable infective gastroenteritis" to "gastroenteritis with a possible GI bleed requiring active exclusion of a bleeding source."
  Missing history: No documented NSAID or other gastro-irritant medication use (relevant to a possible peptic ulcer/gastritis-related bleed), no documented alcohol history, no quantification of stool volume/frequency trend beyond the day of presentation, and no clarification of whether the "dark coloured" stool was confirmed as melena versus simply concentrated/altered stool from dehydration.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Thin built, dehydrated (marked as "+"): Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Documented dehydration is consistent with, and expected from, a week of vomiting plus 2 days of diarrhoea, supporting volume loss as a contributor to the tachycardia noted next.
  3b. Vitals: PR 120 bpm, BP 120/80 mmHg, Temp 101°F: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: A pulse of 120 with a maintained blood pressure could represent compensated hypovolaemia from dehydration/diarrhoeal losses, or early compensation for an occult GI bleed — the exam alone cannot distinguish these, and the text does not document orthostatic vitals, which would help discriminate.
  3c. CVS S1S2 normal, lungs clear; abdomen — epigastric tenderness present, bowel sounds present: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Focal epigastric tenderness is consistent with gastritis/peptic disease as the pain source and supports (without confirming) an upper-GI contributor to the overall picture, but a soft, non-peritonitic abdomen argues against a surgical acute abdomen.
  Finding listed but not clearly followed up: No documented digital rectal examination or direct stool inspection description (beyond patient-reported "dark coloured, occasionally blood tinged") is recorded, despite this being a basic, high-yield step to characterise the reported blood-tinged stools directly rather than relying solely on patient report.
  System 1 vs System 2: The classic history (recent food exposure, vomiting, then diarrhoea, fever, dehydration) likely triggered System 1 pattern recognition toward acute gastroenteritis. The dark/blood-tinged stool description required deliberate System 2 follow-up (haemoglobin trend, stool examination, consideration of endoscopy) that is not evidenced as having occurred in the fetched text.

  4a.   4b. Treatment includes an explicit standing instruction "W/F [watch for] bleeding manifestations" on both Day 1 and Day 2: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This treatment-plan line is the clearest textual evidence that the treating team did recognise and actively monitor for a bleeding risk, which is reassuring and suggests the dark/blood-tinged stool finding was not overlooked clinically, even though the specific investigation results supporting or refuting active bleeding are not visible in this fetch.
  Unused/unclear results: No haemoglobin/haematocrit trend, no platelet count, and no stool occult blood or microscopy result are extractable from text, despite being the key investigations needed to resolve the bleeding-versus-infective-diarrhoea question raised at Node 2e.
  Missing investigations (per available text): No dengue NS1/serology result is visible in text despite the relevant 2-year-prior history of fever with thrombocytopenia; no upper GI endoscopy or imaging is mentioned, though the 3-day treatment course shown may not have required escalation to endoscopy if bleeding was self-limited (this cannot be confirmed either way from the fetched text).

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Acute gastroenteritis.
  Point of closure: Stated immediately after the first investigation image block, before any of the daily treatment notes are shown.
  Closure type: This reads as a Premature closure with respect to the dark/blood-tinged stool finding specifically — "acute gastroenteritis" is a reasonable unifying label for the vomiting/diarrhoea/fever/dehydration picture, but the diagnosis as stated does not explicitly account for the blood-tinged component, even though the treatment plan's "watch for bleeding manifestations" instruction shows the team was clinically alert to this risk in parallel.
  Open branches at closure: (1) whether the dark/blood-tinged stools reflect a genuine GI bleed (and if so, its source and severity) or are an artefact of severe gastroenteritis/dehydration is never explicitly resolved in the visible diagnosis; (2) the relevance (if any) of the 2-year-prior fever-with-thrombocytopenia episode to the current illness is never revisited; (3) the specific infective agent (viral, bacterial, food-toxin-mediated) is not named, though the empirical ceftriaxone suggests a bacterial cause was considered likely enough to treat.
  Synthesis insight: The label "acute gastroenteritis" is a reasonable working diagnosis for the overall syndrome and the treatment plan appropriately hedges against a bleeding complication even without a stated confirmatory diagnosis for it — this is a case where the diagnosis line under-states the full clinical picture relative to what the treatment plan implies the team was actually worried about, which is a useful distinction between the stated closure and the team's real-time vigilance.

  Availability: Not clearly identifiable from the fetched text — insufficient information to flag with confidence.

  Framing: Node 1 — The blog post's own title lists vomiting, loose stools, and abdominal pain but does not mention blood-tinged or dark stools, which may reflect how the case was framed by the presenting complaints originally recorded versus what emerged over the course of the history.
  Omission bias: Node 3/4 — No documented direct stool inspection or digital rectal exam, and no extractable haemoglobin/platelet trend, despite the bleeding risk being explicitly flagged in the treatment plan.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 2e / Node 5 (dark, blood-tinged stool not explicitly reconciled with the stated diagnosis of "acute gastroenteritis")
  Why: This is the clearest example in this case of a genuinely alarming finding (possible GI bleeding) being actively monitored for in the treatment plan but not reflected in the diagnosis label itself. The transferable lesson: when a treatment plan includes a specific safety-net instruction (e.g., "watch for bleeding manifestations"), the diagnosis should either explicitly account for that risk or explicitly state that it remains an unresolved possibility — leaving it implicit in the orders alone creates a gap between what the team is watching for and what the documented diagnosis says is happening.
