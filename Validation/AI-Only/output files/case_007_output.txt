DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 007 / 60F, HFrEF with suspected bronchogenic carcinoma (T3N3M0) and acute gastroenteritis with oral candidiasis

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: 60-year-old woman presenting with cough with expectoration and SOB x1 month, fever x1 month, and vomiting and loose stools x4 days.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Bifurcating
  Insight: The presentation splits cleanly into two temporally distinct problems — a subacute-to-chronic month-long respiratory/constitutional syndrome (cough, SOB, fever) and a superimposed acute 4-day gastrointestinal illness — and should activate separate branches from the outset rather than being folded into a single differential, since the eventual working diagnosis explicitly names both a pulmonary process and acute gastroenteritis as distinct entities.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Baseline of intermittent cough once a month for 20 years, relieved with medication, now with a new high-grade fever with chills, low backache, and SOB one month ago, treated as "anaemia" on ceftriaxone, then worsening over the last 4 days with hemoptysis and wheeze: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A change in a decades-long stable cough pattern into a new syndrome with fever, hemoptysis, and wheeze — especially after empirical antibiotic treatment for a vague "anaemia" diagnosis without a clear source being fixed — is a red flag for a new structural or infective process (malignancy, TB, abscess) rather than a flare of the baseline chronic cough, and should have been a decision point prompting imaging early rather than continued empirical management.
  2b. Significant unintentional weight loss (4–5 kg over one week) and loss of appetite: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Rapid unintentional weight loss combined with a new hemoptysis-associated respiratory syndrome in an older adult strongly raises pre-test probability for malignancy and should be treated as a red-flag constitutional symptom requiring early cross-sectional imaging, not just symptomatic management.
  2c. Family history: brother died 25 years ago of similar complaints; sister has chronic cough/SOB and uses inhalers: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: This raises two competing hereditary/environmental branches — a familial chronic obstructive/restrictive lung disease (e.g., a fibrotic or structural lung condition) versus shared environmental exposure — and is directly relevant given the exam later reveals a saddle-nose deformity and severe malnutrition/wasting, findings that suggest a chronic systemic process rather than an isolated new illness.
  2d. No history of hypertension, diabetes, epilepsy, thyroid disorder, asthma, or tuberculosis: Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: The explicit absence of a prior TB history does not rule out active TB given the endemic setting and radiographic findings later (cavitary lesion), and should not be over-weighted as closing that branch.
  Most important history feature: The combination of new hemoptysis, wheeze, and profound unintentional weight loss in the setting of a previously stable chronic cough is the single feature set that most should have redirected the differential toward malignancy/structural lung disease over the prior working diagnosis of "anaemia."
  Missing history: No smoking history is documented (notable omission given the eventual lung cancer staging), no occupational/environmental exposure history, and no clear timeline connecting the ceftriaxone course to symptom trajectory — these are genuine gaps in the source text, not filled in here.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. Severe malnutrition: BMI 13.25, skin-fold thickness 4mm, temporal and muscle wasting, ichthyotic skin: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This degree of cachexia is disproportionate to 4 days of GI symptoms alone and points to a chronic wasting process (malignancy, chronic infection, or severe malabsorption) operating well before this acute presentation — a decision point that should have been weighted heavily toward an underlying chronic disease process.
  3b. Pallor, koilonychia, clubbing with "fluctuation": Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Koilonychia and pallor suggest chronic iron-deficiency anaemia (consistent with the outside diagnosis of "anaemia"), while clubbing — especially with a fluctuant/possible hypertrophic component — is a strong pulmonary red flag (associated with lung malignancy, chronic suppurative lung disease, or fibrosis) that should have prompted urgent chest imaging rather than being subsumed under the anaemia label.
  3c. Left supraclavicular lymph node, 2.5 cm, hard, mobile: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: A left supraclavicular (Virchow's) node is a classic sentinel finding for intra-abdominal or intrathoracic malignancy and should be treated as a near-diagnostic decision point warranting urgent tissue diagnosis, which the case does eventually pursue (lung biopsy).
  3d. Tracheal deviation to the right, dilated chest wall veins, decreased breath sounds and dullness/flatness over the left hemithorax, with a left-sided visible cardiac impulse and Broadbent's sign: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This constellation (mediastinal shift away from the left, left-sided dullness, and a shifted cardiac impulse) localizes a large-volume process — collapse, effusion, or mass — to the left hemithorax, directly explaining the later chest X-ray finding of bilateral lung collapse with a possible right upper lobe cavity and lower mediastinal shift.
  3e. Saddle nose deformity: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: A saddle nose deformity raises a separate branch (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis, or severe chronic infection such as TB/leprosy) that is not clearly reconciled with the eventual malignancy-focused diagnosis in the visible text — this is a finding that appears under-followed-up.
  3f. Distended abdomen with shifting dullness, distended abdominal veins, mild splenomegaly, and an examiner's own query "? Portal hypertension": Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: The exam itself flags a possible portal hypertension picture, but the working diagnosis list does not include a hepatic/portal explanation, and no follow-up (liver function tests, ultrasound Doppler) for this specific branch is visible in the extractable text.
  Findings listed but not followed up: The saddle nose deformity and the examiner's own "?Portal hypertension" query are both raised but not visibly reconciled with the final diagnosis in the extractable text.
  System 1 vs System 2: The left supraclavicular node combined with weight loss and hemoptysis likely triggered rapid System 1 recognition of malignancy. The coexistence of cachexia, saddle nose, and a possible portal hypertension picture required System 2 reasoning to avoid anchoring solely on the pulmonary malignancy branch — this multi-system complexity is a case where the DRM highlights several open branches even after the working diagnosis was reached.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. ABG: pH 7.24, PCO2 32 mmHg, PO2 79.3 mmHg, HCO3 13.2 mmol/L: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This shows a metabolic acidosis with partial respiratory compensation (low pH, low HCO3, low-normal PCO2), consistent with the acute GI losses (vomiting/diarrhea) and/or sepsis, and confirms this is a physiologically significant acute-on-chronic presentation.
  4b. 2D echo: moderate TR with pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH), mild MR/AR, LAD and RCA/LCx hypokinesia, moderate LV dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction, no pericardial effusion: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Regional wall motion abnormalities (LAD and RCA/LCx territory hypokinesia) plus PAH support a component of ischemic and/or pulmonary-hypertension-driven cardiac dysfunction (consistent with the "HFrEF" component of the working diagnosis), separate from the pulmonary mass process.
  4c. USG: bilateral grade 1 renal parenchymal disease (RPD) changes: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Mild, non-specific renal changes; this does not identify a dominant new pathology and mainly serves as background renal status.
  4d. Chest X-ray: bilateral lung collapse with a possible cavitary lesion in the right upper lobe, lower mediastinal shift to the right, inwardly pulled right costophrenic angle: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This confirms and extends the exam-based localization (Node 3d) and directly supports pursuing tissue diagnosis of a mass lesion, while the cavitary component keeps a TB/abscess branch open alongside malignancy.
  4e. RFT (Day 2): urea 145 mg/dl, creatinine 1.9 mg/dl, uric acid 10.4 mg/dl, calcium 8.6 mg/dl, phosphorus 3.4 mg/dl, sodium 136 mEq/l, potassium 4.1 mEq/l, chloride 102 mEq/l: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: The elevated urea/creatinine and markedly elevated uric acid raise a branch for either acute kidney injury (from volume depletion given the vomiting/diarrhea) or a tumour-lysis-adjacent/high-cell-turnover process; the source text does not explicitly reconcile which mechanism is operative.
  4f. Viral/hepatitis screen: Anti-HCV negative, HBsAg negative, HIV 1/2 negative, Dengue negative: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Systematically closes several infectious branches (viral hepatitis, HIV, dengue) as contributors, narrowing the differential toward the malignancy/TB axis suggested by imaging and exam.
  4g. HRCT thorax, lung biopsy (mass in left upper lobe, 4th ICS), and cytology/histology images referenced but not extractable as text: Decision Point: Cannot be fully classified — the HRCT findings and biopsy result descriptions are embedded as images and not extractable as text.
  4h. Staging "T3N3M0" documented explicitly in text: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: This confirms a diagnosis of malignancy with locally advanced nodal involvement (N3) reached via the biopsy, directly validating the pathway from the left supraclavicular node and chest imaging findings toward a bronchogenic carcinoma diagnosis, though the specific histological subtype is not stated in extractable text.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3f — The examiner's own "?Portal hypertension" query (shifting dullness, mild splenomegaly, distended abdominal veins) that is never visibly reconciled with the final diagnosis.
  Why: In a patient with three already-established parallel diagnoses (malignancy, cardiac dysfunction, GI infection), a fourth possible process flagged by the examining team themselves but left unaddressed is the clearest example in this case of a decision point that should have generated its own explicit branch (liver function tests, abdominal ultrasound with Doppler) and closure, rather than being absorbed into the existing diagnostic narrative.
