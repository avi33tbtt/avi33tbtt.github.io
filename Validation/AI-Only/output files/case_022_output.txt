DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — 54-year-old man with fever, headache, and recurrent GTCS (generalized tonic-clonic seizures)
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Seizures since the day before presentation, on a background of fever (6 days prior, low-grade, intermittent with chills) followed by severe headache with blurring of vision and diplopia, then multiple GTCS episodes with tongue bite and unconsciousness.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: A febrile prodrome followed within days by severe headache with visual symptoms and then new-onset seizures in a diabetic, hypertensive adult over-50 is a classic sequence for a CNS infective/inflammatory process (encephalitis/meningoencephalitis) and should immediately activate that pattern rather than being read as a primary seizure disorder.

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Fever x2 days, low-grade, intermittent, with chills, relieved by medication (day 1–2): Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: An antecedent febrile illness preceding neurological symptoms by only a few days strongly favours an infective CNS process over a purely metabolic or vascular seizure cause, and should shift pre-test probability toward encephalitis/meningitis from the outset.
  2b. Severe headache with blurring of vision and diplopia (day 2 of fever): Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: New severe headache with binocular visual disturbance (diplopia, blurring) occurring during a febrile illness is a red-flag combination for raised intracranial pressure or CNS infection/inflammation, and by itself should have triggered urgent neuroimaging and CSF evaluation consideration well before the seizure onset.
  2c. Progression to recurrent GTCS (multiple episodes across several days and facilities) with tongue bite, unconsciousness, and eventual referral: Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: The clustering of seizures (cluster/near-status pattern) confirms a significant acute symptomatic process rather than an isolated event, and closes off "single unprovoked seizure" as the framing, though the underlying cause remains open at this point.
  2d. No urinary/bowel incontinence, no vomiting or headache immediately after episodes documented at the time of this hospital's admission; no head injury, ear discharge, neck stiffness, or photophobia: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: Absence of neck stiffness and photophobia somewhat lowers (but, given a negative Kernig's sign noted later and known low sensitivity of meningeal signs, does not exclude) probability of meningitis specifically, while still being compatible with encephalitis, which often lacks prominent meningismus.
  2e. Known diabetic (15 years, on metformin + glimepiride) and hypertensive (3 years, on atenolol); occasional alcohol; ex-smoker (stopped 20 years back): Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Long-standing diabetes both raises the risk of a metabolic/hyperglycaemic seizure trigger (relevant given the admission GRBS of 335 mg/dl) and raises risk of cerebrovascular disease and infection susceptibility — this single feature keeps at least three mechanistic pathways (infective, metabolic, vascular) simultaneously live and needed explicit disambiguation.
  2f. Outside CT Brain reported "chronic lacunar infarcts in lentiform nucleus": Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: Chronic (not acute) lacunar infarcts on CT lower the probability that this admission's seizures are directly caused by a new acute stroke, redirecting weight back toward an infective/metabolic acute precipitant rather than the chronic vascular finding.
  Single most important history feature: 2b (severe headache with diplopia/blurring of vision preceding the seizures by roughly a day) — it is the feature most specific for a CNS structural/infective process and is the clearest documented pivot point toward the eventual diagnosis of viral encephalitis.
  Missing history: No documented vaccination history, no travel or vector-exposure history (relevant for assessing viral encephalitis risk, e.g., arboviral causes common in this region), no documented recent respiratory or other systemic infective symptoms preceding the fever, and no clear timeline correlation between the GRBS trend and the seizure episodes across the several days before this admission.

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. GCS 15/15, conscious/coherent/cooperative at this admission despite the referenced history of multiple prior seizure episodes and documented unconsciousness during earlier episodes: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: A normal GCS between episodes is consistent with a post-ictal recovery pattern rather than an ongoing depressed sensorium/encephalopathic state at the time of this specific examination, though it does not rule out an underlying encephalitic process, which can wax and wane.
  3b. GRBS 335 mg/dl on admission: Decision Point: Type Bifurcating — Insight: Significant hyperglycaemia could itself be a seizure-provoking metabolic derangement or a stress response to the acute illness/seizures — the map cannot, from the fetched text, determine which direction the causality runs, and this ambiguity is appropriately reflected in the final diagnosis listing "diabetic nephropathy" separately rather than asserting hyperglycaemia as the seizure cause.
  3c. Motor system: power 5/5 upper limbs bilaterally, 4/4 lower limbs bilaterally; reflexes globally reduced (1+ throughout) with flexor plantars; cranial nerves normal; sensory and cerebellar systems intact: Decision Point: Type Missed — Insight: Bilateral lower-limb weakness (4/5) is a positive focal-ish finding that is not explicitly explained in the text — it could reflect post-ictal (Todd's) paresis, a pre-existing deficit, or something else, but no interpretive comment is recorded, leaving this decision point unresolved in the documented reasoning.
  3d. Kernig's sign negative: Decision Point: Type Closing — Insight: A negative Kernig's sign lowers (without excluding, given its known low sensitivity) probability of bacterial meningitis specifically, consistent with the eventual diagnosis favouring encephalitis over meningitis.
  System 1 vs System 2: The fever→headache→visual symptoms→seizures sequence, together with a negative Kernig's sign, plausibly triggered System 1 pattern recognition toward "viral encephalitis" in an experienced clinician. The bilateral 4/5 lower-limb weakness required deliberate System 2 follow-up that is not shown as having occurred in the fetched text.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  4a. Outside CT Brain: chronic lacunar infarcts in lentiform nucleus (text-confirmed): Decision Point: Type Closing (partial) — Insight: As above, this reframes the vascular finding as an incidental chronic change rather than the acute cause of this presentation.
  4b. In-hospital investigation panels (three embedded image blocks) and an ophthalmology referral (embedded images, findings not extractable as text): Decision Point: Type Pivotal (implied) — Insight: These likely include the results (e.g., CSF analysis, MRI brain, serum electrolytes/renal/liver panels, HbA1c) that support the multi-part final diagnosis, but no specific numeric or descriptive values are extractable from the fetched text apart from the two serum potassium values documented in the SOAP notes. This is flagged as a data-availability gap, not a fabricated finding.
  4c. Serum potassium 2.7 mmol/L (26/2) and 2.8 mmol/L (27/2), text-confirmed: Decision Point: Type Pivotal — Insight: Significant hypokalaemia is directly documented and explains its inclusion in the final diagnosis list; hypokalaemia can itself lower seizure threshold and cause muscular weakness, offering a plausible alternative or contributing explanation for the bilateral lower-limb weakness noted in Node 3c that is not explicitly cross-referenced in the text.
  4d. Diagnosis line lists hypoproteinemia, diabetic nephropathy, and diabetic retinopathy in addition to viral encephalitis and hypokalemia: Decision Point: Type Pivotal (implied) — Insight: These four additional diagnoses (hypoproteinemia, diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy) are stated as final diagnoses but their supporting values (serum albumin/total protein, renal function/urine albumin, fundoscopy/ophthalmology findings) are not present as extractable text — the ophthalmology referral image likely supports the retinopathy diagnosis but cannot be confirmed from this fetch.
  Unused/unclear results: The bilateral lower-limb weakness (4/5) noted on examination is never explicitly reconciled with any of the five final diagnoses (encephalitis, hypokalaemia, hypoproteinemia, diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy) in the visible text.
  Missing investigations (per available text): No CSF analysis result, no MRI/CT brain result from this admission, and no serum albumin/renal function values are present as text in the fetched page, despite being necessary to support the stated diagnoses.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: Generalized tonic-clonic seizures secondary to viral encephalitis, hypokalemia, hypoproteinemia, diabetic nephropathy, and diabetic retinopathy.
  Point of closure: Stated after the investigation image block and ophthalmology referral, combining multiple contributing diagnoses rather than a single cause.
  Closure type: Analytical — this is a multi-factorial synthesis (infective + metabolic + microvascular diabetic complications) rather than a single pattern-match closure, which is appropriate given the febrile-neurological presentation in a long-standing diabetic; however, from the fetched text alone the specific evidence for encephalitis (CSF/MRI) and for the two diabetic microvascular diagnoses (renal function, fundoscopy) is not visible.
  Open branches at closure: (1) the specific CSF/imaging evidence for "viral encephalitis" versus an alternative CNS infective/inflammatory process is not shown in text; (2) the cause and clinical significance of the bilateral 4/5 lower-limb weakness is never explicitly addressed against any of the five diagnoses; (3) whether the hyperglycaemia (GRBS 335) and hypokalaemia are causally linked (e.g., osmotic diuresis, treatment effect) or independent findings is not discussed.
  Synthesis insight: The team reached a reasonable multi-diagnosis synthesis that plausibly explains the febrile-neurological presentation plus known diabetic complications, but the reasoning chain connecting each specific diagnosis to its supporting evidence is not fully visible in the fetched text — this is the map's main audit-relevant gap, distinct from the mechanistically well-supported hypokalaemia diagnosis, which is directly evidenced by the reported lab values.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 1/2f — The outside CT finding of "chronic lacunar infarcts" could have anchored early reasoning toward a vascular/stroke-related seizure cause before the fever-headache-visual-symptom sequence was fully weighted; the final diagnosis correctly moves past this anchor toward an infective cause, but the map notes this as a point where anchoring was a live risk.
  Availability: Not clearly identifiable from the fetched text — insufficient information to flag with confidence.
  Premature closure: Not strongly evidenced — the five-part final diagnosis suggests the team did not prematurely collapse to a single cause; if anything, the unresolved lower-limb weakness (3c) suggests a finding that was under-integrated rather than a case that closed too early on the wrong single diagnosis.
  Framing: Node 1 — The outside-hospital referral framing (repeated seizures, prior CT with lacunar infarcts) may have primed the receiving team to weight the vascular finding before the infective-prodrome sequence was reconstructed.
  Omission bias: Node 3c/4 — The bilateral 4/5 lower-limb weakness does not appear to have prompted a specifically documented follow-up (e.g., repeat neurological exam, spinal imaging, correlation with potassium trend) in the fetched text.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 3c (bilateral lower-limb weakness never reconciled with any final diagnosis)
  Why: In a case with five listed final diagnoses, a positive focal-ish examination finding that is not mapped to any of them is exactly the kind of loose thread most likely to represent either an under-recognised sixth process or a documentation gap. Building the habit of explicitly reconciling every positive examination finding against the final diagnosis list — even when the list is already long and multifactorial — is the transferable reasoning lesson here.
