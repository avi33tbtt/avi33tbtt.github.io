DIAGNOSTIC REASONING MAP — Case 32: 16M with right LMN facial palsy (House-Brackmann grade 4)
Date: 2026-08-17
Learner: N/A
Module: DRM — Diagnostic Reasoning Map

NODE 1 — Presenting Complaint
  Finding: Deviation of mouth (to left) x5 days; inability to close right eyelid x5 days, with a 12-hour gap between the two symptoms' onset.
  Decision Point: Yes — Type: Pivotal
  Insight: An acute, isolated unilateral facial droop with inability to close the ipsilateral eye in an otherwise well adolescent should immediately activate the pattern for a peripheral (LMN) facial nerve palsy — most commonly idiopathic (Bell's palsy) — and should trigger a mental checklist to actively exclude other CN VII branch involvement, ear pathology, and any signs suggesting a central (UMN) or systemic cause before defaulting to "idiopathic."

NODE 2 — History
  2a. Difficulty chewing food: Decision Point: Bifurcating — Insight: Chewing difficulty could reflect either weakness of facial/buccinator muscles (consistent with peripheral CN VII palsy, causing food to pool in the cheek) or, less likely, a trigeminal (CN V) motor problem — the exam finding that all cranial nerves are intact except CN VII effectively resolves this in favor of the facial-nerve explanation, but the history alone leaves it open.
  2b. History of cold exposure (slept outside) x10 days before onset: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: Cold/exposure history is a classically cited (though not definitively proven) precipitant for idiopathic Bell's palsy; it is documented here but not explicitly used in the case's reasoning to support the idiopathic label over alternative causes.
  2c. No fever, cold, sore throat, loss of taste, decreased salivation/lacrimation: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Absence of taste loss and normal salivation/lacrimation argues against a lesion proximal to the chorda tympani/geniculate ganglion (which would affect taste and secretomotor fibers), supporting a distal (or at least non-proximal) facial nerve lesion — consistent with a typical idiopathic presentation rather than one complicated by more proximal nerve involvement.
  2d. No ear infection, discharge, or decreased hearing: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: This appropriately lowers the probability of otogenic causes of facial palsy (e.g., cholesteatoma, acute/chronic otitis media, or a mastoid process compressing the nerve), supporting an idiopathic (Bell's) working diagnosis over a structural/infective one.
  2e. No history of upper/lower limb weakness; no significant past history; no known allergies: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: Absence of limb weakness argues against a broader peripheral neuropathy or Guillain-Barré-spectrum process (which can present with bilateral or progressive facial diplegia plus limb involvement); a clean past history reduces the pretest probability of a recurrent or syndromic facial palsy.
  Missing history: No explicit documentation of a vesicular rash in or around the ear (needed to exclude Ramsay Hunt syndrome/herpes zoster oticus, a key mimic of Bell's palsy); no travel history or tick-exposure history (relevant for Lyme disease, an important cause of facial palsy in endemic areas); no family history of recurrent facial palsy (relevant to rare syndromic causes).

NODE 3 — Examination
  3a. All cranial nerves intact except CN VII: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: An isolated CN VII deficit without involvement of adjacent nerves (V, VI, VIII, IX) argues strongly against a cerebellopontine-angle or brainstem lesion, which would typically produce additional cranial neuropathies, and supports a peripheral, extra-axial facial nerve process.
  3b. Right-sided motor findings — absent eyebrow raise, loss of forehead wrinkles, inability to close right eye, mouth deviated to left, unable to blow out cheeks or whistle, involving the forehead: Decision Point: Pivotal — Insight: Forehead involvement (loss of wrinkling, absent eyebrow raise) on the same side as the lower facial weakness is the single most important localizing sign in this case — it confirms a lower motor neuron (peripheral) lesion rather than an upper motor neuron (central/stroke-type) facial palsy, because UMN lesions characteristically spare the forehead due to bilateral cortical innervation of the frontalis muscle.
  3c. No loss of taste sensation (anterior 2/3 tongue); lacrimation and salivation unaffected: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: As with the history, intact taste and secretomotor function place the lesion at or distal to the point where the chorda tympani and greater petrosal nerve fibers branch off, consistent with a distal, uncomplicated peripheral facial nerve lesion.
  3d. Reflexes — corneal and conjunctival reflexes present bilaterally, abdominal and plantar reflexes normal/flexor, jaw jerk present, deep tendon reflexes 1+ symmetric, power 5/5 in all limbs, normal tone: Decision Point: Closing — Insight: A fully intact reflex and motor exam outside the facial nerve distribution strongly argues against any broader central or peripheral neurological process, closing off differentials like Guillain-Barré variants, brainstem stroke, or a mass lesion.
  System 1 nodes: forehead involvement + isolated CN VII deficit → LMN facial (Bell's) palsy (rapid, appropriately applied pattern match).
  System 2 nodes: the analytical step of using taste/lacrimation/salivation sparing to localize the lesion along the facial nerve's course is present in the exam findings but not explicitly narrated as a localization exercise in the case text — this is implicit reasoning that a learner should be asked to make explicit.

NODE 4 — Investigations
  Investigations ordered and results: None are documented in this case — no audiometry, no imaging (MRI/CT), no serology (e.g., for Lyme or varicella-zoster), no blood counts or inflammatory markers are recorded.
  4a. Absence of any investigation being ordered: Decision Point: Missed — Insight: In a case with clinical features strongly supportive of idiopathic Bell's palsy (isolated CN VII, no red-flag findings), the decision *not* to investigate further is itself a defensible decision point reflecting reliance on clinical diagnosis — appropriate practice for typical, uncomplicated Bell's palsy — but this reasoning ("clinical diagnosis is sufficient given the absence of atypical features") is never made explicit in the case record.
  Unused results: None (no investigations were performed).
  Missing investigations: No documentation of why imaging was deemed unnecessary (which would normally be reserved for atypical features — bilateral palsy, other cranial neuropathies, slowly progressive course, or a mass on exam — none of which are present here, arguably justifying the omission); no serologic workup for Lyme disease if the case occurred in or the patient had travel to an endemic area (not stated); no audiometry to formally document hearing status despite "no decreased hearing" being reported subjectively.

NODE 5 — Diagnostic Synthesis
  Working diagnosis reached: House-Brackmann grade 4 LMN facial palsy (right-sided).
  At what point reached: Immediately following the full history and cranial nerve/general examination, with no further investigations obtained before diagnosis and treatment.
  Closure type: Pattern-recognition closure (System 1), appropriately reinforced by systematic negative findings (System 2 confirmation) — the isolated CN VII deficit with forehead involvement and no red-flag features is a genuinely low-uncertainty clinical picture, and closure here is reasonably warranted rather than premature.
  Open branches at closure: Herpes zoster oticus/Ramsay Hunt syndrome (not excluded by explicit documentation of the external ear/vesicles); Lyme disease (not addressed, relevant depending on geography/exposure); a purely idiopathic ("Bell's palsy") label was not explicitly used in the case's own diagnosis line (it stops at "LMN Facial palsy" with a House-Brackmann grade), leaving the etiology formally unlabeled even though the clinical picture is classic for idiopathic Bell's palsy.
  Synthesis insight: The severity grading (House-Brackmann IV) is a meaningful and appropriately applied objective scale that adds prognostic value beyond a binary "palsy present" diagnosis, but the case stops short of committing to (or explicitly excluding) an etiology, which matters because it determines whether steroids/antivirals were indicated — the treatment plan documented (Zincovit, physiotherapy, eye protection) notably does not include corticosteroids, which are the standard of care for acute Bell's palsy when started within 72 hours, raising a question about whether the 5-day-old presentation fell outside the steroid-benefit window or whether steroids were simply not considered.

BIAS AUDIT OVERLAY
  Anchoring: Node 3b — The forehead-sparing vs. forehead-involvement teaching point was likely applied correctly and early, appropriately anchoring the team to a peripheral rather than central lesion; no evidence of harmful anchoring in this case.
  Availability: Node 5 — Bell's palsy is the most common and clinically "available" cause of acute isolated facial palsy in a young patient, which may have led the team to close the case without explicitly considering or documenting exclusion of less common mimics (Ramsay Hunt, Lyme), even though the final workup pattern is consistent with what is typically done for classic Bell's palsy.
  Premature closure: Node 4 — No investigations were pursued at all; while this may be entirely appropriate clinical practice for a classic presentation, the case does not document the explicit reasoning ("no red flags, therefore purely clinical diagnosis is appropriate") that would distinguish deliberate, justified closure from unreflective closure.
  Framing: Node 1 — The blog's own title ("16 y/o male with LMN Facial Palsy") pre-frames the case with the diagnosis already assigned, which is a documentation-level framing effect rather than a real-time clinical bias, but worth noting for anyone using this log to practice diagnosis "blind."
  Search satisficing: Node 4 — Once the isolated CN VII pattern was recognized, no further diagnostic search (labs, imaging, ENT exam of the external canal for vesicles) was pursued — reasonable for classic Bell's palsy, but the case gives no explicit statement that atypical features were actively sought and excluded before stopping the search.

HIGHEST-YIELD DECISION POINT
  Node: 5 — the decision to treat without explicitly naming (or excluding) an etiology, and without corticosteroids.
  Why: The single most transferable lesson from this case is that "LMN facial palsy" is a localization, not a diagnosis — the reasoning pathway should explicitly proceed from localization (peripheral CN VII lesion, correctly established here) to etiology (idiopathic/Bell's vs. Ramsay Hunt vs. Lyme vs. other), because the etiology directly determines whether time-sensitive treatments (oral corticosteroids, ideally within 72 hours of onset, +/- antivirals for suspected zoster) are indicated. Earlier and more explicit engagement with this step — even to conclude "idiopathic, and outside the steroid window" — would have made the reasoning pathway complete rather than stopping at localization.
