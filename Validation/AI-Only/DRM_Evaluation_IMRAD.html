Caveat on methodology. The DRM outputs, their auditor evaluations, and this cross-case evaluation summary were all generated using the same underlying LLM (Claude), across separate, independent sessions and by multiple different users. No independent (non-LLM, or differently-modeled) verification layer was used at any stage. Findings should be read with this in mind, and independent human or cross-model validation is recommended before drawing strong conclusions about real-world reliability.
Abstract. Twenty-four outputs of an automated Diagnostic Reasoning Map (DRM) generator were independently audited against re-fetched primary source case text. Scoring covered five reasoning nodes (presenting complaint, history, examination, investigations, diagnostic synthesis), a bias-detection overlay, and a fabrication check. The generator achieved near-ceiling scores in the large majority of cases (3 perfect scores; ~79% at ≥90% of maximum), with only one critical fabrication flag across the entire sample. Residual errors clustered around temporal precision and completeness rather than invented clinical content, indicating a narrow, tractable target for improvement.

Introduction

Diagnostic Reasoning Maps (DRMs) are structured reconstructions of a clinical case's reasoning trajectory — from presenting complaint through history, examination, investigations, to diagnostic synthesis — intended to make implicit clinical reasoning explicit and auditable. Because DRMs are generated automatically from unstructured case notes, two risks dominate: (1) fabrication — asserting findings, values, or events not present in the source, and (2) premature or unsupported diagnostic closure — resolving genuinely open cases into false certainty. This evaluation set out to determine how reliably the DRM generator reconstructs case reasoning without introducing either failure mode, and to characterize any recurring weaknesses across a diverse case sample.

Methods

Data availability: primary case notes are indexed at the case links page above; raw per-case auditor evaluation files are available in the linked GitHub repository (Validation/AI-Only); the DRM generation protocol/prompt is documented in Module 50.

Twenty-four DRM outputs were each independently audited by re-fetching the primary source case note and scoring the DRM against that source (not against the DRM's own internal claims). Each case was scored across five reasoning nodes:

  1. Presenting Complaint
  2. History
  3. Examination
  4. Investigations
  5. Diagnostic Synthesis

Each node received a 0–2 score for decision-point classification accuracy and a 0–2 score for insight correctness (most cases: 5 nodes × up to 2 sub-scores → composite out of 40 or normalized to 10). In addition, auditors applied:

  • A bias overlay check (anchoring, availability, framing, omission bias) — rated correct / overcalled.
  • A binary fabrication flag (Y/N), with critical fabrications reported separately from the composite score per protocol, rather than averaged in.
  • A missed-data flag assessing whether claimed gaps in the source were genuine.

Case topics spanned encephalitis, hepatic encephalopathy/cirrhosis, anemia workups, stroke, sepsis, hyponatremia, and other general-medicine presentations, providing reasonable diagnostic heterogeneity.

Recurring strengths

  • Verbatim fidelity on history, exam, and investigation panels across nearly all cases.
  • Honest gap-reporting — e.g., case_002 logs an undocumented TB-contact history as a gap rather than asserting a negative; case_020 hedges image-only lab values instead of stating exact numbers.
  • Resistance to premature closure — cases 002, 003, 005, and 010 correctly decline to synthesize a final diagnosis where the source itself leaves the case open.
  • Genuine contradiction-catching (not manufactured) — e.g., case_003's catch of "higher mental functions intact" vs. "not oriented to time/place/person" within the same note.
  • Reliable bias-overlay tagging — anchoring, availability, and framing biases rated "correct" in essentially every case reviewed.

Notable individual cases

  • case_017 (40/40): cleanest reconstruction in the sample; catches a real chief-complaint-vs-exam discrepancy and clearly labels inferences as such.
  • case_020, case_023 (10/10): fully grounded, verbatim-verified reconstructions with only trivial completeness notes.
  • case_016 (31/40, weakest): the DRM's own stated timeline ("~2–3 weeks") conflicts with the source's explicit "5 months back," and a 2-month interim dialysis course is omitted entirely.
  • case_019 (flagged separately): otherwise strong output, but contains an unsupported claim about Artesunate start timing — reported outside the composite average per protocol as the sample's sole critical fabrication.

Discussion

Limitations. The most significant limitation of this evaluation is methodological rather than clinical: the DRM outputs, the per-case auditor evaluations, and this summary report were all produced by the same LLM (Claude), run across different sessions and by multiple users, but without an independently-sourced or cross-model check at any stage. This means the "auditor" and the "generator" share the same underlying model family, training data, and potential blind spots — an error pattern the model is prone to could plausibly be missed by an audit performed by the same model, even in a fresh session. The consistently high scores and low fabrication rate reported here should therefore be interpreted as a measure of internal self-consistency and protocol adherence, not as a substitute for independent clinical or cross-model validation.

Across 24 heterogeneous cases, the DRM generator demonstrates strong reconstruction fidelity and — notably — good epistemic discipline: it resists inventing diagnoses, outcomes, or lab results even when a source case is genuinely unresolved. The single critical fabrication (case_019) and the small cluster of soft notes are concentrated almost entirely in temporal and administrative precision (dates, durations, counts) rather than in clinical substance, suggesting the underlying reasoning process is sound but under-constrained on exact timing arithmetic and enumeration tasks.

The bias-detection overlay performed reliably, and the generator's occasional self-flagging of its own inferences (e.g., case_017, case_011) shows the capability exists — the inconsistency (e.g., case_013 missing an analogous ambiguity to one caught in case_011) suggests this behavior is not yet fully systematized across runs.

Recommendations

  1. Add an explicit date/timing cross-check step before finalizing synthesis nodes.
  2. Standardize investigation/image counting via a mechanical recount pass.
  3. Enforce a consistent "flag your own inferences" checklist per node.
  4. Introduce a dedicated confidence/provenance tag distinguishing "explicitly documented" from "inferred" values.
  5. Run a final completeness sweep confirming every history/complaint line in the source was used.

Conclusion

The DRM generator is a high-fidelity, well-hedged reasoning system with a low true-fabrication rate (1 in 24 cases) and strong diagnostic-synthesis judgment, including the harder skill of not forcing false closure. Remaining error mass is narrow and tractable — concentrated in timing precision and completeness rather than invented clinical content.
