20-Year-Old Woman with Markedly Elevated Blood Pressure — Young-Onset Hypertension Under Evaluation

Source case log: https://102-crazymandadi.blogspot.com/2021/11/young-onset-hypertension.html

Case Narrative

A 20-year-old woman presented to the outpatient department after being found to have persistently elevated blood pressure. A year earlier, during an episode of fever, she had been noted to have a blood pressure of 170/90 mmHg, which she attributed to the fever at the time and did not pursue further. Two months before presentation she received a COVID-19 vaccination, followed by fever and chills that resolved the next day. Five days before presentation, a routine check-up again showed markedly elevated blood pressure at 180/110 mmHg, prompting her family to bring her in for evaluation.

She denied reduced urine output, polyuria, polydipsia, or muscle weakness suggestive of an endocrine cause, and had no personal history of diabetes, hypertension, asthma, tuberculosis, epilepsy, or chronic kidney disease prior to this presentation. Her family history was notable for hypertension in both her father and grandfather.

On examination she was well, without pallor, cyanosis, icterus, lymphadenopathy, or edema. Vitals showed a temperature of 98.2°F, pulse 102/min, and respiratory rate 16/min, with SpO2 98% on room air. Four-limb blood pressure measurement was markedly abnormal and asymmetric — 180/110 mmHg in the left upper limb, 160/110 mmHg in the right upper limb, and strikingly higher readings in the lower limbs at 260/130 mmHg on the left and 190/130 mmHg on the right. Cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, and neurological examinations were otherwise unremarkable.

Given her young age, the markedly elevated and asymmetric four-limb blood pressures, and a family history of hypertension, the case was carried forward as de novo hypertension of young onset requiring further evaluation — with the limb blood-pressure discrepancy in particular warranting work-up for a secondary or vascular cause rather than assuming primary (essential) hypertension.
