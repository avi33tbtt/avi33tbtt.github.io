54-Year-Old Man with Chest Pain, Fever, and Left Upper Lobe Pneumonia — Suspected Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 54-year-old man presented with ten days of left-sided chest pain, breathlessness, and cough with expectoration. The chest pain was pricking in character, sudden in onset, non-radiating, and worsened with exertion; his breathlessness was graded as moderate-to-severe (grade 3), and his cough produced yellow, non-bloody, non-foul-smelling sputum. He also had high-grade intermittent fever with chills and rigors and body aches over the same period, partially relieved with medication. Notably, he reported weight loss and evening rise of temperature over the preceding two to three weeks — a classic constitutional symptom pattern that, combined with a persistent upper-lobe respiratory focus, raised strong suspicion for pulmonary tuberculosis alongside or underlying an acute pneumonic process. He denied palpitations, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, burning micturition, loose stools, or reduced urine output, and had no prior history of diabetes, hypertension, stroke, asthma, epilepsy, or tuberculosis.

On examination he was afebrile at the time of assessment with stable vitals, and general examination showed no pallor, icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, koilonychia, or generalized edema. Respiratory examination localized the abnormality to the left upper zone: chest expansion was reduced, vocal fremitus was decreased over the left suprascapular region, and auscultation confirmed reduced breath sounds in the same area without added sounds — a focal finding consistent with upper lobe consolidation or cavitation, both patterns classically associated with reactivation tuberculosis. Cardiac, abdominal, and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

He was started empirically on broad-spectrum antibiotics (augmentin and azithromycin), gastric protection, bronchodilator nebulization, and symptomatic antipyretic and mucolytic support while the working diagnosis of pneumonia secondary to suspected pulmonary tuberculosis was being worked up.

Outcome: He remained on empirical antibiotic and supportive respiratory therapy through the documented days of the admission, with a provisional diagnosis of pneumonia secondary to pulmonary tuberculosis; the source record ends before confirmatory tuberculosis testing results or a final outcome were documented.
