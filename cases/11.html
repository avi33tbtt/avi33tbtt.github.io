41-Year-Old Man with High Coloured Urine and Pain Abdomen — Acute Pancreatitis with Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 41-year-old man presented to casualty with high-coloured urine, present intermittently for two years, and abdominal pain for two days. His history stretched back several years: about six years earlier he was found to have a ureteric calculus at a private hospital (no records available), and by three years before this admission he was having on-and-off episodes of high-coloured urine roughly twice weekly. He was diagnosed with hypertension around that time but took medication for only about a week on each occasion he was prescribed it, including at a checkup the following year — a pattern of poor treatment adherence that likely contributed to his subsequent renal picture. He had also been hospitalized with a similar presentation six months prior. His abdominal pain on this admission was diffuse, continuous, and squeezing, worsened by eating, without associated loose stools, vomiting, or fever.

His personal history included heavy alcohol use — about 120 mL daily for the past year — and a remote history of smoking a decade earlier. He had undergone cholecystectomy a year before this admission. On examination he was conscious and cooperative, markedly hypertensive at 180/120 mmHg with a heart rate of 110 bpm, and his abdomen showed epigastric and bilateral hypochondrial tenderness with guarding in the epigastrium and right hypochondrium, but no rigidity — findings consistent with acute pancreatitis, for which he was started on supportive management including IV fluids, gastric protection, antiemetics, vitamin support, analgesia, and nasogastric (Ryle's tube) decompression.

Over the following days his abdominal pain gradually subsided and he was transitioned to oral fluids. However, by the fourth day his urinalysis revealed plenty of red blood cells, marked proteinuria, and plenty of pus cells, and he continued to report the longstanding high-coloured urine. This shifted the diagnostic picture: alongside resolving pancreatitis, he was now recognized as having a significant underlying urinary tract/renal process. He was started on antibiotics for a presumed urinary tract component and an antihypertensive, and by the fifth day his burning micturition had improved somewhat, though hematuria persisted. His diagnosis was ultimately framed as acute pancreatitis (resolved) with chronic glomerulonephritis — reflecting his years-long history of hematuria, poorly controlled hypertension, and now-confirmed active urinary sediment.

Outcome: His acute pancreatitis resolved with supportive treatment over the admission, while the persistent hematuria, proteinuria, and pyuria led to a working diagnosis of chronic glomerulonephritis; further nephrology follow-up outcome was not documented in the source.
