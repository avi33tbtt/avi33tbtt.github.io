Case 66 — 31-Year-Old Man with Drug-Induced Hepatitis Secondary to Borderline Lepromatous Leprosy (Dapsone Syndrome)

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 31-year-old photographer presented with four days of yellowish discoloration of the eyes, two days of fever that was insidious in onset and subsided with medication, and two days of non-bilious, non-projectile vomiting with food content, having sought care at a local hospital first.

His background was notable: seventeen years earlier, his mother had noticed a hypopigmented patch on his cheek along with multiple hypopigmented patches elsewhere on his body, which was initially dismissed as a vitamin deficiency. Fifteen days before this presentation, a new hypopigmented patch appeared on his right arm, prompting evaluation at a local hospital where he was diagnosed with Hansen's disease (leprosy) and started on multidrug therapy — a regimen that characteristically includes dapsone. His current presentation, with fever and jaundice appearing in close temporal relation to starting antileprosy therapy, raised concern for a drug reaction to this treatment. He denied breathlessness, palpitations, headache, orthopnea, or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and was non-alcoholic and non-smoking.

On examination he was conscious, coherent, and oriented, febrile at 99.9°F, with a blood pressure of 130/80 mmHg and pulse of 83/min. He had marked pallor and icterus but no cyanosis, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal examination revealed mild splenomegaly with normal bowel sounds. Neurological examination showed normal tone and full (5/5) power in all limbs with normal deep tendon reflexes. Sensory testing showed a pattern consistent with his known leprosy: intact fine touch, crude touch, pain, and temperature sensation over the forearm and neck lesions, but loss of pain and touch sensation over the lower-limb lesions — a classic finding of peripheral nerve involvement in leprosy affecting cooler, more distally located skin.

Given the combination of jaundice, fever, pallor, and splenomegaly emerging shortly after starting antileprosy therapy, the working differential included drug-induced hepatitis (attributed to the dapsone-containing regimen) alongside a consideration of Wilson's disease given the hepatic and hemolytic-type picture, in the context of his known Hansen's disease. He was managed with hepatoprotective therapy (ursodeoxycholic acid), a non-absorbable antibiotic and lactulose to reduce ammoniagenic load, a proton pump inhibitor, antiemetic cover, and supportive intravenous fluids with vitamin supplementation, with close vital sign monitoring.

Outcome: Managed as drug-induced hepatitis, most likely related to his antileprosy (dapsone-containing) regimen, with hepatoprotective and supportive therapy alongside continued management of his underlying Hansen's disease.
