Case 2 — Fever with Cough and Loose Stools in a Diabetic Man

Source: ashakiran923.blogspot.com — Unit 2 Admission

Case Narrative

A 55-year-old man who worked as a daily wage labourer presented with fever and cough of about 20 days' duration, along with loose stools that had started two days before admission. He had been apparently well until 20 days prior, when he developed a low-grade, continuous fever without an obvious source — no headache, vomiting, burning micturition, or ear discharge to point toward a focus. The fever was accompanied by a cough productive of small amounts of whitish sputum, continuous through the day but without the weight loss or night sweats that might have raised concern for tuberculosis. He also reported chest and abdominal distension. A day before admission, this settled clinical picture was punctuated by five episodes per day of watery, non-bloody loose stools without abdominal pain, suggesting a possible enteric component layered onto the underlying febrile illness.

His background was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus of 20 years' duration, on treatment, and a history of chronic alcohol use (about 90 mL/day) that he had stopped three months earlier. He had no known hypertension, tuberculosis, epilepsy, or cardiovascular disease.

On examination he was afebrile at the time of assessment, with a blood pressure of 100/70 mmHg, pulse 79/min, oxygen saturation 94% on room air, and a random blood glucose of 271 mg/dL — reflecting suboptimal glycaemic control that likely contributed to his vulnerability to infection. He was conscious, coherent, and oriented, with no pallor, icterus, clubbing, cyanosis, lymphadenopathy, or pedal edema. Cardiovascular and respiratory examination were unremarkable, and neurological examination — including higher mental functions, cranial nerves, tone, and reflexes — was normal, arguing against a central process and keeping the differential focused on an infective or metabolic cause for his prolonged fever.

He was worked up for fever of unclear origin, with a broad panel sent to cover common causes of prolonged febrile illness in a diabetic patient from this setting — complete urine examination, fasting and postprandial blood sugars, HbA1c, blood and urine cultures, a malaria parasite strip test, sputum for acid-fast bacilli with culture and sensitivity and Gram staining, liver function tests, and a rapid dengue test. He was started empirically on intravenous pantoprazole, intravenous augmentin, oral paracetamol, and maintenance intravenous fluids while these results were awaited.

The available documentation ends at this point, with the diagnostic workup in progress; the eventual causative diagnosis and clinical outcome are not recorded in the source material.
