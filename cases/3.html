Case 3 — Fever with Altered Sensorium: Meningoencephalitis, Viral vs Tubercular

Source: ashakiran923.blogspot.com — Unit 2 Case

Case Narrative

A 42-year-old woman working as a farm labourer was brought in with fever for five days, altered sensorium for three days, and irrelevant talking for four days. On the day of admission she developed involuntary movements of the right upper limb, which prompted the family to bring her to hospital. She had been apparently well five days earlier, with no prior hospital contact.

The fever was high-grade and intermittent, with chills and rigors, and had partially responded to treatment from a local practitioner. On the first day of fever she had two episodes of vomiting that settled with medication, along with headache while she was still relatively well. From the fourth day, however, she began speaking irrelevantly and talking to herself; she progressively became confused, stopped responding to her family's commands, and her sensorium continued to deteriorate — a trajectory that, together with the headache, vomiting, and fever, pointed toward an evolving central nervous system infection rather than a purely systemic illness. She had no known diabetes, hypertension, asthma, epilepsy, or coronary disease.

On examination she was conscious but with altered, drowsy though arousable sensorium (GCS E4V1M4), pupils reacting to light bilaterally, with no pallor, icterus, clubbing, cyanosis, lymphadenopathy, edema, or koilonychia. Vitals showed a blood pressure of 130/90 mmHg, pulse 94/min, respiratory rate 21/min, oxygen saturation 97% on room air, and a normal blood glucose. Cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal examination were unremarkable. Neurologically, however, she was disoriented to time, place, and person, with hypertonia in all four limbs, exaggerated reflexes in both upper and lower limbs, and possible neck stiffness, though other meningeal signs could not be reliably elicited because of the hypertonia — a constellation strongly suggestive of an encephalitic process with meningeal involvement.

Investigations included a full hemogram, urine examination, ECG, MRI brain, fundoscopy (which showed no features of raised intracranial tension), ultrasound, hepatitis B and HIV screening, anti-HCV, liver and renal function tests, and arterial blood gas. A lumbar puncture was performed, with CSF analysis showing a lymphocyte-predominant pleocytosis (10% neutrophils, 90% lymphocytes) with normal sugar, protein, and chloride — a pattern more in keeping with a viral or, alternatively, an early tubercular process than an acute bacterial meningitis. CSF, blood, and urine cultures were sent, along with a thyroid profile and serial fever charting. The working diagnosis at this stage was viral encephalitis versus metabolic encephalopathy.

She was started on intravenous pantoprazole, ondansetron, ceftriaxone, paracetamol for fever spikes, phenytoin for seizure prophylaxis, optineuron, ryles tube feeds for nutrition, maintenance intravenous fluids, mannitol and dexamethasone for presumed cerebral edema. By day 2, she had one fever spike and no improvement in sensorium, with hypertonia now more pronounced on the left than the right and an extensor plantar response, prompting the addition of intravenous acyclovir (empirical antiviral cover for herpes encephalitis) and antimalarial treatment.

By day 3 she had deteriorated further — five fever spikes, no bowel movements for two days, and a decline to an unconscious, drowsy, non-arousable state (GCS E1V1M4), though pupils remained reactive. Tone had paradoxically "improved" in both limbs but reflexes remained exaggerated with an extensor plantar response, and the working diagnosis was revised to viral encephalitis with possible metabolic encephalopathy. Treatment continued along similar lines, with acyclovir, antipyretics, anticonvulsant, osmotic and steroid therapy, and supportive nursing care including tepid sponging and regular repositioning.

By day 7, she was noted to open her eyes only to stimulus (GCS E2V1M4), with a low-grade fever (101.6°F) and continuing hemodynamic and respiratory parameters within a manageable range. Motor examination at this point revealed profound weakness — power graded 0/5 in the right upper and lower limbs and 0/5–1/5 on the left — with hypertonia in all limbs, an extensor plantar response, and an asymmetric, largely absent reflex pattern. The working diagnosis was revised to meningoencephalitis, of uncertain viral versus tubercular etiology, and treatment was continued with intravenous fluids, antisecretory, antiemetic, and antiviral cover.

The documented record ends at day 7, with the patient still critically ill, quadriparetic, and with sensorium not meaningfully recovered; the eventual outcome is not recorded in the available source.
