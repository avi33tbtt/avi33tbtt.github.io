15-Year-Old Girl with Jaundice and Vomiting — Common Variable Immunodeficiency with Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia, Acute Hemolytic Episode

Source case log: https://102-crazymandadi.blogspot.com/2021/11/15-year-old-female-with-common-variable.html

Case Narrative

A 15-year-old girl with a known history of common variable immunodeficiency and autoimmune hemolytic anemia, previously admitted and discharged some months earlier, presented to casualty with five days of yellowish discoloration of the eyes associated with dark-colored urine, and three days of non-bilious, non-projectile vomiting with food content (three episodes). Mosquito bites two days before presentation were followed by a rash on both limbs. She denied fever, abdominal pain, cough, headache, constipation, or diarrhea.

Since her last discharge she had experienced a documented fall in hemoglobin requiring four blood transfusions in total, including two units of packed red cells in August and one further unit in September, and had been maintained on monthly intravenous immunoglobulin and oral steroids, with azathioprine added at 50 mg once daily. At her most recent follow-up in September she remained on prednisolone (branded as Wysolone) and azathioprine, but her family reported that this episode of jaundice was more severe than previous ones.

Her broader past history included bronchial asthma diagnosed at age three, managed initially with syrups and tablets and later an inhaler that was discontinued after symptom resolution, and pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosed at age three, treated with a six-month course of anti-tubercular therapy. She was not a known case of diabetes, hypertension, or epilepsy, and had no known drug allergies or significant family history.

On examination she had pallor and icterus without cyanosis, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, or edema. Vitals were stable: temperature 98.6°F, pulse 80/min, respirations 20/min, blood pressure 100/60 mmHg, and SpO2 99% on room air. Cardiac and respiratory examinations were normal; the abdomen was soft and non-tender with moderate splenomegaly, and the neurological examination was unremarkable. Given the acute worsening of jaundice and hemolysis in a patient with known CVID and autoimmune hemolytic anemia, the working diagnosis was an acute hemolytic episode, with viral hepatitis considered as a possible trigger. She was managed with prednisolone, azathioprine, doxycycline, folic acid, and iron supplementation, with calcium and vitamin D added over the following days, and an ophthalmology referral was made for diminished visual acuity. Over the two days of documented follow-up she remained afebrile with no fresh complaints and stable vital signs, with persistent moderate splenomegaly on serial examination.
