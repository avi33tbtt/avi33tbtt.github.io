5. Main educational gaps

5.1 Investigation listing versus investigation reasoning

Investigations are highly visible, but the write-ups do not always show why a test was selected, how its result changed diagnostic probability, or what action followed. This is the classic transition from data collection to clinical interpretation.

Required structure for each decisive investigation
Why was it ordered? What does the result mean? What did it change in the plan?

5.2 Differential lists versus ranked diagnostic arguments

Differential-diagnosis language occurs in 64.3% of records, but explicit evidence-linkage language appears in only 25.6%. Students may therefore be listing alternatives more often than explaining the supporting and opposing evidence.

The rounds and WhatsApp discussions are ideal settings for teaching that a differential is a ranked argument, not an inventory of disease names.

5.3 Management plans without targets or reassessment

Management language appears in 91.3% of articles, but 655 articles (61.7%) contain management signals without clear outcome or follow-up signals. Only 31.5% include outcome-related language, and 28.9% include escalation-related language.

The students need to move from treatment lists to adaptive management:

Intervention→Target response→Time to reassessment→Failure threshold→Escalation or revision

5.4 Safety and escalation need stronger visibility

Safety language appears in 54.2% of articles and escalation language in 28.9%. This is a priority for multisystem cases involving renal dysfunction, fluid management, antibiotics, anticoagulation, insulin, contrast, respiratory failure, or neurological deterioration.

5.5 Outcome and reflection closure

Only 31.5% of records show outcome-related language, and only 11.0% contain explicit reflection or learning-point signals. This is the clearest educational gap. A case should not end at “treatment given.” Students need to show response, complications, disposition, follow-up, prevention, and what they learned.

7. How to interpret the consultant and WhatsApp contribution

The described teaching model likely contributed to several positive features of the corpus:

  • Students encountered questions that required prioritization rather than memorization.
  • Complex cases were repeatedly reframed through problem-solving discussions.
  • The senior consultant provided a model of expert illness-script organization and clinical judgment.
  • Batch discussion exposed students to alternative interpretations and different reasoning routes.
  • Students could revisit cases after the bedside encounter and consolidate learning asynchronously.

However, this model also creates an assessment challenge. Group discussion may produce high-quality reasoning that is not attributable to one learner. To evaluate individual growth, future records should distinguish:

  • Observed by the student: what the student personally saw, measured, or did.
  • Reasoned by the student: the student’s own differential, prioritization, and interpretation.
  • Prompted by the consultant: reasoning reached after senior questioning.
  • Contributed by peers: ideas introduced in batch discussion.
  • Transferred independently: whether the student applies the same reasoning in a new case.

5. Decisive investigation
Why it was ordered, what it showed, and what it changed.

6. Management with safety
Intervention, contraindications, monitoring, expected response, and escalation threshold.

7. Reassessment
What happened after treatment and whether the response matched expectations.

8. Reflection
What was difficult, what changed the trajectory, and what would be done differently next time.

For research, the most defensible design is a longitudinal mixed-methods study combining:

  • Written case analysis before and after structured critical-node training.
  • Observed bedside reasoning or oral case presentation.
  • Faculty ratings using the rubric above.
  • OSCE or clinical-vignette performance.
  • Qualitative analysis of rounds and WhatsApp discussion, with consent and privacy safeguards.
  • Transfer assessment using new cases not previously discussed by the batch.

10. Highest-priority educational interventions

  1. Make oral reasoning visible: require each student to record one differential, one mismatch, one key decision, and one reassessment trigger.
  2. Use a diagnostic timeout: ask what else could explain the case, what cannot be missed, and what would change the current plan.
  3. Teach treatment as a feedback loop: intervention, target, reassessment, failure threshold, escalation.
  4. Require outcome closure: students must document response, complications, disposition, follow-up, and prevention.
  5. Separate scaffolding from independence: mark whether an insight was generated independently, prompted by the consultant, or contributed by peers.
  6. Use the corpus for formative feedback rather than automated grading: flag records with investigations but little interpretation, management without monitoring, no outcome, no reflection, or no safety plan.

11. Final conclusion

The student work reflects a meaningful, clinically rich educational process. The consultant-led rounds and continued WhatsApp-based case discussion likely created a strong environment for guided participation, clinical questioning, and problem solving. The corpus shows that students are engaging with the major components of clinical care and with complex multisystem presentations.

The next developmental goal is not simply more complete documentation. It is visible, transferable, adaptive clinical reasoning:

What mattered most? What did not fit? What could deteriorate? Which decision could change the trajectory? How did the patient respond? What would I do differently in the next case?

Used this way, the corpus becomes more than an archive of case reports. It becomes evidence of a longitudinal MBBS learning system and a foundation for evaluating how bedside apprenticeship, case-based questioning, and peer discussion shape diagnostic reasoning and clinical judgment.
