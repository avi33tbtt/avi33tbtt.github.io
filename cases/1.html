Case 1: A 60-Year-Old Man with Ascites, Hypoproteinemia, and Renal Dysfunction

Source case log — Unit 2 admission. Adapted for teaching.
Original: http://ashakiran923.blogspot.com/2021/03/60-years-old-male-fever-under-evaluation.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 60-year-old man, known to have chronic kidney disease and managed conservatively, walked into the casualty department with a fortnight of fever behind him. It had been high-grade and intermittent, breaking with medication but always returning, and for the last three days it had brought something new: breathlessness that started with exertion and, by the time he reached the hospital, was present even at rest.

He had no cough, no cold, no pain in his abdomen to speak of — but he did have burning while passing urine, on-and-off puffiness of his face, and a vague sense that swallowing wasn't quite right, raising the question of aphthous ulcers. There was no orthopnea, no paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, no chest pain, no palpitations, and no pedal edema — a constellation that pointed away from a straightforward cardiac story even as his breathlessness worsened.

His history added texture: long years of smoking and drinking, both stopped only three months earlier, and known CKD sitting quietly in the background. On examination he was conscious and oriented, but pale, with a low-grade temperature, a pulse hovering around 100, and a blood pressure of 110/100 — a narrow pulse pressure worth noting. His abdomen was distended and everted, its umbilicus drawn into a transverse slit, guarding present, shifting dullness confirming free fluid, and dilated veins visible over the surface — ascites, and long-standing ascites at that, though no organ was frankly palpable beneath the fluid.

The working list of possibilities — UTI, BPH, fever under evaluation, coronary disease, later hypertension — evolved from day to day as more was gathered: hemogram, renal and liver function, chest X-ray, malaria smear, dengue serology, cultures, viral markers. He was started on antibiotics for presumed urinary infection, a diuretic for the fluid overload, gastroprotection, and supportive care, with fluid restricted to a litre a day.

The following days told a story of slow deterioration wrapped in small, misleading improvements. His stools and flatus returned on day two, briefly reassuring, but his appetite fell away and his urine output began to dwindle. By day three, hypertension had crept into the assessment. By day four, "grade 1 hypertensive encephalopathy" appeared in the notes. By day five, his intake-output balance was frankly negative — a litre in, six hundred millilitres out — and the diagnosis had shifted again, this time to hepatic encephalopathy layered on his known renal and cardiac disease.

Vitamin K, lactulose, erythromycin, and supportive measures followed, but the trajectory did not reverse. The log entries grow sparser through days six, eight, and nine — a pattern that itself says something about how a ward note thins out as a patient worsens — and the record closes with a death summary.
