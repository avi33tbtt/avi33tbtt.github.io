44 Year Old Man with Rapidly Progressive Anasarca — Glomerulonephritis Likely Secondary to Amyloidosis from Chronic Rheumatoid Arthritis

Source case log: https://2018-21batchpgy3gmpracticals.blogspot.com/2021/08/18100006003-case-presentations.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 44-year-old stonemason presented with a three-day history of rapidly progressive, bilaterally symmetrical generalised swelling. It began suddenly one night with facial puffiness, noticed the next morning while brushing his teeth, followed quickly by symmetrical pitting swelling of both legs extending to mid-shin. He denied breathlessness, palpitations, or chest pain, but reported frothy urine, a gradually falling urine output over the three days, and no blood in the urine, fever, rash, or recent drug exposure — a pattern pointing away from a cardiac or hepatic cause and strongly toward a glomerular process, since heavy proteinuria with oliguria and anasarca in the absence of volume loss or urinary obstruction is a near-classic presentation of acute glomerulopathy.

His background added an important layer: since 2011 he had a slowly progressive, initially asymmetric but eventually bilaterally symmetric, inflammatory polyarthritis affecting the small joints of his hands and wrists, with prominent early-morning stiffness lasting about an hour, pain and swelling that improved with activity, and gradual involvement of larger joints (shoulders, elbows, knees, hips) over nearly a decade. He had been intermittently treated with etoricoxib and febuxostat for the preceding eight months, and over the past year had developed involuntary weight loss, decreased appetite, and new subcutaneous swellings near his finger joints — features raising concern for longstanding, poorly controlled inflammatory arthritis with systemic sequelae.

On examination he was visibly uncomfortable, guarding his elbows and wrists, with blood pressure elevated at 140–150/90 mmHg and a mild respiratory acidosis pattern on breathing. Head-to-toe examination showed periorbital puffiness and edema, conjunctival pallor, leukonychia, and pitting pedal edema to mid-shin. Musculoskeletal examination was extensive and striking: warm, tender, swollen wrists and elbows with markedly limited range of motion; ulnar deviation and palmar subluxation at the MCP joints; swollen, erythematous PIP joints with sparing of the DIP joints; and swollen, warm knees that initially prevented him from standing. This distribution — symmetric, small-and-large-joint, DIP-sparing, deforming polyarthritis with radiographic erosions and a "scallop sign" on hand X-ray — met ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis even though his rheumatoid factor came back negative.

Urine microscopy showed dysmorphic red blood cells, a highly specific marker for glomerular bleeding, together with hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin 2.5 g/dl), secondary hypertension, and a 24-hour urinary protein of 1500 mg, all supporting acute glomerulonephritis. Given his decade of poorly treated inflammatory arthritis, the leading explanation for his renal disease was secondary (AA) amyloidosis, a recognised complication of chronic, undertreated systemic inflammation, with IgA nephropathy, primary glomerulonephritis, drug-induced nephropathy, and gout-related crystal nephropathy considered and judged less likely. He was also found to be hyponatremic (dilutional, related to his anasarca) and to have hyperuricemia contributing to his joint symptoms, alongside anemia of chronic disease.

He was started on prednisolone and febuxostat, free water restriction for his hyponatremia, and haemodialysis was arranged given his worsening renal function, with plans for an abdominal fat pad biopsy to confirm amyloidosis and synovial fluid aspiration to assess for coexisting gout. His longer-term outcome is not documented in the source record, which was structured primarily as a diagnostic reasoning exercise.
