32 Year Old Man with Epigastric Pain and Hypotension — Septic Shock

Source case log: https://03saitarun.blogspot.com/2021/08/03-sai-tarun-24-08-21.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 32-year-old man presented to casualty with sudden-onset epigastric pain, two episodes of vomiting, and giddiness since the afternoon, along with a day of fever. He had been well until the previous day, when he developed abdominal discomfort with throbbing epigastric pain (non-radiating) and a single non-bilious, non-projectile vomiting episode containing food content, without associated constipation or loose stools. The fever was a single high-grade episode that morning with chills and rigors, without diurnal variation or associated cold, cough, breathlessness, or headache. He had consumed alcohol the day before presentation and initially sought care at another facility with vomiting and epigastric pain, where he was given intravenous fluids; while there, he had a rotational giddiness episode without headache, palpitations, sweating, blackouts, or visual disturbance. On presentation to this facility his extremities were cold, pulse was feeble, and blood pressure was markedly low at 70/40 mmHg, prompting immediate fluid resuscitation.

His background included regular heavy alcohol use — four times daily, half a quarter to a full bottle, for twenty years — and daily smokeless tobacco (kaini) use for five years. He had childhood asthma, exacerbated by cold weather and certain foods, managed with an inhaler. He was not a known diabetic, hypertensive, or cardiac patient, and had no prior surgeries or transfusions. His father had a history of diabetes. On examination he was conscious, coherent, and cooperative but moderately dehydrated, with a low-grade fever (99.4°F), a low-volume pulse of 60 bpm, and persistently low blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg despite initial resuscitation, with an oxygen saturation of 97%. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the epigastric and right hypochondrial regions without a palpable mass, free fluid, or organomegaly, and neurological examination was entirely normal with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15/15.

Given the combination of fever with rigors, epigastric pain, and refractory hypotension with cold extremities and a feeble pulse — clinical hallmarks of a distributive shock state — he was diagnosed with septic shock, likely originating from an intra-abdominal or biliary source given the localizing pain. He was managed with aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole), a proton pump inhibitor, antiemetics, and vitamin support, with strict input/output monitoring. The documented record ends at this point in his resuscitation and evaluation.
