Adult Patient with Persistent Cough, Breathlessness, and Loss of Appetite — Tubercular Left-Sided Pleural Effusion with CKD

Source case log: https://22bangarilaxmiraj.blogspot.com/2020/06/a-patient-with-lack-of-resolution-of.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

This entry documents a structured diagnostic-reasoning exercise built around a patient with persistent cough, breathlessness, and, more recently, loss of appetite, rather than a first-hand admission note. The presenting complaints — heaviness and pain on the left side of the chest with breathlessness and reduced breath sounds on that side — were worked through systematically against a broad differential that included COPD, asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, congestive heart failure, pulmonary thromboembolism, pneumonia, and neuromuscular disease, before converging on left-sided pleural effusion as the most likely unifying diagnosis, given that chest heaviness with reduced breath sounds is a classic presentation of fluid in the pleural space.

Having localised the problem to a pleural effusion, the reasoning then moved to identifying its cause, weighing parapneumonic effusion, tuberculosis, subdiaphragmatic disease, malignancy, nephrotic syndrome/chronic kidney disease, post-myocardial-infarction syndrome, and asbestos-related effusion, each assessed against features such as fever, socioeconomic and family tuberculosis risk, weight loss, prior malignancy, signs of chronic kidney disease, and occupational exposure history.

Chest X-ray showed obliteration of the left costophrenic angle with a fluid level, confirming pleural effusion, while high-resolution CT additionally suggested an endobronchial infective process on the left side alongside chronic kidney disease affecting the left kidney. Pleural fluid analysis showed a high lymphocyte count and high protein content, a pattern consistent with tuberculous rather than malignant or parapneumonic effusion. The final diagnosis reached was pulmonary tuberculosis with underlying chronic kidney disease, and the recommended treatment was a standard course of anti-tubercular therapy. No detailed vitals, formal examination record, or subsequent clinical course are documented, as this entry functioned as a case-based learning exercise rather than a bedside record.
