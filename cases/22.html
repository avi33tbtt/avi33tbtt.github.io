54 Year Old Male with Seizures (GTCS) — Secondary to Viral Encephalitis

Source case log: http://mohithak.blogspot.com/2021/02/54year-old-male-patient-with-status.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 54-year-old man presented with a day of recurrent seizures. Six days before admission he had developed a two-day febrile illness — low-grade, intermittent, with chills — that settled with medication. On the second day of fever he developed severe headache with blurring of vision and diplopia. Two nights later he had involuntary movements of the upper and lower limbs with tongue bite and loss of consciousness; while being transported by ambulance he had two further similar episodes within thirty minutes. At a private hospital he went on to have three more seizure episodes overnight, and a CT brain was obtained. At a government hospital, CT brain showed chronic lacunar infarcts in the lentiform nucleus. Over the following two days he had a total of seven seizure episodes, remaining conscious between them, before being referred onward. At the time of admission he had three further episodes with stiffening, tongue bite, and eye-rolling, but no loss of consciousness or incontinence, and no vomiting or headache afterward.

He was a known diabetic of 15 years on metformin and glimepiride, and hypertensive for 3 years on atenolol, with occasional alcohol use and a remote smoking history (stopped 20 years prior). On examination he was conscious, coherent, and cooperative with a GCS of 15/15, afebrile, blood pressure 130/90 mmHg, pulse 79 bpm, oxygen saturation 97% on room air, and a markedly elevated random blood glucose of 335 mg/dL. Neurological examination showed mildly reduced power (4/5) in the lower limbs bilaterally with otherwise preserved tone, reflexes, and sensation; cerebellar testing and gait were normal, and meningeal signs (Kernig's) were negative.

The seizures were attributed to viral encephalitis, compounded by hypokalemia, hypoproteinemia, and underlying diabetic nephropathy and retinopathy (the latter identified on ophthalmology referral). He was loaded with intravenous levetiracetam and phenytoin, given supportive fluids, and started on antibiotics and neuro-supportive measures. Over the next two days he had no further seizures, though he developed headache and low-grade fever; serum potassium was found to be significantly low (2.7–2.8 mmol/L) and was corrected with potassium chloride syrup alongside continuation of levetiracetam, empirical antibiotics, and analgesia. The documented record ends at this point in his recovery, with seizures controlled and potassium correction underway.
