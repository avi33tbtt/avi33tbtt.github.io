14-Year-Old Boy with Abdominal Pain, Poor Urinary Stream, and Fever — Iron Deficiency Anemia Under Evaluation

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 14-year-old boy, an eighth-grade student, presented with a month-long history of abdominal pain and loss of appetite, together with ten days of poor urinary stream and six days of fever. His abdominal pain began insidiously a month before admission, accompanied briefly by two days of vomiting and loose stools that settled with medication, though the pain and poor appetite persisted. Ten days before admission he developed a poor urinary stream with post-void dribbling, without burning micturition. Around the same time his fever began — initially low-grade and continuous, but over the final two days developing a clear evening rise in temperature with chills and rigors, a pattern often seen with an evolving systemic infective or hematologic process. He had no cough, headache, ear symptoms, or urinary burning, and no past history of type 1 diabetes, hypertension, asthma, or tuberculosis.

On examination he showed pallor without icterus, cyanosis, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, or pedal edema, and his vitals were stable. Cardiac, respiratory, and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, and neurologically he was intact with no focal deficits. Given the combination of pallor, fever with evening spikes, and abdominal pain, he was admitted with a provisional diagnosis of anemia under evaluation.

Over the first day of admission, his fever spikes actually increased overnight and he passed dark-coloured stools — a finding that warranted attention for possible gastrointestinal bleeding or a hemolytic process — while his systolic blood pressure rose and his heart rate increased, though his abdominal exam remained soft with no organomegaly or palpable lymph nodes. He was started on antimalarial therapy (artesunate) alongside deworming treatment, iron repletion, and antipyretic measures, given the differential of a febrile illness with anemia in an endemic setting. A peripheral smear confirmed microcytic hypochromic anemia, and his serum LDH was mildly elevated. By the second day, his abdominal pain and appetite had both improved, urinary symptoms had resolved, and fever spikes had stopped, allowing the diagnosis to be refined from a broad "anemia under evaluation" to a more specific iron deficiency anemia. He continued on iron sucrose infusions and supportive care, and by day three his fever spikes recurred, prompting the addition of an antibiotic (norfloxacin) once culture-sensitivity results became available, continued through day four with ongoing iron therapy and monitoring.

Outcome: His diagnosis was refined to iron deficiency anemia, and he continued iron sucrose infusions with antibiotic therapy for recurrent fever spikes through Day 4 of admission; the source record does not extend to a final resolution or discharge summary.
