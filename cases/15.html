46-Year-Old Man with Severe Metabolic Acidosis and Right Heart Failure — Starvation/Alcoholic Ketoacidosis with Wet Beriberi

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 46-year-old man presented with breathlessness that began the morning of admission. The story leading up to it was initially confusing — his family first reported a possible insect or snake bite at the farm the day before, but attenders later clarified he had actually been at home the entire time. What emerged as the more reliable history, provided by his son, was that he was a chronic alcoholic who had been drinking continuously for two days beforehand and had not eaten anything in that time. Roughly three hours before arrival he developed profuse sweating and became rapidly breathless, prompting the family to bring him in. He denied chest pain, palpitations, fever, cough, or burning micturition, and had no known history of diabetes, hypertension, cancer, stroke, or epilepsy.

On arrival he was markedly unwell: tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 40, profoundly hypotensive with a systolic blood pressure of only 60 mmHg, and hypoxic with oxygen saturation around 88% on room air, improving to 96% with supplemental oxygen. He required a fluid bolus and low-dose inotropic support to bring his blood pressure up to 110/70. His blood glucose was low at 61 mg/dL, prompting glucose correction. This combination of profound shock, hypoxia, and hypoglycemia in a starved, actively drinking man pointed toward a combined metabolic and cardiac crisis.

Bedside echocardiography revealed right heart failure with a D-shaped left ventricle (suggesting right ventricular pressure or volume overload) and a dilated inferior vena cava. Given the combination of right heart failure, hypotension, hypoxia, and elevated D-dimers, a CT pulmonary angiogram was performed to rule out pulmonary embolism as a cause of his acute right heart strain; it was negative, with normal lung parenchyma. Arterial blood gas showed severe metabolic acidosis, and urine ketones were positive, together pointing toward starvation ketoacidosis versus alcoholic ketoacidosis as the metabolic driver, with the right heart failure attributed to wet beriberi — a thiamine-deficiency cardiomyopathy that classically follows prolonged alcohol use and poor nutritional intake, and which can closely mimic other causes of acute right heart failure.

He was treated with intravenous dextrose-containing fluids and high-dose thiamine, targeting both the ketoacidosis and the presumed thiamine deficiency underlying his cardiac findings. By the following day his acidosis had corrected and his tachypnea had improved. A repeat echocardiogram showed improvement — a dilated right atrium with decreasing right ventricular dilation and resolution of the D-shaped left ventricular configuration, with normal LV contractility — supporting the wet beriberi diagnosis, since thiamine repletion produced rapid hemodynamic improvement.

Outcome: His acidosis resolved and his cardiac findings improved substantially with fluids and thiamine, but he was discharged at his family's request against medical advice for personal reasons before the recommended further hospital stay could be completed.
