45 Year Old Man with Seizures Secondary to Hepatic Encephalopathy, Alcoholic Hepatitis, and Thrombocytopenia

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 45-year-old man who ran a Xerox shop, a chronic heavy drinker consuming around 180 mL of whisky daily with only one meal a day, was brought to casualty after developing loss of appetite, two episodes of vomiting, and two brief episodes of involuntary upper-limb movements. On the morning of admission he had nausea and non-projectile, non-bilious vomiting containing food particles; in the afternoon he had another vomiting episode. In the evening, while sitting in a chair, he had an episode of involuntary upper-limb movement lasting one to two minutes, witnessed by neighbours, followed thirty minutes later by a second similar episode with an associated tongue bite. He had no involuntary defecation or micturition. He had a remote history of epilepsy — multiple episodes a decade earlier on irregular treatment, with two further episodes six months prior — and had been drinking heavily for twenty years.

On examination he was conscious, coherent, and cooperative with icterus but no pallor, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, cyanosis, or pedal edema. Vitals were stable (BP 110/70 mmHg, pulse 98 bpm, saturation 98% on room air) with a mildly elevated glucose of 165 mg/dL. Cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurological examinations were unremarkable, and the abdomen was soft and non-tender. Screening for hepatitis B, HIV, hepatitis C, and dengue was negative. Coagulation studies were deranged (PT 20 seconds, INR 1.45, APTT 39 seconds), consistent with significant hepatic synthetic dysfunction. The working diagnosis was focal seizures under evaluation, secondary to probable hepatic encephalopathy with thrombocytopenia in the context of chronic alcohol use, and he was started on thiamine, lactulose, and seizure precautions with a protein-restricted diet.

Over the first two days he improved symptomatically — passing stools and having fewer vomiting episodes — but on the second full hospital day developed a thirty-minute episode of irrelevant talk, prompting a psychiatry referral for suspected alcohol withdrawal and revision of the working diagnosis to include delirium. He was managed with vitamin K, ursodeoxycholic acid (Udiliv), rifaximin (Rifagut), lactulose, and L-ornithine L-aspartate (Hepamerz) for hepatic support, alongside benzodiazepines and, subsequently, low-dose haloperidol, olanzapine, and oxazepam for agitation and withdrawal symptoms; he also received two units of fresh frozen plasma for the deranged coagulation profile, with catheterisation for output monitoring. By the third and fourth hospital days he remained delirious intermittently, with the working diagnosis expanding to include alcoholic hepatitis with delirium.

By the fifth hospital day his sensorium began to improve, though he became persistently febrile and his abdomen was noted to be distended (girth 84–85 cm), with imaging suggesting cholelithiasis and cholecystitis with possible common bile duct obstruction and obstructive jaundice; broad-spectrum antibiotics (cefotaxime) and, briefly, artesunate were added given the fever, and coagulation parameters normalized (PT 15 sec, INR 1.1). By the sixth and seventh days his fever resolved and sensorium continued to improve, though a urinary tract infection (E. coli on culture) was identified and treated with ofloxacin. From the eighth day onward he developed increasing bilateral pitting pedal edema and abdominal distension, managed with diuretics (furosemide, later spironolactone), fluid and salt restriction, and continued hepatoprotective therapy; a course of corticosteroids (prednisolone) was later added for the alcoholic hepatitis component. Over the following two weeks his sensorium remained clear with no further seizures or delirium, and his abdominal girth and body weight both trended steadily downward (weight from 63 kg to 54 kg; girth from a peak of around 91 cm down to 77 cm), indicating resolving ascites, with decreasing pedal edema. By the last documented review (hospital Day 23), he was conscious, coherent, hemodynamically stable, with resolved delirium and UTI, and continuing to improve on diuretics, steroids, and supportive hepatic therapy.

Clinical course
  1. Day 1 (admission): Presented with seizures and vomiting; diagnosed with probable hepatic encephalopathy; started on thiamine, lactulose, and seizure precautions.
  2. Day 2: Passed stools, vomiting decreased; treatment escalated with vitamin K, UDCA, rifaximin, and hepamerz; psychiatry referral placed for suspected alcohol withdrawal.
  3. Day 3: Brief episode of irrelevant talk (delirium); olanzapine added; two units FFP transfused for deranged coagulation; Ryles tube and catheterisation placed.
  4. Day 4: Continued delirium management.
  5. Day 5: Sensorium improving; became febrile with abdominal distension (girth 84 cm); imaging suggested cholelithiasis/cholecystitis with possible obstructive jaundice; antibiotics escalated, artesunate given empirically.
  6. Day 6: Persistent high fever and cannula-site pain; delirium resolved; continued broad-spectrum antibiotics and hepatic support.
  7. Day 7: Fully oriented and conscious; fever/cough resolved; blood culture negative but urine culture grew E. coli; started on ofloxacin for UTI.
  8. Day 8: Sensorium improved further; new bilateral pitting pedal edema noted; diuretic (furosemide) started.
  9. Day 9: Pedal edema increasing; diuretic and fluid/salt restriction intensified; further FFP given for coagulopathy.
  10. Days 10–21: Progressive resolution of ascites and edema, tracked by steadily falling abdominal girth (91→77 cm) and weight (63→54 kg); diuretics, steroids (prednisolone), and hepatic supportive therapy continued; UTI treated to resolution.
  11. Day 22–23 (last documented): Sensorium clear, no focal neurological deficits, left leg swelling and pain improving, sleeping well; continued on diuretics, prednisolone, and supportive care.
Outcome
At the last documented review (hospital Day 23), the patient was conscious, coherent, and clinically stable, with resolved delirium and UTI, no further seizures since admission, and steadily improving ascites/edema (weight down from 63 to 54 kg, abdominal girth down from a peak of ~91 cm to 77 cm) on diuretics, corticosteroids, and hepatoprotective therapy for underlying alcoholic hepatitis with decompensated chronic liver disease.
