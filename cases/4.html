Case 4 — 65-Year-Old Man with Abdominal Distension, Suspected Iatrogenic Cushing Syndrome

Source: harshamnvsr.blogspot.com — 65Y Man with Abdominal Distension

Case Narrative

A 65-year-old farmer presented with worsening shortness of breath (grade 3) over 10 days, on-and-off fever for two days, and decreased urine output for three days. His history, however, stretched back much further and helped explain why he was so vulnerable to this acute presentation.

Ten years earlier he had developed a dragging pain in the right upper limb, for which he was prescribed painkillers and steroids that he continued for two years. He subsequently developed haematemesis, was hospitalised for large-volume blood loss requiring transfusion, and was later discharged on weekly medication before eventually stopping all treatment for around four years. A road traffic injury — a fall from a cart — then led him back onto steroid therapy, which he continued for the past three years. This long, largely unsupervised exposure to corticosteroids is clinically important: it raised the question of iatrogenic Cushing syndrome as the thread connecting his abdominal distension, skin changes, and poor wound healing.

Alongside this, he described a year-long history of pedal edema and abdominal distension that waxed and waned with medication but had worsened over the preceding 10 days, together with shortness of breath that had been present for three months but sharply worsened in the last 10 days, with occasional palpitations. Two months before admission he sustained a wound that was initially neglected while he continued his usual field work; when discharge from the wound increased, he sought care, and cultures grew Pseudomonas, though he did not complete treatment. He also gave a striking account of stepping on broken bangles with his right leg, rubbing the sole of his foot against his leg while trying to remove the fragment, and sustaining a bleeding injury to the posterior leg that he continued to expose to muddy field water for over a week before it was formally dressed — a history that speaks to delayed wound healing and poor tissue integrity, both recognised features of prolonged glucocorticoid excess. He also reported a 10-year history of intermittent polyarthritis affecting the small joints of the hands, wrists, elbows, shoulders, knees, and ankles, for which he had not required medication in the preceding four to five years.

On examination he was conscious, coherent, and cooperative, afebrile to touch, with a pulse of 88/min, respiratory rate 18/min, blood pressure 140/90 mmHg, oxygen saturation 99% on room air, and a blood glucose of 102 mg/dL. Cardiovascular and respiratory examination were normal, and there was no neurological deficit. The abdomen was soft but distended, with a transverse slit-like umbilicus, all quadrants moving equally with respiration, and no visible scars or sinuses — but with multiple rashes scattered across the abdomen and skin that was notably dry, coarse, and scaly, findings that, together with his steroid history, supported a cutaneous manifestation of chronic glucocorticoid excess.

Laboratory investigations were sent, and the working impression was Cushing syndrome secondary to iatrogenic (self-directed, unsupervised) steroid abuse. The diagnosis was documented approximately nine days after admission, with the chart updated two days after that.

The available record ends with this working diagnosis; further investigation results, definitive confirmatory testing, and the patient's eventual clinical outcome are not documented in the source.
