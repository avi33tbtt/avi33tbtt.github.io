55-Year-Old Man with Right-Sided Weakness and Facial Deviation — Suspected Acute Stroke on a Background of Diabetes and Hypertension

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 55-year-old man presented with sudden weakness of his right arm and leg, together with tingling and numbness on the same side, all beginning the day before. The onset was abrupt — he felt difficulty walking immediately upon getting out of bed, with associated deviation of his mouth to the left, a finding that, together with the acute, non-progressive, one-sided weakness, strongly suggested an acute cerebrovascular event. He had no loss of consciousness, no seizures, and no difficulty swallowing or bowel/bladder involvement. Functionally he retained some fine motor ability — able to comb his hair and button his shirt — though he had noted slippage of his sandal on the affected side while walking, a subtle early clue to his lower limb weakness. There was no history of fever, trauma, vomiting, or double vision.

His background was significant for hypertension for eight years, managed with telmisartan-amlodipine, and diabetes newly diagnosed three months earlier, on a single oral agent. He had no known history of prior stroke, chronic kidney disease, or asthma. On examination he was alert and oriented, mildly hypertensive, with pedal edema noted, and cardiac and respiratory exams were unremarkable.

Neurological examination showed preserved tone bilaterally but reduced power on the right side — 4/5 in the right leg with the right arm intact at 5/5, and grip strength preserved in both hands. His reflexes were notably brisker on the right (biceps and knee reflexes graded 3+ versus 2+ on the left), and his right plantar response was equivocal, while the left was flexor — findings in keeping with an evolving upper motor neuron lesion on the right side, corresponding to the left-sided cerebral hemisphere given the crossed pyramidal pathways. There were no cerebellar or meningeal signs, and sensory examination was intact. A CT brain performed at an outside facility was obtained to further characterize the lesion.

He was managed with vitamin B-complex support, gastric protection, continuation of his diabetes and hypertension medications, dual antiplatelet therapy, and a statin, alongside physiotherapy for the right limbs and close monitoring of vitals and glucose.

Outcome: He was managed as an acute stroke with continuation of his diabetes and hypertension treatment plus secondary prevention therapy (antiplatelets, statin) and physiotherapy; the final imaging report and discharge outcome were not detailed in the source.
