65-Year-Old Man with Progressive Limb Weakness and Back Pain — Myeloneuropathy with Aortic Insufficiency

Source case log: https://12avirnenivaishnavi.blogspot.com/2020/06/myeloneuropathy.html

Case Narrative

A 65-year-old man presented with weakness of both lower limbs over fifteen days. His history extended back to his early thirties, when he began experiencing episodes of pounding palpitations with chest heaviness that resolved with rest; these symptoms progressed gradually over the following decades. At around age sixty he had his first episode of nocturnal dyspnea, though without orthopnea or pedal edema. He also described longstanding knee and lower back pain that had progressively worsened over the years, more severe on the left side.

Over the fifteen days before presentation he developed increasing difficulty getting up from bed, becoming completely unable to do so in the final four days. No sensory symptoms were reported. On examination, Duroziez's sign was positive — an audible diastolic murmur over the femoral artery, indicative of aortic regurgitation — and a pulsus bisferens (a biphasic arterial pulse also associated with aortic insufficiency) was noted. Neurologically, lower-limb power was slightly reduced, ankle reflexes were absent, Beevor's sign was positive (indicating lower abdominal muscle weakness characteristic of a spinal cord lesion), vibration and proprioception were impaired, and plantar responses were extensor bilaterally.

This combination of long-standing valvular heart disease, progressive lower-limb weakness with absent ankle reflexes, impaired proprioception and vibration sense, and extensor plantar responses — a pattern combining both upper and lower motor neuron features — raised suspicion for myeloneuropathy, a condition marked by simultaneous involvement of the spinal cord tracts and peripheral nerves of the lower limbs. ECG and chest radiograph were unremarkable. Echocardiography showed a dilated inferior vena cava and right atrium, mild tricuspid regurgitation, mild pulmonary hypertension, and trivial aortic regurgitation with preserved ejection fraction. Urinalysis showed pus cells, and ultrasound demonstrated grade 2 prostatomegaly, with digital rectal examination confirming a flat, non-nodular prostate. Serologic testing for syphilis (RPR) was performed to exclude tabes dorsalis as a mimicking cause of his sensory ataxia, and returned negative.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the spine revealed lesions at the T8, T10, and L2 vertebral levels causing cord compression, providing a structural correlate for his myeloneuropathy. He was managed with supportive supplementation as part of his ongoing evaluation and treatment.
