55-Year-Old Man with Right Upper Quadrant Pain and Fever — Suspected Liver Abscess

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 55-year-old man, a toddy climber by occupation, presented with ten days of right upper quadrant abdominal pain and seven days of fever. The pain was sudden in onset, gradually progressive, dragging in character, and non-radiating; it worsened on standing and improved somewhat with medication, without associated nausea, vomiting, or loose stools. About three days into the pain, he developed high-grade continuous fever with chills and rigors, though notably without cold, cough, breathlessness, neck pain, or headache — features that helped narrow the differential away from a primary respiratory or meningeal source and toward an intra-abdominal focus.

He had been hospitalized with similar complaints two weeks before this admission and had received a three-day course of intravenous antibiotics, but the symptoms recurred. He had no history of diabetes, hypertension, epilepsy, asthma, stroke, or coronary disease. His personal history was notable for heavy, sustained toddy consumption (about one litre daily for 30 years) and beedi smoking (about 10 per day for 30 years) — both relevant risk factors for hepatobiliary and infective pathology.

On examination he was afebrile at the time of assessment, with normal vital signs, mild scleral icterus, and pitting pedal edema that was progressive and extended to the ankles. Respiratory examination revealed reduced air entry and fine crepitations in the right lower lobe region, and a chest X-ray confirmed a mild right-sided pleural effusion — findings that can accompany a subphrenic or hepatic process irritating the diaphragm. Abdominal examination showed right upper quadrant tenderness with a liver span of 11 cm, but no palpable mass, free fluid, or organomegaly, and bowel sounds were normal.

Taken together — right upper quadrant pain, prolonged fever, mild icterus, pedal edema, and a background of decades of heavy alcohol use — the clinical picture pointed toward a hepatic focus. Investigations including liver and renal function tests, urinalysis, ultrasound abdomen, echocardiography, and a coagulation profile were sent to characterize this further, and ultrasound findings were used to support a working diagnosis of pyogenic liver abscess arising on a background of underlying liver pathology and likely bacterial infection.

Outcome: The working diagnosis at the point the record ends was a bacterial liver abscess on a background of chronic liver disease; the source did not document further treatment course or final outcome.
