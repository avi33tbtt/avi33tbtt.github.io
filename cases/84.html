38 Year Old Man with Progressive Proximal Limb Weakness Since Childhood — Muscular Dystrophy Under Evaluation

Source case log: https://2018-21batchpgy3gmpracticals.blogspot.com/2021/08/18100006009-case-presentations.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 38-year-old man presented with difficulty walking that had been present for roughly eight years and progressive weakness for about seven years. His symptoms began with difficulty climbing uphill, rising from a chair without support, squatting, and getting up from the floor, and slowly progressed to affect level-ground walking as well. Over the same period he developed difficulty lifting his arms above his head — struggling to comb his hair, shake hands, or brush his teeth — while fine distal tasks such as buttoning or gripping objects were relatively preserved, a pattern classically seen in proximal, limb-girdle-type muscle weakness rather than a peripheral nerve or neuromuscular junction problem.

Sensation was entirely normal throughout — he could feel pain, temperature, and touch normally and had no numbness — which argued against a sensory neuropathy, and there was no history of double vision, swallowing difficulty, or facial weakness to suggest a neuromuscular junction disorder like myasthenia. He reported intermittent muscle cramps and spasms after prolonged sitting, mild bowel complaints relieved by hydration, but no bladder symptoms, cranial nerve involvement, or autonomic disturbance. He had second-degree parental consanguinity and mild developmental delay in walking and speech, though no family member had similar complaints, and his parents were unavailable for further history, having died in an accident.

On examination he was well oriented with intact higher mental function, though his attention and calculation score was mildly reduced on formal testing. Motor examination showed symmetrical hypotonia and reduced power most pronounced proximally — around 3/5 in hip and trunk muscles versus 4/5 more distally — with reduced but present reflexes and normal sensory and cerebellar testing. Cardiovascular and respiratory examination were unremarkable. Creatine phosphokinase was markedly elevated at 780 IU/L, nerve conduction studies were normal, and electromyography showed a reduced-amplitude polyphasic motor pattern consistent with a myopathic rather than neuropathic process.

Taken together, the clinical picture — slowly progressive, symmetrical, proximal greater-than-distal weakness without sensory loss, cardiac involvement, or a family history — was considered most consistent with a limb-girdle or Becker-type muscular dystrophy, with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy and proximal motor neuropathy raised as less likely alternatives given the myopathic EMG pattern and lack of significant sensory findings. A muscle biopsy was planned to confirm the diagnosis; its result and the patient's subsequent course are not recorded in the source material.
