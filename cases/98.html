45 Year Old Woman with Snake Bite and Delayed Vomiting — Acute Kidney Injury with Thrombocytopenia

Source case log: https://21buttisonali.blogspot.com/2021/08/general-medicine-case-presentation-1.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 45-year-old woman presented seven days after being bitten by a snake, identified as a Russell's viper, on her right foot. She had received anti-snake venom within an hour of the bite at a local hospital and initially recovered well, but the next day developed swelling of the right lower limb, which was treated with a topical ointment and gradually subsided. Three days after that, she developed two to three episodes of bilious vomiting with severe abdominal pain — a delayed complication pattern that can follow viper envenomation, which is known to cause coagulopathy and, less commonly, renal injury. She had no local necrosis at the bite site, no photophobia or phonophobia, and no cardiovascular or respiratory symptoms.

On examination she was conscious, oriented, and moderately built, without jaundice, clubbing, cyanosis, lymphadenopathy, or pedal edema. Her blood pressure was elevated at 150/90 mmHg with a mildly raised capillary glucose of 180 mg%, and her abdomen was distended and tender without a palpable mass. Given her recent viper bite and subsequent vomiting and abdominal pain, she was evaluated for delayed venom-related complications, and the working diagnosis settled on acute kidney injury with thrombocytopenia — both recognised sequelae of viper envenomation, thought to result from the venom's effects on the clotting cascade and renal microvasculature.

She was managed with Foley catheterisation for accurate urine output monitoring, strict fluid intake-output charting, high oral fluid intake, and supportive medication including a diuretic, an antiemetic, and an antispasmodic for her abdominal pain, alongside coagulation and renal function monitoring. Her subsequent laboratory trend and final outcome are not documented in the source record.
