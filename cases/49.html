70-Year-Old Man with Pedal Edema — Acute-on-Chronic Renal Failure, Sepsis-Induced, with Analgesic Nephropathy

Source case log: https://102-crazymandadi.blogspot.com/2021/10/70-year-old-male-with-acute-on-chronic.html

Case Narrative

A 70-year-old man presented to the casualty department with a one-and-a-half-month history of progressive, bilateral pitting pedal edema associated with facial puffiness. He denied breathlessness, oliguria, or abdominal distension, but reported persistent hiccups over the preceding fifteen days and an episode of non-projectile, bilious vomiting containing food particles ten days before admission — he noted a pattern of vomiting specifically related to travel by bike or other transport. He had had a similar episode of bilateral pedal edema two years earlier that had resolved with medication.

He was a known hypertensive of one-and-a-half years' duration and was not known to have diabetes, coronary artery disease, asthma, or tuberculosis. His urinary history included hesitancy and dribbling without increased frequency. He drank alcohol occasionally and had smoked a local cigarette daily until stopping three years earlier; there was no significant family history.

On examination he had bilateral grade 2 pitting pedal edema, was afebrile, with a pulse of 78/min, respiratory rate 23/min, blood pressure 200/100 mmHg, and SpO2 98% on room air. Cardiac auscultation was normal; the respiratory examination showed decreased air entry at the left infra- and inter-scapular areas; the abdomen was soft and non-tender, and the neurological examination showed no signs of meningeal irritation. The initial working diagnosis was congestive cardiac failure versus renal failure, and he was started on a stat dose of intravenous furosemide with regular blood pressure monitoring while further work-up proceeded, including ECG, chest radiography, echocardiography, abdominal ultrasound, arterial blood gas, and urinalysis — the latter showing significant proteinuria.

Over the following days his diagnosis was refined to acute-on-chronic renal failure, attributed to sepsis with a contribution from analgesic nephropathy. He was managed with ongoing fluid and salt restriction, twice-daily intravenous furosemide, antipyretics as needed for intermittent fever spikes, and antihypertensive therapy that was adjusted across his stay — including amlodipine and metoprolol initially, with metoprolol later down-titrated and a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker (nicardia) added — alongside strict monitoring of blood pressure, temperature, and fluid balance. His course included a transient fever spike on day 3 that resolved, and by the final recorded days his working diagnosis remained acute-on-chronic renal failure (sepsis-induced) with analgesic nephropathy, with recurring pedal and periorbital edema requiring ongoing diuretic and antihypertensive adjustment.

Outcome
