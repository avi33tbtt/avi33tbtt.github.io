60 Year Old Man with Left Hand Tremor and Wasting — Kinetic Tremor Under Evaluation

Source case log: http://sruthi995.blogspot.com/2021/04/60-year-old-male-came-with-co.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 60-year-old right-handed mason presented with a two-year history of involuntary movement of the left hand fingers, occurring intermittently with several days between episodes, along with swelling and a burning sensation in the hand, and possible walking difficulty with giddiness over the same period. He described a sudden, non-progressive inability to grip objects with the left hand at work, with weakness most apparent in holding weight (unable to lift a bucket, divide chapati dough, or manage buttons) while proximal shoulder movements remained normal. The tremor itself was present at rest, worsened when reaching for an object (kinetic component), and was also present with the hand outstretched (postural component) — a mixed tremor pattern. He also described burning, pricking, and itching paraesthesia in the left upper limb, but no autonomic symptoms, seizures, speech disturbance, or loss of smell, and his sense of touch, bowel, and bladder function were preserved.

His past history was significant for an anterior wall myocardial infarction in November 2017, treated with coronary angiography, and a stroke five months before this presentation — right-sided bilateral MCA territory infarcts with facial and left upper-limb weakness — that occurred shortly after he learned of his son's death from a third myocardial infarction at age 30; this earlier stroke was managed medically. He had known hypertension for two years on amlodipine-class therapy, aspirin, and metoprolol. On examination he had clubbing and koilonychia, and a striking discrepancy in blood pressure between limbs — 110/80 mmHg in the left arm versus 210/150 mmHg in the right arm — raising the possibility of a vascular (e.g., subclavian stenosis) contribution. Neurological examination showed measurable wasting of the left upper limb compared to the right (circumference differences at both the upper and lower arm), increased tone at the left wrist, and mild but consistent distal weakness (4/5) in several left forearm and hand muscle groups, with normal proximal strength — a pattern of relatively isolated distal upper-limb involvement.

Given his known bilateral MCA territory infarcts, coronary artery disease, and hypertension, together with the localized kinetic/postural tremor and distal hand weakness/wasting without sensory or pyramidal involvement, the working diagnosis was kinetic tremor under evaluation in a patient with known cerebrovascular and cardiac disease, with a discussion raised by the team of differentials including Hirayama disease (a rare, usually younger-onset, focal amyotrophy of the distal upper limb) — though his age at presentation argued against this specific entity. He was managed with antihypertensives, a tremor-targeted agent (tetrabenazine), pregabalin for the paraesthesia, and multivitamin supplementation, with regular blood pressure and pulse monitoring. The documented record ends at this point of evaluation and treatment initiation.
