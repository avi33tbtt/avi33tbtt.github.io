Chest Radiograph Teaching Discussion in a Patient with Acute Kidney Injury — Left Lower Lobe Collapse

Source case log: https://12avirnenivaishnavi.blogspot.com/2020/06/active-learning.html

Case Narrative

This entry documents a Socratic teaching discussion built around the chest radiograph of a patient who had presented with acute kidney injury, rather than a full clinical case record. The discussion began with an observation that the film showed consolidation, raising an initial question of whether this represented pulmonary edema related to the acute kidney injury.

Through guided questioning, the discussion moved from a description of increased density on the left hemithorax toward a more precise radiological interpretation. It was established that consolidation is classically marked by air bronchograms, and that the film in question showed asymmetry between the two lung fields with reduced visible lung volume on the left side, without evidence of pleural fluid accounting for that volume loss.

The discussion then reviewed the distinguishing radiological signs of left upper versus left lower lobe collapse — for the upper lobe, features such as obscuration of the heart border and aortic arch with the Luftsichel sign; for the lower lobe, the retrocardiac sail sign, a double cardiac contour, inferior displacement of the left hilum, and flattening of the left heart border (the flat waist sign). Applying these signs to the patient's film, in the presence of air bronchograms and a triangular density in the expected location, the pattern was judged most consistent with left lower lobe collapse rather than simple consolidation or pulmonary edema.

The discussion closed with a note of appropriate diagnostic caution, encouraging comparison against a normal chest radiograph before finalizing the interpretation, since some of these findings can occasionally represent normal variants.
