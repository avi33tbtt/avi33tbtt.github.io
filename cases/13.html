31-Year-Old Man with Left-Sided Numbness and Weakness — Acute Ischemic Stroke

Source case log: original article

Case Narrative

A 31-year-old man, a water supplier by occupation, was brought to casualty at night after developing sudden numbness of his left arm and leg while riding his motorbike, quickly followed by weakness of the left leg that progressed to involve the left arm within about five minutes. He was unable to change gears and had to stop and call for help. During that call he had slurred speech for about fifteen minutes, which then resolved on its own — a classic pattern of an acute, sudden-onset focal neurological deficit with a transient speech component, strongly suggestive of an acute cerebrovascular event. He was taken first to a private hospital for initial fluids before being brought in for further evaluation. He denied fever, headache, ear discharge, visual disturbance, double vision, light sensitivity, head injury, or abnormal movements.

He had no known history of hypertension, diabetes, tuberculosis, asthma, cardiovascular disease, or epilepsy, though there was a strong family history of diabetes and hypertension. His personal history was notable for alcohol use (500 mL on alternate days for 18 years) and smoking (4–5 cigarettes daily for 18 years) — both recognized vascular risk factors. On examination he had marked truncal obesity (BMI 41.2 kg/m²) with acanthosis nigricans and skin hesitation cuts, findings that pointed toward underlying insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome as a contributing risk factor even though he had no formally diagnosed diabetes.

Neurological examination confirmed left-sided hypotonia and reduced power (graded around 4-/5) across shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, and knee movements on the left, while higher mental functions, speech, memory, cranial nerves, cerebellar function, and sensory examination were all preserved. His deep tendon reflexes were notably diminished or absent throughout, a somewhat atypical finding for an acute cortical or subcortical stroke, though the overall pattern of acute-onset, non-progressive focal weakness fit an ischemic vascular event. Imaging confirmed an acute infarct localized to the body of the right caudate nucleus and right lentiform nucleus — small, deep structures whose involvement explains the pure motor weakness pattern without cortical signs such as aphasia or neglect.

He was started on standard secondary stroke prevention with dual antiplatelet therapy and a high-intensity statin, alongside physiotherapy planning for the affected left limbs.

Outcome: He was diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke due to infarction of the right caudate and lentiform nuclei and started on dual antiplatelet and statin therapy; the documented record ends as Day 2 treatment was being initiated, without further follow-up documented.
