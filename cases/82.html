60 Year Old Man with Vomiting and Giddiness — Acute-on-Chronic Subdural Hematoma with Cervical Myelopathy

Source case log: https://171mkfarhanelogcases.blogspot.com/2021/12/a-60-year-old-male-with-acute-on.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 60-year-old man, a farmer by occupation, presented with vomiting and giddiness of two weeks' duration. Two months before this presentation he had sustained a head injury and was subsequently found to have a subdural hematoma, which was managed conservatively at the time. His vomiting was non-bilious, non-projectile, and occurred immediately after eating, without associated fever, abdominal distension, or facial swelling — a pattern that, combined with his head injury history, raised concern for a chronic subdural collection re-expanding or for raised intracranial pressure.

He was not a known diabetic or hypertensive, had no history of seizures, and was a smoker. On examination he was conscious, alert, and fully oriented, with a blood pressure of 150/90 mmHg and otherwise stable vitals. Neurological examination was largely preserved — cranial nerves, sensory system, and coordination were intact — but reflexes were symmetrically brisk (3+ throughout) with normal power (4+/5 in all limbs), findings that pointed toward an additional compressive process along the cervical spinal cord rather than a purely intracranial problem.

He was managed conservatively with supportive medication for nausea and B-vitamin supplementation, alongside close monitoring of blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and Glasgow Coma Scale. On review a few days into admission his vitals had settled further (blood pressure 140/80 mmHg, pulse 74 bpm), and he remained clinically stable without new deficits. The working diagnosis was refined to chronic subdural hematoma with compressive cervical myelopathy, and management continued along these lines; an unresolved clinical question raised during the admission was why his blood pressure had risen following the initial trauma.
