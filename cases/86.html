28 Year Old Man with Sudden Fall and Quadriparesis — Tuberculous Cervical Spondylitis with Epidural Abscess (Pott's Spine)

Source case log: https://2018-21batchpgy3gmpracticals.blogspot.com/2021/08/18100006006-case-presentations.html

Part 1 — Case Narrative

A 28-year-old man presented with sudden weakness of both lower limbs and loss of hand grip that had begun ten days earlier, accompanied by bowel and bladder incontinence. His story began a month before, when he developed a productive cough and low-grade fever, was found sputum-positive for acid-fast bacilli, and was started on standard four-drug anti-tuberculosis therapy. He then developed generalised weakness and muscle aches, and ten days before this presentation he suddenly fell while getting up to answer the door, without loss of consciousness; from that point he progressively lost the ability to move his legs, turn in bed, or use his hands for daily tasks, while still able to move his fingers and bring his hand partway to his mouth.

He also described an electric-shock-like sensation radiating from his neck into both arms on neck flexion — a Lhermitte's-type sign — along with tingling in his hands, all pointing toward a cervical spinal cord process rather than a peripheral or cerebral one. Cranial nerve function, speech, vision, and higher mental function were all preserved, narrowing the localisation further to the spinal cord itself. His father had a history of tuberculosis treated with anti-TB therapy for two years, reinforcing the likely infective aetiology.

On examination he was thin (BMI 16.5) but alert and fully oriented. Neurological examination showed flaccid-then-spastic paraparesis with 0/5 power in both legs and preserved but reduced 4/5 power in the arms, increased tone in the legs, brisk reflexes (3+) throughout, and bilateral extensor plantar responses — a classic upper motor neuron pattern below the level of a cervical cord lesion. Vibration and joint-position sense were lost distally while pain, temperature, and touch were preserved, and there was no cerebellar or cranial nerve deficit. This combination localised the lesion anatomically to the cervical cord, functionally to quadriparesis, and — given the tuberculosis history — most likely to a compressive extramedullary, extradural process such as Pott's spine.

He was started empirically on anti-tuberculosis therapy alongside vitamin B supplementation while the diagnosis was confirmed, with vitals monitored closely. Imaging subsequently confirmed infectious spondylitis involving the C4 through D1 vertebral levels with an epidural abscess at C5–C6, and he underwent surgical drainage of the abscess. Following surgery he regained control of his bowel and bladder, and his power and tone improved to the point that he was documented as walking again at the time of discharge, with a plan to continue his full course of anti-tuberculosis therapy.
